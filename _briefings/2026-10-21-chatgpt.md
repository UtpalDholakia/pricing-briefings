## Key takeaways

- Chewy cut the visible fee and narrowed parts of the reward schedule. Net value depends on what a member buys and uses.
- Moving from individual deals to a subscription changes when customers pay and how certain the savings are.
- Price fairness can depend on perceived harm or access even when there is no comparison price.

## What happened

### 1. Restaurant.com turns occasional discounts into a subscription

**Sources:** [Giftify announcement](https://www.globenewswire.com/news-release/2026/10/13/3379700/0/en/giftify-announces-conversion-of-restaurant-com-to-membership-based-model-expanding-recurring-revenue-opportunity.html) · October 13, 2026 · [Membership details](https://www.restaurant.com/membership/learn-more)

Starting October 27, Restaurant.com will replace individual deal purchases with unlimited access for $14 monthly or $99 annually. Existing certificates remain valid. Points can fund membership, and automatic renewal is optional. Individual restaurant restrictions still apply.

**Tags:** subscription pricing, Membership

### 2. Chewy lowers the membership price while narrowing some rewards

**Sources:** [Subscription Insider analysis](https://www.subscriptioninsider.com/blog/chewy-cuts-chewy-fee-refocuses-rewards-on-pet-essentials) · October 9, 2026 · [Current Chewy+ offer](https://www.chewy.com/app/membership/sign-up)

Chewy+ now costs $59 rather than $79 annually. Food, health, and pharmacy purchases retain 5% rewards. Most other eligible purchases earn 2%, and the annual reward ceiling falls from $150 to $100. Chewy is giving greater prominence to veterinary and poison-control benefits.

**Tags:** Rewards Program, Customer loyalty

### 3. Explaining who funds a discount changes its legitimacy

**Sources:** [“Price Judgments in Advertising-Funded Discounts”](https://doi.org/10.1002/cb.70267) · October 16, 2026 · [Wiley Online Library](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/cb.70267)

Two experiments compared describing an advertising-funded discount as a return of advertising revenue with describing it as an ordinary promotion. The revenue-return explanation increased perceived legitimacy, which was associated with higher purchase intention. Inferences of manipulation were less consistent and appeared more clearly for utilitarian purchases.

**Tags:** Price Promotions, Cross-subsidization

### 4. Price fairness may begin with inferred harm, not comparison prices

**Sources:** [“Painful Prices”](https://doi.org/10.1093/jcr/ucaf045) · Online July 9, 2025; October 2026 issue · [academic.oup.com](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article/53/3/444/8195730)

Across eight studies, the researchers model price fairness as a moral judgment shaped by inferred harm. Consumer vulnerability, product welfare effects, pricing strategy, and perceived firm self-protection all matter. The framework explains why paying more than another customer can sometimes seem fair, and why lowering the price of a harmful product can seem unfair.

**Tags:** Fairness, Consumer welfare

### 5. Adaptive products face a ceiling on acceptable price premiums

**Sources:** [“Limiting Accessibility”](https://doi.org/10.1093/jcr/ucag003) · March 21, 2026; October 2026 issue · [academic.oup.com](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article/53/3/467/8533544)

Across nine main and six supplementary studies, consumers found innovation premiums less acceptable when products targeted people with disabilities rather than the mass market. Cost explanations did not eliminate the response. The authors identify pity as an important driver and describe the result as a potentially paternalistic constraint on accessible innovation.

**Tags:** Accessibility, Premiumization

## Why it matters

**1. Restaurant.com turns occasional discounts into a subscription** The customer moves from buying a known saving to prepaying for uncertain future opportunities. My hypothesis is that prepayment will encourage deal seeking and redemption. The annual option also makes underuse more consequential. Initial signups financed with existing points will reveal less than cash renewals.

**2. Chewy lowers the membership price while narrowing some rewards** The lower entry price is highly visible. The smaller reward rate and cap require customer-specific calculation. Some heavy users can therefore receive less net value despite paying a lower fee. Acquisition may improve while renewal depends on whether members actually use the newly emphasized health benefits.

**3. Explaining who funds a discount changes its legitimacy** Customers evaluate the exchange structure, not only the reduced price. “Advertising subsidizes your price” can suggest a reciprocal allocation of value. That is different from simply announcing a discount. The evidence covers experimental intentions, not observed subscription or purchase behavior.

**4. Price fairness may begin with inferred harm, not comparison prices** Reference prices and margins are incomplete fairness diagnostics. Practitioners should also ask who gains access, who appears vulnerable, and whether the price facilitates benefit or harm.

**5. Adaptive products face a ceiling on acceptable price premiums** Higher customer benefit does not necessarily expand acceptable willingness to pay. Targeting language can activate a moral expectation that adaptive value should not carry a premium. Universal positioning may change that inference, but the research also warns against letting pricing concerns reduce market provision.

## Ideas to apply

- Calculate the old and new membership value for customers with different mixes of purchases. Track renewals alongside new signups.
- Distinguish signups paid with existing points from renewals paid in cash when evaluating a membership launch.
- When testing prices for adaptive products, measure perceived access and harm alongside willingness to pay.
