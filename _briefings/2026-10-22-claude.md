## 1. Prices ending in 9 resisted tariff pass-through, and the cost showed up elsewhere — Hirshman & Ignatenko, CESifo WP 13056 (SSRN, posted Oct 19)

**Source:** [papers.ssrn.com](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=7622838)

Using retail price data around recent US import tariffs, the authors find tariffs passed through mostly to prices that do not end in 9. Nine-ending prices absorbed far less of the shock, and afterwards inflation ran higher for non-9-ending products, including ones that were never tariffed. Left-digit bias is usually studied on the demand side; this treats it as a pricing friction that shapes how macro cost shocks reach consumers, and it suggests retailers may be reallocating price increases across the assortment to protect psychological thresholds.

## 2. Two courts, two theories of drip pricing: FTC v. Ticketmaster proceeds, while Canada's Federal Court says itemizing is not dripping — Venable's All About Advertising Law (Oct 14) and Bennett Jones (Oct 8)

**Source:** [allaboutadvertisinglaw.com](https://www.allaboutadvertisinglaw.com/2026/10/court-allows-ftc-drip-pricing-lawsuit-against-ticketmaster-to-proceed.html) · [bennettjones.com](https://www.bennettjones.com/Insights/Blogs/2026/10/Federal-Court-Interprets-Drip-Pricing-No-Drip-No-Class)

A California federal court let the FTC's deception claim go forward, reasoning that the lower face price shown first is what shoppers use to filter options, so disclosing the full total before checkout does not cure it. The court also found a "+Fees" label too vague to signal increases of up to 44%. In Kuling v. Ticketmaster, Canada's Federal Court defined drip pricing as concealment plus sequential reveal, and held that a fee shown right next to the face value is lawful partitioned pricing. Together they amount to a legal test of where partitioned pricing ends and drip pricing begins; the US "initial contact" logic maps neatly onto the consideration-set and anchoring literature. (Same week: the FTC settled with Florida's Greenway Auto Group, alleging over 92% of buyers paid more than $3,350 above advertised prices once mandatory fees were added; see [retailconsumerproductslaw.com](https://www.retailconsumerproductslaw.com/2026/10/ftc-updates-october-5-october-9-2026/))

## 3. Uber Eats and Thuisbezorgd agree to compensate Dutch customers for end-of-funnel fees — NL Times (Oct 20)

**Source:** [nltimes.nl](https://nltimes.nl/2026/10/20/uber-eats-thuisbezorgd-compensate-customers-extra-charges)

After a review by the Dutch Consumers' Association (Consumentenbond), both platforms agreed to hand out vouchers to past customers over service, small-order and packaging fees that appeared only at the end of checkout. Thuisbezorgd customers were often hit with 2 to 4 euros in such charges, and advertised discounts sometimes coincided with higher prices. This is a negotiated settlement driven by a consumer group rather than a regulator. The tiered remedy (Uber Eats scales vouchers by order frequency) is an unusual attempt to estimate harm from drip pricing across years of repeat purchases.

## 4. India's ₹5 and ₹10 "magic price points" are cracking under cost pressure — Outlook Business (Oct 20)

**Source:** [outlookbusiness.com](https://outlookbusiness.com/corporate/fmcg-5-10-rupees-packs-costs-rising-brands-margins-declining-shrinkflation)

NielsenIQ data show the share of ₹5/₹10 packs falling across FMCG categories. Brands are cutting grammage (a ₹5 chips pack went from about 17g to 8g over time), slimming bottles, retiring packs, and steering buyers to ₹20 packs framed as better value. An industry source puts the point where consumers feel cheated at roughly 20g, or two or three biscuits per pack. This is a rich natural setting for price-point rigidity, shrinkflation thresholds and the size-versus-price trade-off at very low absolute prices, and it is a useful non-Western counterpart to the US shrinkflation literature.

## 5. LLM pricing agents can sustain high prices, depending on how they are wired — Bichler et al., TU Munich (arXiv 2610.21711, Oct 2026)

**Source:** [arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21711)

In repeated Bertrand simulations, LLM agents that see raw histories and rival prices can hold supracompetitive prices, while agents that output price distributions from summarized own-profit histories drift toward competitive levels. In mixed markets they produce "anchor-and-harvest" cycles, where one firm holds a high price and the other periodically undercuts it. The authors argue that audits should cover the whole deployment (information feed, memory, update frequency) rather than the prompt alone. On the buyer side, a companion read is Kireyev's PriceBench (arXiv, Sept 25), which finds price sensitivity varies more than tenfold across 28 LLM hotel-booking agents, and that a change in an agent's price/quality trade-off moved the average booked rate from $247 to $393: [arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.31468v1)

## 6. United's CEO reframes the middle seat as the reference point — Aero Crew News (Oct 18)

**Source:** [aerocrewnews.com](https://aerocrewnews.com/2026/10/18/united-ceo-avoiding-the-middle-seat-should-come-at-a-price/)

Scott Kirby argued that avoiding the middle seat should cost extra. That reverses the usual framing in which window and aisle seats are paid upgrades: the worst seat becomes the default and escaping it becomes a fee. Kirby tied it to a new economy product with a deliberately blocked middle seat. It is a live case of a seller trying to shift the reference point so that a surcharge reads as avoiding a loss rather than buying a gain, and a good classroom example for fairness perceptions of unbundling.

## 7. European regulators converge on game monetization: PEGI moves paid loot boxes toward a 16+ rating as EU consumer authorities probe virtual-currency pricing — iXBT Games, citing Eurogamer (Oct 21); Tech Insider (EU CPC action, announced Sept 30)

**Source:** [ixbt.games](https://ixbt.games/en/news/2026/10/21/populiarnye-igry-s-lutboksami-polucat-reiting-16-pegi-uzestocaet-pravila.html) · [tech-insider.org](https://tech-insider.org/eu-probe-nine-gaming-companies-virtual-currency-2026/)

PEGI will raise games rated 3, 7 or 12 to 16+ if they sell paid random-content items, and it will weigh how games encourage children to spend (EA Sports FC 27 has already been re-rated). Separately, the EU CPC network is examining nine publishers (including Supercell, King, Riot and Mojang) over prices shown mainly in in-game currency, currency bundles that don't match item prices, and hidden exchange rates. Both target the token-currency mechanisms that decouple spending from money, a direct policy test of the pain-of-paying and mental-accounting research on payment abstraction.
