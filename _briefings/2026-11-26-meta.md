## 1. [Salesforce will sell you Core for $115 a seat — if you sign by November 30 and are one of the first 5,000](https://activator.salesforce.com/proposals/core-bundle-first-5-000/)

**Source:** Salesforce, Core Bundle First 5,000, November 2026; edition list prices via ClonePartner, Nov 2026: [activator.salesforce.com](https://activator.salesforce.com/proposals/core-bundle-first-5-000/) and [clonepartner.com](https://clonepartner.com/blog/salesforce-core-advanced-max-2026-renewal-migration-guide)

Salesforce is running a Black Friday-style deadline on its own enterprise software: the first 5,000 eligible small and midsize customers who sign an order form by November 30, 2026 get Sales Cloud Core Edition at $115 per user per month, billed annually, against a list price of $195. The pitch is built on the gap between three numbers for the same seat — the $115 promotional price, the $195 list price, and $332.50 if you bought every bundled piece à la carte at list (Sales Cloud Enterprise at $175, Slack Business+ at $15, Premier Success at $52.50, Tableau Next at $40, call intelligence at $50). Core replaced Enterprise on September 3, 2026, listing $20 above it (+11%), and Advanced replaced Unlimited at $395 against $350 (+13%), while Max held at $550; Salesforce says legacy customers' pricing is unchanged until they move. The pricing point is the deadline mechanics: a capped quantity, a hard date and a 41%-off-list anchor, all aimed at getting a signature this week rather than at the next renewal. List-vs-net: $115 is a promotional price and $195 is the published list price; what buyers actually pay after negotiation is not disclosed.

## 2. [Zoom never announced a price rise in 2026. It raised the price of staying, one quiet change at a time](https://forum.unchainedstack.org/t/zoom-did-not-announce-a-price-rise-in-2026-it-raised-the-price-of-staying-one-quiet-change-at-a-time/739)

**Source:** UnchainedStack forum, Nov 17, 2026, verified against Zoom's pricing page: [forum.unchainedstack.org](https://forum.unchainedstack.org/t/zoom-did-not-announce-a-price-rise-in-2026-it-raised-the-price-of-staying-one-quiet-change-at-a-time/739) and [zoom.us](https://zoom.us/pricing)

Zoom published no big price overhaul in 2026, and the changes still add up. On March 2 the annual Pro price rose from $13.33 to $14.16 per user per month while the annual discount shrank from up to 21% to up to 16%; the monthly Pro price is now $16.99, up from $15.99 in mid-2025, and Business is $21.99 monthly or $18.33 billed annually. In October 2026 Zoom withdrew the free Basic Scheduler — the booking-page tool that let people arrange meetings against your availability is now chargeable, and the community thread confirming it is full of Pro customers who thought scheduling was part of the meeting product. In February the Custom AI Companion add-on rose from $12 to $20 a month, and on June 1 Zoom launched ZoomMate, an AI teammate, at $20 per user a month with AI credits included, while AI meeting summaries stay free on paid plans. Zoom's own page is running the same playbook right now: 30% off the first year of Pro or Business for new subscribers, $9.91 and $12.83 respectively, expiring December 7, 2026. The pricing point is the mechanism: no single change looks like a price rise, but the bill for the same work went up through the list, the discount, the withdrawn free feature and the second AI meter. All figures are published list prices; net price not disclosed. One caveat: the forum author discloses that he leads a vendor-migration company.

**Source:** Threads, Nov 13, 2026; Instagram reel, Nov 3, 2026; Facebook posts, Nov 25, 2026; YouTube creator video, Nov 25, 2026: [threads.com](https://www.threads.com/@zerkalo_io/post/DfrylAFDWvl) and [instagram.com](https://www.instagram.com/reel/DfSVgN7yYl3/) and [youtube.com](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TA-YMaOfS9E)

A Threads post from Belarus shows a store's Black Friday sign over racks of clothes with the caption that Belarusians are complaining about prices rising before mass sales. An Instagram reel from a British MP's committee hearing shows a Samsung vacuum at £299 in May–June, £399 in August–September, then offered as a Black Friday deal at £350 — still £51 above the spring price — while commenters recount the same pattern at Currys, Halfords and Argos and recommend price trackers. The pricing point is the trust cost: the complaint is not that the discount is small, it is that the reference price is fake, and shoppers are now doing the price-history work the retailer should have done. No private individual is named; the posts are paraphrased, not quoted.

## 4. [College tuition rose about 3% this year — and the price students actually pay has fallen for a decade](https://research.collegeboard.org/trends/college-pricing/highlights?mf_ct_campaign=msn-feed&utm_content=syndication)

**Source:** College Board, Trends in College Pricing and Student Aid 2026, November 2026: [research.collegeboard.org](https://research.collegeboard.org/trends/college-pricing/highlights?mf_ct_campaign=msn-feed&utm_content=syndication) and [newsroom.collegeboard.org](https://newsroom.collegeboard.org/average-sticker-prices-public-colleges-rise-less-inflation-grant-aid-brings-average-net-tuition)

The College Board's annual report, released this month, says sticker tuition rose in 2026-27 by 2.9% at public four-year colleges in state ($12,290), 3.5% out of state ($32,730), 2.4% at public two-year colleges ($4,210) and 3.7% at private nonprofit four-year colleges ($46,350) — all before adjusting for inflation. The same report says the price students actually pay moved the other way: after adjusting for inflation, the average net tuition and fees paid by first-time, full-time, in-state students at public four-year colleges peaked at $4,600 in 2012-13 and fell to an estimated $1,920 in 2026-27, and at private nonprofits it fell from about $20,000 to $17,280. The sticker still varies wildly by state — public four-year in-state tuition runs from $6,360 in Florida to $18,710 in New Jersey — and the full cost of attendance before aid reaches $67,610 at a private nonprofit. The pricing point is the two-number problem: the published price is the anchor everyone argues about, and the net price is the price almost nobody pays attention to, and they have been moving in opposite directions for a decade.

## 5. [Car insurance stopped getting more expensive — after rising 32% in three years](https://insurify.com/car-insurance/news/average-car-insurance-rates-november-2026/)

**Source:** Insurify, average rates as of November 2026; NerdWallet, November 2026 rate analysis: [insurify.com](https://insurify.com/car-insurance/news/average-car-insurance-rates-november-2026/) and [nerdwallet.com](https://www.nerdwallet.com/insurance/data)

After two years of double-digit increases, car insurance inflation has stalled. Insurify's November 2026 figures put the national average for full coverage at $187 a month, flat through October, with liability-only at $99; its analysts project only about a 1% rise by the end of the year, with 32 states seeing small increases led by Connecticut at 4%. NerdWallet's November analysis, using a different method, puts the median at $2,357 a year for full coverage, up 2.4% year on year in the first quarter of 2026 — but up 32% since 2023. The two sources measure different things (Insurify reports quote averages; NerdWallet reports medians from Quadrant data), so the levels do not match, but the direction agrees: the increases have slowed to a crawl after the 2023–24 surge. The pricing point is the pause, not the level: a market that rose by a third in three years has gone flat, and the next move depends on repair costs and claims, not on another round of catch-up pricing.

## 6. [Child care got harder to afford in 2026 — and nearly 20 states moved to do something about it](https://www.childcareaware.org/how-state-legislation-moved-child-care-forward-in-2026/)

**Source:** Child Care Aware of America, Beyond the Patchwork, November 2026; promoted on Facebook, Nov 25, 2026: [childcareaware.org](https://www.childcareaware.org/how-state-legislation-moved-child-care-forward-in-2026/) and [facebook.com](https://www.facebook.com/reel/1116498701002739/)

Child Care Aware of America's new report, Beyond the Patchwork, opens with the price: child care got harder to afford in 2026, with prices up nearly 23% from 2021 to 2025, against a national average annual price of $13,184 in its May 2026 benchmark. The report's news is the response, not the price: nearly 20 states moved child care forward in 2026, led by Maine capping copays at 7% of family income, Nebraska letting more families keep help paying for care, and New Hampshire eliminating copays for grandparents and relatives raising children. On the supply side, California put $269.2 million into restoring and adding subsidised slots, Virginia $137 million to add about 6,700 slots and clear its waitlist, and Connecticut $411.7 million for new spaces and lower costs; Pennsylvania added $5 million in retention bonuses for educators. A companion report, An Uneven Start 2026, finds average per-child state investment rose from about $1,400 in fiscal 2025 to about $1,700 in fiscal 2026 — while seven states still invest nothing beyond what is required to draw down federal dollars. The pricing point is the split: the market price keeps rising, and the policy response is now priced in copay caps and per-slot funding rather than in the price itself.

## 7. [Meta's own auction got 23% more expensive in the US and Canada — and a new default makes the cost look lower](https://falia.co/en/insights/paid-advertising/facebook-instagram-ads-cost/)

**Source:** Falia, Nov 13, 2026, on Meta's third-quarter 2026 results, Oct 21, 2026, and PPC Land, Oct 27, 2026: [falia.co](https://falia.co/en/insights/paid-advertising/facebook-instagram-ads-cost/)

Meta's third-quarter results, published October 21, show the average price of a Meta ad rose 14% worldwide year on year and 23% in the United States and Canada, on 8% more impressions in the region — price rising almost three times faster than the number of ads delivered. Advertising revenue reached $63.06 billion, up 26%. Chief financial officer Susan Li attributed the rise to ad performance gains and better economic conditions, citing its GEM model lifting conversions 1.7% and Andromeda 1.4% on Facebook — Meta's own measurements, with no published method. The catch is in the measurement: since November 12, 2026, Meta has been turning on by default, according to PPC Land, a setting that measures some conversions on the customer's device, so reported conversions can rise and cost per result can look lower without any change in sales. The pricing point is the double move: the auction price rose sharply, and at the same time the yardstick for judging it changed, so an advertiser whose dashboard looks better may not be selling more.

## 8. [New York's next subway fare rise is in the budget — but no fare has been proposed yet](https://www.mta.info/document/192536)

**Source:** MTA November Financial Plan 2026–2029, Nov 18, 2026; New York Post, Nov 13, 2026, on a Community Service Society survey; HPL, Nov 2026: [mta.info](https://www.mta.info/document/192536) and [nypost.com](https://nypost.com/2026/11/13/us-news/1-in-5-nyc-transit-riders-say-they-cant-afford-fares-as-mta-plans-another-hike/) and [hpl.nyc](https://hpl.nyc/2026/11/mta-budget-2027-fare-hike/)

The MTA's November Financial Plan, presented November 18, proposes biennial fare and toll rate changes in March 2027 and March 2029 to generate a 4% yield increase in farebox and toll revenues, on a proposed $22.9 billion 2027 operating budget. No fare has been proposed: the 4% is a revenue target across subways, buses, trains and tolls, and the board has to approve a specific proposal after public hearings; the board votes on the budget itself on December 14 and 16. Applied directly to the current $3.00 subway and bus fare, 4% would be about $3.12 — an illustration, not a proposal, and the MTA usually rounds fares. The affordability context comes from a Community Service Society survey reported November 13: about one in five riders say they cannot afford the $3 fare, and nearly three in ten say they earn too much to qualify for the city's half-price Fair Fares programme, which caps eligibility at 200% of the federal poverty level; the Society proposes raising it to 300%. The pricing point is the gap between the plan and the price: the budget already counts the money, the fare itself is still unwritten, and a fifth of riders already cannot afford the one in force.

## Pick of the day

Item 4, the College Board report. Every tuition argument in the country is fought over the sticker price, and the sticker did rise — about 3% this year. But the price students actually pay, after grant aid and adjusted for inflation, has fallen by more than half since 2012 at public four-year colleges. A debate conducted entirely about the number nobody pays is a debate about an anchor, not a price.
