## Key takeaways

- Pooling storage can benefit one heavy user while reducing capacity for a household with several heavy users.
- Domino’s example shows why per-order points should not be described as a fixed rebate on spending.
- A targeted discount message does not establish that customers were charged different final prices.

## What happened

### 1. Microsoft pools family storage while shrinking the maximum allowance

**Sources:** [Subscription Insider](https://www.subscriptioninsider.com/blog/microsoft-365-cuts-storage-as-it-changes-subscription-benefits) · October 14, 2026 · [Microsoft’s transition guidance](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/onedrive/changes-to-microsoft-365-storage)

Microsoft 365 Family and Premium are moving from up to six separate 1 TB allowances to 2 TB shared across the household. Existing subscribers generally transition at renewal on or after May 2, 2027. Some may receive bonus storage.

**Tags:** Hidden Price Increases, subscription pricing

### 2. Google makes the relevant comparison price depend on membership

**Sources:** [Subscription Insider](https://www.subscriptioninsider.com/blog/google-lets-merchants-carry-membership-into-ai-shopping) · October 5, 2026 · [Google’s primary documentation](https://developers.google.com/merchant/api/guides/loyalty/customer-match-service)

Google’s expanding loyalty infrastructure lets merchants associate consenting customers with membership tiers and surface member-specific prices. A late-September alpha service adds program-management capabilities. Eligibility for benefits to appear in AI Mode and Gemini was announced earlier, in March.

**Tags:** AI in Pricing, Loyalty Program

### 3. A high loyalty return can reward small orders, not large customers

**Sources:** [The Points Guy’s State of Loyalty report](https://thepointsguy.com/state-of-loyalty-2026) · October 2026 · [Food & Wine’s analysis](https://www.foodandwine.com/restaurant-rewards-programs-ranked-by-value-the-points-guy-state-of-loyalty-report-12173196) · October 23, 2026

TPG’s comparison gives Domino’s a potential 25% return toward its first reward. The qualification matters: customers earn ten points per eligible order of at least $5, only once daily. A $100 order earns no more than a qualifying small order. The calculation uses Charlotte menu prices.

**Tags:** Rewards Program, Incentives

### 4. Cheaper phones received bigger advertised discounts, not proven cheaper deals

**Source:** [“From Scroll to Sale”](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.00684) · September 30, 2026 preprint

An audit using 56 automated accounts and more than 80,000 videos found larger advertised percentage discounts on the cheapest tested phones. Median discounts were 45%, versus 28.5% and 20% on the other device classes. Many advertisements omitted the actual product price.

**Tags:** Price Promotions, Price discrimination

### 5. Uniform pricing can work better when customers seek “good enough”

**Source:** [“Unit-Demand Pricing for Min-Buying Customers”](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.17450) · October 12, 2026 preprint

This theoretical paper models customers who choose the cheapest acceptable item rather than maximize net utility. Under specified symmetric valuation conditions, a uniform price is optimal. Under another set of assumptions, it guarantees about 69.9% of optimal differentiated-price revenue. These are mathematical results, not measured shopper behavior.

**Tags:** Consumer decision making, Pricing models

## Why it matters

**1. Microsoft pools family storage while shrinking the maximum allowance** Pooling improves flexibility for one heavy user but reduces capacity for several heavy users. My interpretation: unused capacity can still feel like an owned entitlement. A benefit reduction may therefore provoke resistance even among households currently below the new limit. “More flexible” and “more valuable” are different claims.

**2. Google makes the relevant comparison price depend on membership** My inference is that comparison shopping increasingly requires recognizing the shopper’s existing relationships. A public price may misrepresent that shopper’s actual alternative. Merchants should audit eligibility updates and price consistency. An incorrectly displayed entitlement can create an expectation the checkout cannot honor.

**3. A high loyalty return can reward small orders, not large customers** This is a frequency incentive, not a constant spending rebate. My interpretation: the structure rewards repeated occasions while making percentage value deteriorate with basket size. Practitioners should report rewards by realistic purchase pattern, not only the most favorable redemption.

**4. Cheaper phones received bigger advertised discounts, not proven cheaper deals** Targeting a discount message is not equivalent to charging a lower price. Device price is also an imperfect income proxy. For pricing audits, compare identical products and final payable amounts. Differences in promotional framing alone cannot establish personalized-price discrimination.

**5. Uniform pricing can work better when customers seek “good enough”** Sophisticated price differentiation depends on the decision rule customers actually use. My takeaway: before optimizing a detailed price ladder, test whether customers compare incremental benefits or simply select the cheapest option that meets their needs.

## Ideas to apply

- Test a pooled allowance against both one-heavy-user and many-heavy-user households.
- Audit whether membership prices shown in search are honored in checkout.
- In a promotion audit, compare identical items and actual final prices before drawing conclusions about price discrimination.
