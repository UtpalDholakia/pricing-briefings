## 1. EU's Digital Fairness Act proposal puts reference prices and drip pricing on the per-se blacklist

**Source:** Freshfields briefing on the Commission proposal (Nov 11): [freshfields.com](https://www.freshfields.com/en/our-thinking/briefings/2026/11/digital-fairness-act-the-eus-new-consumer-protection-rulebook-takes-shape)

The proposal adds reference-price claims and drip pricing to Annex I, the list of practices banned in all circumstances. It also requires a real-money equivalent wherever items are sold for in-game currency, adds free-trial conversion reminders and online cancellation rules, and limits dynamic-pricing transparency rules to event ticketing. Personalised pricing is banned only where it relies on profiling and harms minors or vulnerable consumers. For behavioral pricing researchers, this turns decades of lab findings on reference prices, partitioned prices and token currencies into black-letter law. It also leaves open which reference-price formats will count as unfair.

## 2. NYC Council passes a ban on personalized "surveillance pricing" with a detailed loyalty carve-out

**Source:** Fox Rothschild Privacy Compliance & Data Security blog (Nov 12): [dataprivacy.foxrothschild.com](https://dataprivacy.foxrothschild.com/2026/11/articles/general-privacy-data-security-news-developments/new-york-city-council-passes-surveillance-pricing-ban/)

Int. 891-A bars algorithms from using personal data to charge different people different prices. It allows "bona fide" loyalty tiers based on purchase history, and self-identification discounts such as veteran pricing, as long as they are disclosed and not combined with inferred data. NYC would be the second city to pass such a ban, after Seattle in October, and the bill still needs the mayor's signature. The law's line between first-party purchase history (allowed) and inferred data (banned) is a testable claim about where consumers draw the fairness boundary in price discrimination.

## 3. Clip-to-activate "checkbox" coupons lift brand choice less than an equivalent price cut

**Source:** Market Business News, reporting an EMAC 2026 abstract by Heinemann & Gedenk, Univ. of Hamburg (Nov 11): [marketbusinessnews.com](https://marketbusinessnews.com/checkbox-coupons-were-less-effective-than-direct-discounts-in-an-online-shopping-experiment/455618)

In an incentive-compatible simulated online store, coupons that shoppers had to tick to activate (as on Amazon) produced a smaller increase in choice of the promoted brand than a temporary price reduction of the same value. Among buyers in the coupon condition, 93% redeemed the coupon. The authors attribute the gap to price confusion and lower perceived savings. This is a clean test of promotion format on a mechanism that is now everywhere online, and it suggests the activation step costs perceived savings rather than adding the engagement benefit often claimed for it.

**Type:** Research · **Tags:** Academic research, Price Promotions, Discounting, Incentives, Branding, Amazon

## 4. Offering a menu of discounts (fixed vs. chance-based) can cut purchase incidence

**Source:** Market Business News on Zhang, Zhang & Wu, Journal of Business Research (Nov 10): [marketbusinessnews.com](https://marketbusinessnews.com/offering-a-choice-of-discounts-can-sometimes-reduce-purchases-study-finds/455064/)

Two field studies and a preregistered online study find that letting customers choose between a sure discount and a lottery-style promotion lowered purchase rates compared with offering just one promotion. The authors call this "preference cannibalization": each option makes the other look less valuable. Buyers in the menu condition consumed more afterward, which the authors link to sunk-cost effects. The result runs against the usual intuition that more promotion choice raises uptake, and it splits the effects of choice into an acquisition stage and a usage stage.

**Type:** Research · **Tags:** Academic research, Price Promotions, Discounting, Menu pricing, Consumer decision making, Behavioral Economics, Usage-based pricing

## 5. Non-round prices help sell small plates, but not regular portions

**Source:** Ohio State News on Yu, Liu, Kandampully & Huang, Journal of Hospitality and Tourism Management (Nov 12): [news.osu.edu](https://news.osu.edu/secret-to-selling-small-plates-at-restaurants-nonrounded-pricing)

Interviews (n=36) found that diners see small plates as poor value for their size. In an experiment (n=244), $7.99 beat $8.00 on purchase intention for small meals, but $11.99 vs. $12.00 made no difference for regular meals, and the effect ran through perceived fairness. This is a boundary condition for price-ending effects: precision seems to matter most when the product already raises a value or fairness concern. It fits a fairness account of why price endings work better than a left-digit account does. Effect sizes were not reported in the release.

## 6. In Japan, raising prices still calls for an apology

**Source:** Malay Mail / AFP (Nov 9): [malaymail.com](https://www.malaymail.com/news/money/2026/11/09/in-japan-raising-prices-isnt-just-business-its-something-to-apologise-for/242444)

Uniqlo reversed an October price increase on its basic socks after a backlash. Saizeriya's president apologized for menu increases and said he would look for reasons to cut prices again, while keeping its ¥100 glass of wine. Economist Tsutomu Watanabe estimates Japanese consumers need another three to four years to accept regular inflation. The piece shows reference prices and fairness norms that hardened over three decades of deflation, and it raises a cross-cultural question about whether a public apology changes how fair a price increase seems.

## 7. Subscribers quit after price rises largely because they can't pause or downgrade

**Source:** Bango / 3Gem survey press release, n=2,500 US adults (Nov 12): [globenewswire.com](https://www.globenewswire.com/news-release/2026/11/12/3401696/0/en/more-than-one-in-five-americans-cancelled-a-subscription-after-a-price-rise.html)

22% cancelled a subscription after a price increase in the past year. 40% cancelled one they wanted to keep because they couldn't pause it or move to a free tier (50% of Gen Z). 49% say they now pay extra for features that used to be included. This is a vendor survey that promotes bundling, so treat the numbers with care, but the pause/downgrade finding suggests price-increase churn may come from a lack of exit options more than price sensitivity, which is a researchable distinction.

**Type:** Practice · **Tags:** subscription pricing, Subscription Renewals, Tiered Pricing, Price Bundling, price increases

## 8. Hotel fee audit: "fees included" is now universal, but the fee itself is rarely shown

**Source:** Everything-PR research desk (Nov 10): [everything-pr.com](https://everything-pr.com/hotel-price-transparency-audit)

A small purposive audit of 12 US hotels with mandatory resort fees found that all 11 hotels showing prices said fees were included in the headline rate, a sign of FTC junk-fees rule compliance. Only 2 of the 11 showed the fee amount itself. Fees averaged a median 14.9% of the lowest nightly price, and Hyatt pages showed a member rate first. Once fees are folded into the headline price, the remaining levers are which number is shown first (member rate, per-night price), which makes this a natural setting to study anchoring after the rule.

**Type:** Practice · **Tags:** Price transparency, Partitioned Pricing, Regulation, Membership, Hospitality, Resort pricing, Price presentation

## 9. Restaurant "admin fees" are shrinking tips, and Florida now requires disclosure before ordering

**Source:** Jammin 99.9 FM (syndicating Fox News Digital) (Nov 9): [jammin999fm.com](https://www.jammin999fm.com/2026/11/09/restaurant-admin-fees-spark-backlash-as-diners-question-what-theyre-paying-for/)

A Reddit post about a 3% "Admin Fee" at a Denver restaurant drew many replies from diners saying they cut tips to offset such charges. Florida now requires restaurants to disclose operations charges before customers order, and one chain dropped its 3% fee after being cited by a state inspector. Some operators prefer higher menu prices plus a cash discount instead. The story is anecdotal, but it points to a testable mental-accounting prediction: diners treat surcharges and tips as one pool of discretionary spending.

## 10. An analyst argues GTA VI's near-zero price elasticity makes it a poor guide for $80 game pricing

**Source:** Nintendo Wire, quoting Alinea Analytics' Rhys Elliott (Nov 13): [nintendowire.com](https://nintendowire.com/news/2026/11/13/one-industry-analyst-believes-grand-theft-auto-vi-and-few-other-games-can-justify-80-price-tag/)

Elliott argues that demand for GTA VI is nearly insensitive to price, so an $80 price will tell publishers little about demand for typical games. He warns that it could become a "Trojan horse" for industry-wide price increases, and says most mid-tier games should be priced at $40-60. This applies the reference-price question directly: one outlier at a new price point could reset the category's reference price, as other publishers will hope, or be seen as a one-off.
