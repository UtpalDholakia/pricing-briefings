## Key takeaways

- A lifetime entitlement can still limit how much a buyer can redeem in a week.
- A personalized pricing model can give most customers a gain while reducing consumer surplus overall.
- Attraction and willingness to pay need not favor the same description of an unlimited offer.

## What happened

### 1. A $238 dinner lets guests decide what to pay

**Source:** [Eater SF’s follow-up](https://sf.eater.com/restaurant-news/213772/merchant-roots-pay-what-you-can-update-san-francisco) · October 8, 2026

Merchant Roots now offers a monthly pay-what-you-can dinner alongside its normally $238 experience. After two evenings, chef Ryan Shelton reports generous payments and enthusiastic guests. Alcohol remains at regular prices. Exact receipts were not disclosed, so this is an encouraging operator account rather than demonstrated profitability.

**Tags:** Restaurant pricing, Price presentation

### 2. Personalized pricing can benefit most shoppers while reducing consumer surplus

**Source:** [Toulouse School of Economics working paper](https://www.tse-fr.eu/sites/default/files/TSE/documents/doc/document/wp_tse_1779.pdf) · October 11, 2026 version

A structural model using grocery purchase histories estimates that one year of data could raise seller profits by 7.1%. Most consumers, 84.1%, gain. Yet concentrated losses among a price-insensitive minority outweigh those gains. These are modeled counterfactual outcomes, not results from a retailer implementing personalized prices.

**Tags:** Price discrimination, Consumer welfare

### 3. Loyalty pricing’s access problem reaches an unusually revealing source

**Source:** [Irish parliamentary questions and written answer](https://prelive.oireachtas.ie/en/debates/question/2026-10-08/152/) · October 8, 2026

Deputy Ken O’Flynn asked whether shoppers without smartphones should receive equivalent loyalty discounts. The minister’s answer says there are no plans to require equivalent discounts for nonparticipants. It also emphasizes clear disclosure of membership conditions and notes that some retailers offer physical cards.

**Tags:** Loyalty Program, Accessibility, Disclosure

### 4. “Pasta for life” has a carefully bounded definition

**Sources:** [Eater SF’s report](https://sf.eater.com/restaurant-news/213407/pasta-supply-co-cole-valley-shop-fundraising-san-francisco) · August 20, 2026 · [Restaurant’s offer page](https://www.pastasupplyco.com/cole-valley) · Checked October 19, 2026

Pasta Supply Co. offers weekly take-home pasta for $550 a year or $4,000 for life. The August report explains that the entitlement is one box weekly. Missed collections cannot be accumulated into a large pickup. The restaurant’s page still lists both offers.

**Tags:** Membership, Unlimited price offers

### 5. Customers can like “unlimited” more but assign a higher price to a finite offer

**Source:** [“The Limits of ‘Unlimited’ Offers”](https://www.amandageiser.com/_files/ugd/d33459_c7a5aa35fccd45ac84d9b879c1e9c5b6.pdf) · Working-paper version updated August 23, 2025

Amanda Geiser and Clayton Critcher compare unlimited offers with finite quantities at or above feasible consumption. Unlimited framing is more attractive, but quantified offers receive higher monetary valuations. The proposed mechanisms are higher anticipated usage and easier comparison with smaller, priced packages. This version is a working paper.

**Tags:** Unlimited price offers, Valuation

## Why it matters

**1. A $238 dinner lets guests decide what to pay** My interpretation is that discretion changes the social meaning of payment. The regular price supplies a reference point, while the invitation can activate reciprocity. Keeping the event occasional also separates access-oriented pricing from the restaurant’s standard offer.

**2. Personalized pricing can benefit most shoppers while reducing consumer surplus** “Most customers benefit” and “customers collectively lose” can both be true. For pricing governance, the distribution of gains and losses deserves attention alongside average outcomes and the proportion receiving discounts.

**3. Loyalty pricing’s access problem reaches an unusually revealing source** The behavioral issue extends beyond the price difference. My reading is that an advertised bargain can become an exclusion signal when customers cannot readily qualify. Physical cards address smartphone access, but do not resolve objections to joining or sharing data.

**4. “Pasta for life” has a carefully bounded definition** This separates a compelling lifetime promise from unlimited consumption. My interpretation: the weekly rule limits redemption intensity and encourages repeat visits. Long-term viability still depends on redemption, costs, and business survival. Early fundraising success does not settle those questions.

**5. Customers can like “unlimited” more but assign a higher price to a finite offer** A preference measure and a willingness-to-pay measure can favor different descriptions of effectively equivalent offers. For pricing research, the implication is to test both attraction and monetary valuation before choosing a frame.

## Ideas to apply

- For a lifetime offer, model redemptions under the weekly terms rather than using a generic unlimited assumption.
- Measure who cannot readily access a loyalty discount, including shoppers who cannot or do not want to use an app.
- When evaluating personalized pricing, show who gains and who loses alongside what happens to the average.
