## 1. The EU's Digital Fairness Act is out, and it targets pricing psychology directly

**Source:** Lewis Silkin — [lewissilkin.com](https://www.lewissilkin.com/insights/2026/11/11/european-commission-publishes-long-awaited-digital-fairness-act-102o9b5)

The Commission's proposal effectively bans drip pricing by requiring the full price up front. It restricts personalised prices that target minors or use sensitive data, and it requires sellers using virtual queues to disclose how prices change in real time and the maximum possible price. It also requires active confirmation before a free trial converts to paid, real-currency values for in-app currencies, and puts fake scarcity cues and pre-ticked defaults on the per-se blacklist. For behavioral researchers, a regulator is now writing default effects, scarcity heuristics and the "capped" dynamic price into law, and each of these is an obvious target for a before/after study.

## 2. Starlink moves home internet to quarterly, location-based dynamic pricing

**Source:** Light Reading — [lightreading.com](https://lightreading.com/satellite/starlink-gets-faster-ahead-of-v3-launches-but-shift-to-dynamic-pricing-could-backfire)

Residential prices will now follow local capacity and demand, with reported increases of around 20% in some places. Analysts expect the main churn risk in suburban markets where customers have alternatives such as fixed wireless. This is unusual: surge logic applied to an ongoing subscription rather than a one-off purchase, so the reference price is the customer's own past bill. It pairs well with Damavandi, Antia and Kopalle's forthcoming JM field experiment, in which a "market" justification for a price increase cut attrition by about 30% while cost and quality justifications did nothing ([haslam.utk.edu](https://haslam.utk.edu/news/ut-haslam-researcher-finds-messaging-matters-in-price-increase-notifications/)).

## 3. PlayStation Store Black Friday deals carry a "Price Depends on Your Account" label

**Source:** Push Square — [pushsquare.com](https://www.pushsquare.com/news/2026/11/ps5-dynamic-pricing-impacts-new-ps-store-black-friday-offers-roughly-usd5-variations-in-prices)

Deal-tracking accounts documented different discounts on the same title for different users, for example Astro Bot at $33.59 or $39.59. Sony has not commented, though a recent job listing mentions "intelligent pricing." The interesting feature is that the personalization is disclosed on the price tag itself. That makes this a live test of whether an explicit "your price is personal" label softens or sharpens fairness reactions compared with hidden variation like Instacart's 2025 tests.

## 4. The FTC's new personalized-pricing study also covers what platforms pay their workers

**Source:** Holland & Knight — [hklaw.com](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/11/ftcs-section-6b-orders-on-personalized-pricing-and-pay-what-businesses)

The 6(b) orders sent to nine airline, ride-share, food-delivery and retail firms demand every method that uses personal data to set prices, along with internal A/B tests, cost-benefit analyses and the exact wording and placement of disclosures. Platforms must also explain how consumer prices feed into worker pay. Most responses are due in mid-December. If the FTC publishes the results, this would be a rare view inside firms' own price-experimentation records, which academics almost never get.

**Type:** Regulation · **Tags:** Price discrimination, Disclosure, Regulation, retailing, Price Communications, Platform pricing

## 5. The FTC tells car dealers the advertised price must be one "any consumer can walk in and pay"

**Source:** Kirkland & Ellis — [kirkland.com](https://www.kirkland.com/publications/kirkland-alert/2026/11/ftc-and-states-signal-price-transparency-is-a-top-priority)

This roundup covers October FTC staff FAQs for auto dealers. They say conditional discounts (loyalty, military, trade-in) cannot drive the headline price, and the actual price must be the most prominent number shown. The roundup also covers warning letters to 24 large healthcare providers about pricing for scheduled care, and a Lens.com case over fees that appeared only at checkout. The auto guidance amounts to a regulatory definition of a legitimate reference price, which marketing researchers have long found hard to pin down.

## 6. Ahead of Black Friday, the UK CMA warns retailers that delivery fees belong in the headline price

**Source:** Lewis Silkin — [lewissilkin.com](https://www.lewissilkin.com/insights/2026/11/03/cma-issues-warning-to-online-retailers-about-delivery-fees-102o7ck)

The open letter says mandatory delivery charges must appear in the total price everywhere a price is shown, from ads to basket. Optional delivery costs cannot be hidden behind pop-outs or drop-downs, and marketplaces should list delivery-inclusive prices. Shipping is the classic partitioned surcharge in the academic literature, so UK e-commerce may now become a natural experiment on what happens to conversion and basket size when that partition is removed.

**Type:** Regulation · **Tags:** Price transparency, Partitioned Pricing, Regulation, Price Promotions, retailing, Platform pricing

## 7. Restaurant "admin fees" keep drawing backlash while menu prices rise anyway

**Sources:** Fox News — [foxnews.com](https://www.foxnews.com/food-drink/restaurant-admin-fees-spark-backlash-diners-question-what-theyre-paying) ; Kit Graham's Substack — [kitgraham.substack.com](https://kitgraham.substack.com/p/squeezed-increased-menu-prices-and)

A Reddit post about a 3% admin fee at a Denver Earls led commenters to say they would cut their tips to compensate. Florida now requires such fees to be disclosed before ordering, and one chain dropped its fee after a state inspection. Graham's October post compares old and new menus: Chicago restaurants that justify surcharges as an alternative to raising prices have raised prices anyway, some by 10–33%. Together they point to a testable idea: diners may treat the surcharge and the tip as one budget, so the fee is partly paid for by staff.

## 8. LLM pricing agents can sustain above-competitive prices through anchor-then-undercut cycles

**Source:** arXiv (Bichler, Hierhager, Köck & Pieroth; posted Oct 15) — [arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21711)

In repeated Bertrand simulations, LLM agents that see raw price histories, including rivals' prices, sustained above-competitive prices. Agents fed only summaries of their own profits drifted toward competitive prices. Mixing the two produced cycles in which one agent sets a high anchor and periodically undercuts it. The behavioral angle is that how the market is shown to the agent, not the algorithm itself, decides whether consumers face stable prices or a sawtooth of "deals" that looks like a sequence of real discounts.

**Type:** Research · **Tags:** Academic research, Algorithmic Pricing, AI in Pricing, Price Promotions, Discounting, Behavioral Economics

## 9. AI shopping agents fall for just-below pricing and promo framing only when search is costly and the goal is vague

**Source:** arXiv (Wadi & Ma; posted Sep 23) — [arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.28372)

With free information, LLM shopping agents were rarely misled by pricing cues. When gathering information had a cost and the instruction was vague, they skipped the attributes needed to compute unit price and made human-like heuristic errors. A specific goal largely removed the effect. This suggests charm pricing and promotional framing work through limited search rather than through anything distinctly human, which is a useful lens back onto human consumers.

**Type:** Research · **Tags:** Academic research, AI in Pricing, Price Promotions, Price presentation, Behavioral Economics, just-below pricing, Processing Prices
