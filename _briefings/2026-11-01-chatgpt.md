## Key takeaways

- Newegg’s price guarantee does not require the customer to watch the price or file a claim.
- In the zero-price studies, free goods attracted more takers, who took fewer units on average.
- The gift experiments distinguish a higher immediate basket from a longer-term loyalty response.

## What happened

### 1. Newegg removes the work from claiming a lower price

**Source:** [Company announcement](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20261030380021/en/Newegg-Brings-Shoppers-New-Doorbuster-Deals-Every-Day-This-Black-November) · October 30, 2026

For eligible purchases made November 1–18, Newegg promises automatic refunds if its price falls by November 28. Refunds arrive by December 8. Customers need not monitor prices or submit claims. Doorbusters are excluded, and differences must exceed $1.

**Tags:** Price Promotions, Customer Experience

### 2. Free goods can attract more people while discouraging larger quantities

**Source:** [“The role of social norms in zero price effects”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/experimental-economics/article/role-of-social-norms-in-zero-price-effects/62AF95538420785B6341938B196AB7E8) · May 26, 2026

Chinese field experiments using chocolates and masks found that free provision attracted more takers than a one-cent price, but takers selected fewer units. The combined effect on total quantity was negative. Separate norm-elicitation work found that taking large quantities looked less socially appropriate when goods were free. Field data were collected in 2021–22, and significance varied across settings.

**Tags:** Social processes, Consumer decision making

### 3. A gift can create gratitude and an uncomfortable obligation

**Source:** [“The effects of unconditional gifts on customer-firm relationships”](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11747-025-01129-x) · February 6, 2026

Two retailer field experiments found higher spending after customers received gifts before shopping, without a purchase requirement. Five additional experiments distinguished gratitude from obligation. Obligation was more closely associated with immediate spending, while gratitude supported loyalty responses. The field allocation procedure was not fully randomized at the individual level.

**Tags:** Customer loyalty, Emotional response

### 4. Paying before delivery changes the decision to return

**Sources:** [“Managing a necessary evil: Can payment methods reduce product returns?”](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jretai.2026.04.008) · May 7, 2026 · [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0022435926000357) · [FH Dortmund research record](https://www.fh-dortmund.de/publikationen/fis.import.pub.de.10782.php?loc=en-US)

Matched retailer data and a laboratory experiment linked payment before delivery with fewer returns than payment after delivery. The proposed mechanisms are stronger psychological ownership and greater perceived effort in returning something already paid for. Crucially, the effect appeared only with free shipping.

**Tags:** Customer Experience, Behavioral Economics

### 5. A refund retains the psychological label “already spent”

**Source:** [“Generous Returners, Vanishing Refunds”](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/bdm.70009) · January 28, 2025

Across ten studies involving 2,710 participants, consumers were more inclined to spend return refunds and select hedonic products than when using ordinary available funds. The proposed mechanism is reduced payment pain: money previously committed to consumption remains mentally tagged as spent. Effects were stronger among people normally reluctant to spend. Evidence includes purchase histories and scenario experiments.

**Tags:** Hedonic Value, Behavioral Economics

## Why it matters

**1. Newegg removes the work from claiming a lower price** The behavioral distinction is between reassurance and usable protection. Automatic reimbursement removes the effort that can undermine a price guarantee. My hypothesis: this reduces anticipated regret about buying early. Practitioners should measure purchase timing and actual reimbursements separately. The announcement supplies no evidence of incremental sales.

**2. Free goods can attract more people while discouraging larger quantities** The practical lesson is to distinguish participation from quantity per participant. A token price may weaken restraint associated with accepting generosity. “Free” does not necessarily maximize units distributed.

**3. A gift can create gratitude and an uncomfortable obligation** For practitioners, a larger basket does not reveal why the customer spent more. Test appreciation separately from indebtedness. The findings concern spending and loyalty measures, not demonstrated long-term retention. Gift costs also belong in the profitability calculation.

**4. Paying before delivery changes the decision to return** This adds a downstream consideration to payment-method design. A checkout option that attracts orders may produce different net revenue after returns. Practitioners should evaluate retained purchases and customer satisfaction together. The observational field component alone cannot establish payment timing as the cause.

**5. A refund retains the psychological label “already spent”** For practitioners, refund-related purchasing is a distinct context for evaluating offers. But a subsequent purchase need not recover the original retailer’s revenue. Experiments also found the effect when shopping elsewhere.

## Ideas to apply

- When making a price guarantee automatic, track changes in purchase timing separately from the reimbursements you actually issue.
- For a sample or giveaway, track the number of people who participate and the quantity each takes.
- Compare retained revenue after returns across payment options. Keep shipping terms fixed when trying to isolate the effect of payment timing.
