## 1. Sony's Black Friday sale shows account-specific discounts on the same game

**Source:** Push Square: [pushsquare.com](https://www.pushsquare.com/news/2026/11/ps5-dynamic-pricing-impacts-new-ps-store-black-friday-offers-roughly-usd5-variations-in-prices) · Also Beebom: [beebom.com](https://beebom.com/playstation-store-black-friday-deals-sony-dynamic-pricing/)

In the PS Store Black Friday sale (from Nov 4), first-party titles such as Astro Bot ($33.59 vs $39.59) and Death Stranding 2 ($34.29 vs $39.89) show different sale prices by account, labeled "Price Depends on Your Account (Dynamic Pricing)." Sony hasn't said what drives the gap. The interesting part is that the personalization sits inside a promotion: everyone sees "a deal," so people compare their discount with other people's discounts, not with a list price. That makes it a live case for studying peer-comparison fairness rather than ordinary reference-price fairness, and it shows the disclosure label failing to head off backlash.

**Type:** News / Practice · **Tags:** Price discrimination, Dynamic pricing, Disclosure, Fairness, Price Promotions, Discounting, Price labels

## 2. FTC orders nine airlines, ride-hail, and delivery firms to explain how they set individual prices; Dollar General sued over shelf-vs-register gaps

**Source:** Arnold & Porter (Nov 5): [arnoldporter.com](https://www.arnoldporter.com/en/perspectives/blogs/consumer-products-and-retail-navigator/2026/11/the-ftc-ramps-up-its-pricing-related-inquiries)

On Oct 28 the FTC sent Section 6(b) orders asking firms to document the inputs, tools, and procedures behind prices that vary by person, including loyalty status and algorithmic use of personal data. Separately, the FTC and 20 states sued Dollar General, citing about 29,000 "did not honor marked price" complaints. The 6(b) study could eventually produce rare firm-side data on how personalization actually works. The Dollar General case is an old-fashioned reminder that a posted price is itself a promise, and breaking it can erode trust the same way opaque pricing does.

## 3. A map of the US fee-transparency crackdown, from contact-lens ads to state all-in pricing laws

**Source:** Kirkland & Ellis (Nov 4): [kirkland.com](https://www.kirkland.com/publications/kirkland-alert/2026/11/ftc-and-states-signal-price-transparency-is-a-top-priority)

The alert covers the FTC's October challenge to Lens.com over low prices advertised in Google ads that left out mandatory checkout fees, warning letters to 24 large healthcare companies, an auto-dealer fee settlement, and a list of states that now require all-in pricing. Massachusetts also requires firms to disclose what each fee is for. The Lens.com case moves drip pricing upstream to the search ad, so the anchor is set before the shopper reaches the site. That suggests a research design that tests where in the funnel partitioned prices do the most harm.

**Type:** Regulation · **Tags:** Price transparency, Disclosure, Partitioned Pricing, Regulation, Pricing Laws, Legal issues in pricing, Behavioral Economics

## 4. Canada proposes banning fees for moving investment accounts

**Source:** Fintech.ca (Nov 5): [fintech.ca](https://www.fintech.ca/2026/11/05/fintechs-welcome-ottawas-proposed-ban-on-investment-transfer-fees/)

Ottawa would ban transfer-out fees at federally regulated banks and set deadlines for transfers. The consumer agency estimates these fees at $50-150 per account, and Wealthsimple says Canadians paid $73M this year to switch to it. This is a fee on leaving rather than on buying, a priced switching cost that leans on inertia and status-quo bias. It's a useful contrast to the checkout-focused junk-fee debate, and a possible natural experiment on how much a modest exit fee suppresses switching.

**Type:** Regulation · **Tags:** Regulation, Pricing Laws, Behavioral Economics

## 5. Opinion: efficient digital grocery prices aren't the same thing as trusted ones

**Source:** Salon (Nov 2): [salon.com](https://www.salon.com/2026/11/02/the-strange-new-math-behind-the-price-of-your-groceries/)

Ashlie Stevens argues that electronic shelf labels and loyalty apps make grocery prices harder for shoppers to read, even where personalization isn't proven. She places Walmart's pledge to price "the product, not the person" next to the earlier Instacart test, where shoppers saw different prices for the same item at the same time. There's no new data here. Its value is the framing: firms are now making public pledges about how they will not price, which raises the question of whether such pledges reassure shoppers or remind them the practice exists. It's an open, testable framing effect.

## 6. [Older: Oct 15] LLM pricing agents: whether they collude depends on how they are deployed

**Source:** Bichler, Hierhager, Köck & Pieroth, arXiv: [arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21711)

In repeated Bertrand competition, LLM agents that see raw price histories (including rivals' prices) can sustain supracompetitive prices. Agents that work from summarized profit histories and output price distributions drift toward competitive levels. Mixed setups produce cycles of a high price with periodic undercutting. For behavioral pricing, the consumer-facing result is that algorithmic price dispersion over time, the thing shoppers see as "the price keeps changing," may come from interface design choices rather than from any demand signal.

**Type:** Research · **Tags:** Academic research, Algorithmic Pricing, AI in Pricing, Collusion
{: .item-meta}

## 7. [Older: Sept 25] PriceBench: AI booking agents differ more than tenfold in price sensitivity

**Source:** Kireyev, arXiv: [arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.31468)

The study fits logit choice models to 3,600 hotel-booking choices made by 28 LLMs over 179 real NYC properties. Price sensitivity varied by more than tenfold, even within a single model family, and mean booked price ranged from about $247 to $393 on identical tasks. Weaker models anchored on list position. If agents stand in for consumers, the "consumer" a firm prices to becomes a model-specific demand curve. Position effects then become a new lever, and willingness-to-pay research needs a non-human respondent class.

**Type:** Research · **Tags:** Academic research, AI in Pricing, Hospitality, Behavioral Economics
{: .item-meta}

## Pick of the day

PriceBench (item 7). It turns agentic shopping into an estimable demand system with real heterogeneity and position biases, and opens a research agenda on how pricing psychology applies when the buyer is a model.
