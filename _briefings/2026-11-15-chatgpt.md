## Key takeaways

- A promotion can fail after it has already persuaded a shopper to come to the store. Test the price at the register, not just in the flyer.
- A subscription can become less valuable without an increase in its monthly charge.
- The terms around an offer matter. The same headline price can refer to a different unit, and two coupons with the same maximum savings can be judged differently.

## What happened

**Family Dollar’s two-for-$20 diaper offer.** On November 12, the FTC and 17 states announced a proposed $63 million settlement over allegations that Family Dollar frequently charged more at checkout than it advertised. The complaint describes a January 2025 diaper promotion that shoppers in at least seven states said stores refused to honor (sources 1–2). These are allegations, not findings at trial.

**Google AI Plus changed what subscribers could select.** According to a subscriber email reproduced by 9to5Google, from November 13, Plus subscribers lost the ability to select Pro models in Gemini and in Google Search’s AI Mode. Flash and Flash-Lite remained. The reported U.S. price stayed at $4.99 a month. Selecting Pro required a higher tier starting at $19.99 (sources 3–4).

**Steam is dressing its deals page for Black Friday.** From November 26 to 30, Steam plans to promote its existing Discounts & Events page. Publisher discounts still face the usual 30-day gap between ordinary discounts. Official seasonal sales, including the Winter Sale, are exempt from that particular cooldown (sources 5–6).

**Meijer put two 99-cent prices in one flyer.** Its November 13–14 circular advertised Cosmic Crisp apples at 99 cents per pound and bell peppers at 99 cents each. The large numerals got the same visual treatment. The units appeared in smaller type (source 7).

**Research discovery: minimum spend versus savings cap.** In seven preregistered studies, including a field study, Shangwen Yi and colleagues found that consumers tended to favor a minimum-spend offer over a comparable capped offer when the spending level that triggered the restriction was low. The preference reversed at higher levels. The offers could deliver the same maximum savings (source 8).

## Why it matters

By checkout, a shopper has already chosen the product and spent time in the store. A price mismatch asks the shopper to absorb the difference or give up that effort. The subscription case raises a different question: What did the customer believe the monthly payment bought? Losing the feature that justified subscribing can feel like paying more, even when the bill stays the same.

The Steam and Meijer examples show why offers need to be read in context. A themed page can look like a coordinated sale even when publishers set their own discounts. A prominent price can look simple until shoppers have to work out what the unit means for their basket. The coupon experiments offer a useful caution: equal maximum savings do not guarantee that two restrictions will feel equally fair.

## Ideas to apply

- Put an advertised offer through an actual register before it goes live. Check the receipt against the sign and the app.
- When moving a feature to a higher tier, track cancellations and upgrades separately among subscribers who actually used it.
- Before approving a flyer, ask a shopper to calculate the cost of two pounds of apples and three peppers without pointing out the units.
- Test a minimum-spend offer and a capped offer at the spending levels customers typically reach. Compare margin and basket size as well as conversion.
