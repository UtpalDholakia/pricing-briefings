## 1. A "discretionary" 10% charge that diners had to ask to remove is unlawful, South African tribunal rules

**Source:** News24 (Nov 20): [news24.com](https://www.news24.com/business/consumer-lookout/restaurants-cant-add-10-tip-to-every-bill-tribunal-rules-20261120-0501); Sowetan (Nov 19): [sowetan.co.za](https://www.sowetan.co.za/news/2026-11-19-restaurants-cannot-force-you-to-pay-10-tip-consumer-tribunal-rules/)

South Africa's National Consumer Tribunal found that TriBeCa Standard, a Pretoria restaurant, broke the Consumer Protection Act. It added a 10% service charge to every bill even though its menu called the charge discretionary. Diners who did not want to pay had to ask management to take it off, and the regulator counted about 200 such charges a day. The tribunal held that the menu did not show the full price payable. It imposed no fine, because no profit or loss was shown. The case is a clean example of a default doing the work of a price. The charge was optional in name, but opting out cost the diner a face-to-face request, and asking a manager to remove the staff's tip is a social cost few diners will pay. The ruling treats a default that is costly to refuse as part of the price. That is a stricter test than the usual disclosure question of whether the charge was mentioned somewhere.

## 2. The UK CMA issues its first provisional drip-pricing notice under its new powers, against viagogo

**Source:** TicketNews (Nov 2026): [ticketnews.com](https://www.ticketnews.com/2026/11/viagogo-cma-provisional-hidden-fees/); City AM: [cityam.com](https://www.cityam.com/viagogo-slapped-with-provisional-warning-over-sneaky-ticket-costs/)

The CMA has provisionally found that viagogo added mandatory charges, such as booking fees and taxes, at later stages of checkout instead of including them in the first price shown. The earlier fee cases under the CMA's direct-enforcement powers ended in settlements. Viagogo did not settle, so this is the first provisional infringement notice the CMA has issued under those powers. The finding is not final, viagogo says it is cooperating, and the next step is due in spring 2027. Resale tickets are where drip pricing does the most damage. The buyer has already chosen a seat and an event, often under time pressure, so fees that appear at the last step meet a buyer who is committed and short of alternatives. For the tracker, this is the first contested case, as opposed to a settled one, under the UK all-in pricing rules. It will show whether the CMA's view of "the first price shown" survives a defended case.

## 3. A price-comparison assistant cuts drip-pricing overpayment by 63%, but buyers then trust the platform less

**Source:** Lu, Markovits, Miller & Van Loo, working paper (SSRN; Yale Center for Contract Law and Economics): [papers.ssrn.com](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5565238); [ccl.yale.edu](https://www.ccl.yale.edu/working-papers-archives/measuring-and-mitigating-drip-pricing-overcharge)

_Older than this week (first posted around October 2025), included because it has not been covered here._ In an online gift-card marketplace experiment with 1,608 participants, fees revealed late in checkout raised average payments by about 10% compared with upfront prices, and by up to about 13%. A digital assistant that flagged a cheaper identical offer, with a link to it, cut the share of buyers who overpaid by 63%. That was enough to make transparent pricing the sellers' profit-maximising choice. There is a twist. Drip pricing lowered trust in the platform even though third-party sellers set the fees, and the assistant made that loss of trust slightly worse. So the buyer blames the venue rather than the seller, and an assistant that exposes the fees also exposes the venue. This is the first experimental estimate I have seen of what a shopping agent does to drip pricing. It suggests agents may discipline fees more effectively than disclosure rules do, as long as the good can be compared like for like, as gift cards can. It is an unreviewed working paper.

## 4. Delivery subscriptions rarely pay off for both sides: what the subscriber gains, the platform loses

**Source:** McCarthy, Oblander, Yang & Park, "Paying for Itself? Value (Mis)Alignment in Subscriptions," SSRN (Sept 25, 2026): [papers.ssrn.com](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=7525743)

The authors study nearly half a million subscribers across eight food-delivery subscription programmes. These programmes charge a flat fee in place of per-order delivery fees. Using difference-in-differences and causal forests, they estimate how much joining raises each subscriber's spending, and whether the subscriber and the platform both come out ahead. Win-win cases turn out to be rare. Thin platform margins cancel out even large increases in spending, so what subscribers gain and what the platform earns are close to zero-sum. The consumer-psychology link is mental accounting. Once the fee is paid, each extra order feels free, so the subscriber orders more to make the fee "pay for itself". That extra ordering is what erodes the platform's margin. It is the flip side of the familiar result that flat rates attract people who overestimate their usage. Here the people who do use the subscription heavily are the ones who cost the platform money. It is an unreviewed working paper, read at abstract level.

**Type:** Research · **Tags:** Academic research, subscription pricing, Membership, customer value, Flat-rate pricing

## 5. Anthropic prices Claude Fable 5.2 20% below its predecessor and charges double for a fast mode

**Source:** Anthropic pricing docs: [platform.claude.com](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing); Anthropic announcement: [anthropic.com](https://www.anthropic.com/claude-fable-and-mythos-5-2)

Anthropic's docs list Claude Fable 5.2 at $8 per million input tokens and $40 per million output tokens, down from $10 and $50 for Fable 5.1. Cache reads cost $0.20 per million tokens and batch jobs are half price. A fast mode, up to 2.5 times faster, costs $16 and $80, and US-only inference costs 1.1 times the standard rate. The flagship got cheaper, and speed and data location became separately priced add-ons. That moves the price from a single number to a menu. The 20% cut is measured against the predecessor's list price, which is a reference point the vendor controls. The fast-mode double rate prices impatience directly, so buyers who care most about latency pay more for the same model. Disclosure: Anthropic makes Claude, the model that writes this digest. The rates were read from Anthropic's indexed docs and will be checked against the provider page in the Dec 1 snapshot.

## 6. Diners say they would rather pay more than get less, but only by a dollar or two

**Source:** Toast survey (fielded Sept 23, 2026): [pos.toasttab.com](https://pos.toasttab.com/blog/data/restaurant-shrinkflation); Stacker (Nov 16): [stacker.com](https://stacker.com/stories/food-drink/restaurant-shrinkflation-would-guests-rather-pay-more-or-get-less)

Toast surveyed 850 US adults who eat out weekly. Half said restaurant portions had shrunk in the past year, mostly judging from memory rather than measurement. Asked to choose, most said they would rather pay more than get a smaller plate. The tolerance bands are narrow, though. For a dish they would normally pay about $15 for, 41% would accept no more than $1 more and 71% no more than $2. And 61% would not accept a portion cut of more than about 10%, including 27% who say any cut would leave them feeling shortchanged. Eighty percent want at least one category offered in a smaller size. A stated preference for price rises over downsizing, once the trade-off is spelled out, fits the finding that downsizing is judged less fair when people know about it. That does not mean diners would notice a quiet cut. The survey is vendor research and measures what people say, not what they do.
