## 1. FTC sends 6(b) orders to nine firms asking whether personal data sets their prices and their workers' pay

**Source:** FTC press release (Oct 28) — [ftc.gov](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/10/ftc-launches-personalized-pricing-compensation-inquiry)

The orders went to Delta, United, JetBlue, Uber, Lyft, DoorDash, Instacart, Target and Walmart. They ask for the inputs, tools and procedures behind any price that varies by person (loyalty status and algorithmic use of personal data are named), and the same for gig-worker pay offers. The FTC says this corrects its 2024 study, which looked at consultancies rather than the companies that actually set prices. Putting price and wage discrimination in one inquiry is new. If the responses are ever made public, they could give researchers rare ground truth on how personalized pricing is actually used, which so far has had to be inferred from scraped data.

## 2. UK CMA tells online retailers that any unavoidable delivery charge belongs in the first price shown

**Source:** CMA open letter (Nov 2) — [gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/online-retailers-including-mandatory-delivery-fees-in-the-total-price/open-letter-to-online-retailers-including-mandatory-delivery-fees-in-the-total-price)

Ahead of Christmas, the CMA says a mandatory delivery fee must be part of the total price at every step, from ads and comparison listings to basket and payment. Where every delivery option costs something, the cheapest option has to be built into the headline price. Optional extras can't be tucked behind links or expandable menus. The CMA says drip-pricing enforcement has already brought over £5.1m in fines. This pushes partitioned-pricing rules into shipping fees, where research on free-shipping thresholds and fee aversion has been strongest. It also raises an open question: will retailers now move shipping costs into product prices, or lean harder on minimum-spend thresholds?

**Type:** Regulation · **Tags:** Price transparency, Partitioned Pricing, Regulation, Legal issues in pricing, Menu pricing, retailing

## 3. PlayStation Store prices change depending on whether you're signed in, and Sony is hiring to expand "Intelligent Pricing"

**Source:** The Game Post (Oct 29) — [thegamepost.com](https://thegamepost.com/playstation-store-dynamic-pricing-sony-intelligent-pricing-targeted-offers/)

Price tracker PSprices says Sony's per-player price tests have grown from about 50 games in 30 regions to more than 190 games in over 70 regions. Players report the same game costing different amounts depending on account status, and one Irish user saw a price rise from €5.99 to €29.99 after signing in. New Sony job listings tie a monetization leadership role to scaling "Intelligent Pricing" and Targeted Offers. This is a live consumer case of personalized pricing that players can see by comparing logged-in and logged-out prices. That makes it a good setting for studying how fairness judgments form when people discover price differences themselves rather than hearing about them from regulators or the press.

**Type:** News · **Tags:** Price discrimination, Fairness, Regulation, Retailer Pricing Strategy, price increases, Platform pricing

## 4. Australia's card-surcharge ban is a natural experiment in surcharge vs. discount framing

**Source:** SmartCompany (Oct 14) — [smartcompany.com.au](https://www.smartcompany.com.au/finance/qld-supermarket-cash-up-30-percent-after-discount-card-surcharge-ban/) ; see also Inside FMCG (Oct 16) — [insidefmcg.com.au](https://insidefmcg.com.au/2026/10/16/two-weeks-on-how-are-retailers-absorbing-card-surcharges/)

Since October 1, merchants can't add card surcharges but can still offer cash discounts. That flips a classic framing question (a surcharge for card vs. a discount for cash) into a policy change. A Queensland supermarket reports about 30% more cash transactions in the first week with a 2% cash discount. A NSW café says its cash share went from 12% to 25% with no discount at all. These are small, early figures, but they suggest loss-versus-gain framing and simple salience both move payment-method choice. It's worth following as more data comes in.

**Type:** Practice · **Tags:** Partitioned Pricing, Pricing Laws, Discounting, retailing, Price presentation, Behavioral Economics

## 5. Card data on 174M consumers show generative-AI subscribers are highly price-sensitive and churn a lot

**Source:** NBER Working Paper 35953, Egan, Russel, Shi & Yao (October 2026) — [nber.org](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35953)

About 2.9% of US consumers paid for a major generative-AI service as of April 2026, mostly on basic plans of $30 a month or less. Annual retention runs only 20–45%, fewer people subscribe to more than one service, and switching jumps around model releases. The structural estimates find high price sensitivity and growing substitutability between services. This is revealed-preference evidence on an important new subscription category. For behavioral scholars, it raises questions about reference prices for AI: the $20 tier looks like an anchor, and frequent switching suggests weak loyalty and little status-quo bias.

## 6. LLM pricing agents: setup choices, not the underlying model, decide whether prices stay high

**Source:** arXiv 2610.21711, Bichler, Hierhager, Köck & Pieroth (TU Munich, Oct 15) — [arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.21711)

In simulated Bertrand markets, LLM agents that saw raw histories including rivals' prices could hold prices above the competitive level when playing against themselves. Agents given summarized histories moved toward competitive prices. When the two types competed, the authors found "anchor-and-harvest" cycles: one agent holds a high anchor price and the other undercuts it. The authors argue that auditing pricing agents means looking at what information they are shown, not just their prompts or models. That ties algorithmic pricing back to anchoring and reference points on the seller side, and gives regulators something concrete to audit.

**Type:** Research · **Tags:** Academic research, Algorithmic Pricing, AI in Pricing, Regulation, Behavioral Economics

## 7. AI shopping agents fall for just-below prices, but only when search is costly and the shopping goal is vague

**Source:** arXiv 2609.28372, Wadi & Ma (posted Sept 23) — [arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.28372)

Using a process-tracing setup where looking up product attributes costs a tool call, the authors tested eight commercial LLMs against just-below (charm-style) prices and promotional framing. When information was free, the cues rarely misled the models. When search was costly and the goal prompt was vague, the agents skipped the attributes needed to work out unit price and made choices that look like human heuristics. Specific goal prompts largely fixed this. The interesting claim is that "heuristic" behavior comes from search costs and how the storefront structures information, not from a built-in bias. That echoes old debates about whether human price heuristics are biases or adaptive responses to costly search.

**Type:** Research · **Tags:** Academic research, AI in Pricing, Price Promotions, Price presentation, Behavioral Economics, just-below pricing, Processing Prices

## 8. Field experiment: only "market" justifications for price increases reduced customer attrition

**Source:** University of Tennessee Haslam College summary (July 14) of Damavandi, Antia & Kopalle, "Cushioning the Blow," Journal of Marketing — [haslam.utk.edu](https://haslam.utk.edu/news/ut-haslam-researcher-finds-messaging-matters-in-price-increase-notifications/)

This is older but still timely. A 10-month field experiment with 1,600 customers of a Canadian self-storage company varied the size of the increase (5% or 15%), the type of justification (cost, quality, market, or none) and how specific the message was. Only market justifications, which reminded customers that alternatives were scarce, cut attrition, by about 29.5%. Cost and quality reasons did no better than giving no reason. That is awkward for the dual-entitlement view that cost-based increases seem fair. It suggests that in practice, signaling switching costs beats appeals to fairness, which also raises ethical questions.

**Type:** Research · **Tags:** Academic research, Fairness, price increases, Price Communications, Costs
{: .item-meta}

## Pick of the day

Wadi & Ma (item 7). It is one of the first careful tests of whether classic price cues work on AI shoppers, and its answer is that they work through search costs and storefront design. That reframes both the agentic-commerce debate and how we interpret human pricing heuristics.
