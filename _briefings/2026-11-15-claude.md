## 1. NYC passes a surveillance-pricing ban that writes a theory of "fair" price discrimination into law — Fox Rothschild Privacy Compliance & Data Security blog

**Source:** [dataprivacy.foxrothschild.com](https://dataprivacy.foxrothschild.com/2026/11/articles/general-privacy-data-security-news-developments/new-york-city-council-passes-surveillance-pricing-ban/)

The City Council voted Nov. 12 to bar algorithmic prices that use personal data to charge different people different prices (it awaits the mayor's signature and would take effect a year later). The carve-outs are the interesting part: discounts based on traits a shopper states (e.g., veteran status) or on the shopper's own purchase history with that seller are allowed, while traits a firm infers from other data are not, and marketplaces may not use purchase-history data to set reference prices. In effect it separates personalization the consumer consents to from personalization done to the consumer, a line fairness researchers can now test directly.

## 2. EU's official Digital Fairness Act text targets reference prices, dynamic-pricing ceilings, and in-game currencies — Captain Compliance

**Source:** [captaincompliance.com](https://captaincompliance.com/news/eu-digital-fairness-act-proposal-official-text/)

The Commission adopted the proposal on Nov. 11. Event sellers using dynamic pricing would have to disclose how many tickets are available at the advertised price and the maximum possible price, virtual queues would show the live and highest price, and games would have to show the real-money value of items bought with virtual currency. Personalized pricing is banned only when it is likely to harm known minors or vulnerable consumers, which is narrower than the leaks suggested. Mandatory max-price disclosure is effectively a regulated upper anchor, and it could provide a natural experiment on whether showing the ceiling dampens or inflates willingness to pay.

## 3. United blocks third-party AI agents from booking, partly to protect its own choice architecture — AeroTime (with analysis on DEV Community)

**Source:** [aerotime.aero](https://www.aerotime.aero/articles/united-airlines-to-block-third-party-ai-agents-from-accessing-digital-platforms) · [dev.to](https://dev.to/deanlee/what-united-airlines-ban-on-ai-booking-agents-means-for-consumer-agent-architectures-1oii)

United announced on Nov. 11 that unauthorized AI assistants may no longer search, book, change itineraries, or redeem miles, including through aggregators. It cited security and the FAA/DOT rule that a human must attest to what is in checked bags. The DEV analysis notes the commercial side: agents skip the merchandising screens where seat and bag ancillaries are sold. If agents strip out the interface where partitioned and drip prices do their work, sellers lose a behavioral lever, and this is one of the first major firms to push back openly.

## 4. Simulation finds a less accurate AI buyer can leave consumers better off — arXiv (Jiaqu Ning)

**Source:** [arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16471)

This Nov. 12 preprint models a duopoly where sellers make prices strategically complex and AI agents shop for consumers. A calculator-assisted agent picks the best offer more often (84% vs. 69%) than one that relies on verified-total disclosure, yet the disclosure regime produces higher consumer surplus in the worst equilibrium, because sellers respond to how buyers process prices. The welfare edge does not hold up under scenario resampling, so it is suggestive rather than definitive. The point for pricing psychology: whether buyers (human or AI) are good at handling complexity changes how much complexity sellers build in.

## 5. Class actions say airlines coordinated fares through a shared data "clean room" — Travelers Today

**Source:** [travelerstoday.com](https://www.travelerstoday.com/articles/61249/20261111/fare-fixing-suits-against-seven-airlines-target-ai-tool-tracking-your-ticket-price.htm)

Suits filed Nov. 3–4 against seven U.S. airlines and ATPCO's 3Victors unit target tools that pool carriers' shopping data (Iris), track rivals' fares in near real time (PriceEye), and read demand from live search activity before anyone buys (DemandView), under a "give-to-get" arrangement. The theory is Sherman Act collusion, not personalized pricing, but it shows how consumers' search behavior can become a shared input that shapes fares. These are allegations only; Southwest denies coordinating prices.

## 6. German shrinkflation watchdog: store brands shrink as much as national brands, and a bigger bottle can still mean a higher unit price — MarkenDetektive (German; data from Verbraucherzentrale Hamburg)

**Source:** [markendetektive.de](https://www.markendetektive.de/2026/11/04/mogelpackung-oktober-2026-rewe-lorenz-lenor/)

October's entries include Rewe and Netto private labels, Ültje trail mix (200 g to 150 g at the same price, a 33% hidden increase), and Lenor fabric softener, where the bottle grew about 20% but the shelf price rose more, so the unit price went up about 11%. The blog's review of all 470 entries on the Hamburg list finds private labels make up about one in nine cases, with typical hidden increases (~20%) similar to national brands. The Lenor case reverses the usual shrinkflation pattern: a visibly larger pack may signal value and hide a higher unit price.

## 7. Survey: many subscription cancellations happen because customers can't pause or downgrade, not only because of price — Bango/3Gem via GlobeNewswire (Manila Times)

**Source:** [manilatimes.net](https://manilatimes.net/2026/11/12/tmt-newswire/globenewswire/more-than-one-in-five-americans-cancelled-a-subscription-after-a-price-rise/2468711)

In a survey of 2,500 U.S. adults, 22% cancelled a subscription after a price increase, but 40% said they cancelled a service they wanted to keep because they could not pause it or drop to a free tier (50% among Gen Z). Also, 49% now pay extra for features that used to be included, and 58% would accept ads for a discount even though 70% say paid plans should be ad-free. The vendor sells subscription bundling, so read with caution, but the finding that missing options drive churn more than price alone is worth testing in the field.

## 8. Australia's ACCC lists the Black Friday tricks it has fined, including a countdown timer that reset to a bigger discount — ABC News (Australia)

**Source:** [abc.net.au](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2026-11-10/shoppers-warned-dodgy-discounts-black-friday-sales/107365588)

Before this year's sales, the ACCC listed recent penalties: digiDirect for "was" prices it rarely charged, Vuly for a timer that reset to a deeper discount when it ran out, and retailers whose "sitewide" sales excluded products. The sweep now covers social media posts and marketing emails, and new unfair-trading laws take effect next July. The reset timer is a useful example of urgency cues and inflated reference prices used together, and the enforcement record is a good source of real cases for teaching.
