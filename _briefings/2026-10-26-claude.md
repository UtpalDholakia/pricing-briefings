## 1. Prices ending in 9 absorbed tariffs; round prices took the hit

**Source:** SSRN / CESifo Working Paper 13056 (Hirshman & Ignatenko) — [papers.ssrn.com](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=7622838)

Posted Oct 19. Tariff costs passed through mostly to prices not ending in 9; 9-ending prices showed much lower pass-through. Non-9-ending products, including untariffed ones, also saw higher inflation afterward. This is a macro-scale demonstration that charm-price thresholds act as a real pricing friction. It also raises a question for behavioral pricing research: do firms protect 9-endings because they believe consumers attend to the left digit, and do they make up the margin on items with "non-psychological" prices?

## 2. Personalized grocery pricing helps 84% of shoppers but lowers total consumer surplus

**Source:** TSE Working Paper (Durrmeyer, Fournel & Samano), covered by Market Business News — [tse-fr.eu](https://www.tse-fr.eu/sites/default/files/TSE/documents/doc/document/wp_tse_1779.pdf)

Uses a structural model with a Bayesian seller learning from 2018 loyalty-card data (about 20,700 shoppers, 24 categories). One year of purchase history lifts retailer profit 7.1% over uniform pricing, about two-thirds of the gain from perfect information. Most shoppers pay less, but large surcharges on a price-insensitive minority outweigh those gains. The most interesting result: opting out of a loyalty program signals price insensitivity, so an optimal program could charge a membership fee and most people would still join. The opt-out decision itself becomes a costly signal.

## 3. Whether LLM pricing agents collude depends on how they are deployed, not on the model

**Source:** arXiv (Bichler, Hierhager, Köck & Pieroth, TU Munich) — [arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.21711)

In repeated Bertrand simulations, LLM agents that set one price per period from raw histories that include rivals' prices can sustain supracompetitive prices. Agents that output price distributions from summarized profit data drift toward competitive prices. Mixed populations produce cycles in which one agent holds a high "anchor" price while another undercuts it. For pricing scholars, the takeaway is that memory, information access, and update frequency, rather than the prompt, may decide whether algorithmic prices behave like collusion.

**Type:** Research · **Tags:** Academic research, Algorithmic Pricing, AI in Pricing, Collusion, Behavioral Economics

## 4. Retailers ask the Second Circuit to strike New York's "set by an algorithm using your personal data" price label

**Source:** Courthouse News — [courthousenews.com](https://courthousenews.com/retailers-slam-surveillance-pricing-warning-label-in-second-circuit-appeal/)

At oral argument on Oct 19, the National Retail Federation argued that the required disclosure is compelled speech that is technically true but misleading, since shoppers may assume sensitive data was used. The panel seemed skeptical, suggesting retailers could add their own explanation next to the warning. This is a live test of whether disclosure can address unease about personalized pricing, and an open empirical question: does such a label change purchase behavior or fairness judgments, or does it just become background noise?

## 5. UK CMA fines Wayfair £4.8m over fake countdown timers and "flash deals"

**Source:** Lewis Silkin (law firm briefing) — [lewissilkin.com](https://www.lewissilkin.com/insights/2026/10/16/deal-or-no-deal-cma-fines-three-more-companies-using-its-new-consumer-powers-102o5m6)

Under its new direct-enforcement powers, the CMA fined Wayfair for time-limited deals whose prices stayed the same or fell after the deadline. Appliances Direct was fined for pre-ticked paid add-ons and "limited time" codes that kept coming back, and Gold's Gym for leaving a mandatory joining fee out of headline prices. Treating recurring discount codes as unfair pressure is a useful precedent: regulators are now judging whether scarcity and urgency cues are true, not only whether prices are disclosed.

## 6. Restaurants keep "rising cost" surcharges while also raising menu prices

**Source:** I'll Be Honest . . . (Kit Graham's Substack) — [kitgraham.substack.com](https://kitgraham.substack.com/p/squeezed-increased-menu-prices-and)

A close look at Chicago and LA restaurants that kept or increased 3–4% surcharges presented as an alternative to menu increases, while raising item prices by up to 84%. Graham notes that menu prices at restaurants with and without surcharges are about the same, and that surcharges are then taxed and tipped on. This is good field material on partitioned pricing: the surcharge outlived its stated justification and now seems to work mainly as a way to keep the posted base price low.

## 7. Leafs fans revolt after Monday games move behind a Sportsnet+ paywall

**Source:** iPhone in Canada — [iphoneincanada.ca](https://www.iphoneincanada.ca/2026/10/21/leafs-fans-sportsnet-plus-monday-night-hockey-cost/)

Rogers made Monday Night Hockey a Sportsnet+ streaming exclusive, so cable subscribers who already pay for every Sportsnet channel must add a C$12.99/month package. This came shortly after a mid-season-start price increase of about 30%. Fan reaction is a clear case of reference-dependent fairness: what used to be included is now an add-on, and people experience that as a loss rather than a new offer. It also shows how much of the backlash depends on timing relative to a commitment already made.

## 8. Sam Fender disowns his own leaked tour prices and scraps premium "front standing"

**Source:** TicketNews — [ticketnews.com](https://ticketnews.com/2026/10/sam-fender-2027-tour-ticket-prices)

After a leaked price list for his 2027 UK stadium tour drew criticism, Fender publicly said the prices would be changed and rejected splitting standing areas into a pricier front zone, which he said he hadn't known about. The prices were fixed tiers set before the sale, not dynamic pricing. The case shows artists absorbing fairness costs for pricing structures set by promoters, and it suggests tiered spatial segmentation, not only surge pricing, is now a source of backlash.

## 9. Court says "+Fees" doesn't cure drip pricing in FTC v. Ticketmaster

**Source:** All About Advertising Law (Venable) — [allaboutadvertisinglaw.com](https://www.allaboutadvertisinglaw.com/2026/10/court-allows-ftc-drip-pricing-lawsuit-against-ticketmaster-to-proceed.html)

In denying the motion to dismiss, the court applied a "first contact" theory: deception that draws a consumer in violates the FTC Act even if the full price is disclosed before purchase. It called the "+Fees" label too vague to signal a markup of up to 44%. Ticketmaster argued that showing all-in prices would have put it at a competitive disadvantage, and the court treated that argument as support for the FTC's view that low partitioned base prices attract buyers. For researchers, this amounts to courts accepting the anchoring account of drip pricing.

**Type:** Regulation · **Tags:** Price transparency, Disclosure, Partitioned Pricing, Regulation, Pricing Laws, Legal issues in pricing, concert ticket pricing

## 10. Half of tracked dynamic price changes were cuts, data firm finds

**Source:** Fortune — [fortune.com](https://fortune.com/2026/10/18/dynamic-pricing-surveillance-prices-consumer-data-digital-labels-algorithmic/)

Web-data firm Decodo tracked 2025 online price changes across electronics, apparel, and home improvement and found about half of U.S. changes were decreases. That contrasts with the popular belief that dynamic pricing only goes up. A Simon-Kucher survey of 2,200+ executives cited in the piece found 28% use AI for real-time price optimization. The asymmetry between symmetric price movements and one-sided consumer perceptions (people notice increases, not cuts) is an underexplored research question. Caveat: this is vendor data, not peer-reviewed.
