## 1. After Australia's card-surcharge ban, one café in six raised coffee prices by far more than the surcharge

**Source:** The New Daily (Nov 2): [thenewdaily.com.au](https://www.thenewdaily.com.au/themoneyedition/cost-of-living/2026/11/02/coffee-prices-skyrocket-in-october-after-surcharge-ban); SmartCompany: [smartcompany.com.au](https://www.smartcompany.com.au/hospitality/coffee-prices-jump-50-cents-on-average-at-one-in-six-cafes-after-surcharge-ban/)

This is the first price data since Australia banned card surcharges on Oct 1. The Small Flat White tracker found that 17% of cafés raised the price of a small flat white after the ban, by about 49 cents (roughly 10.7%) on average. Another 79% left it unchanged and 4% cut it. A separate Hey You and Westpac DataX analysis puts the share closer to one café in ten, with nearly 40% of those raising by 50 cents or more. A typical surcharge of 1–2% on a coffee is a few cents, so these rises are far bigger than the fee they replaced. That fits the ACCC's warning that firms must not blame the ban for increases driven by other costs. The behavioural point is the "occasion" effect. A visible regulatory event gives sellers a reason buyers will accept, and the surcharge stops being a separate line shoppers could argue with. It disappears into a base price shoppers find harder to dispute. This is not yet a test of whether bans shift costs: the data are a self-selected before-and-after comparison, and interchange caps fell on the same day.

## 2. New: ProPublica finds at least nine firms putting tariff refunds into bonuses

**Source:** ProPublica (Nov 16): [propublica.org](https://www.propublica.org/article/trump-tariff-refunds-corporate-bonuses); Stanley Black & Decker 10-Q: [sec.gov](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0000093556/000009355626000039/swk-20261003.htm)

What's new since yesterday's item on the senators' letter: ProPublica went through securities filings and earnings calls. It found at least nine listed firms that put part of their tariff refunds into executive or employee bonuses. TJX accrued $112 million of a $331 million refund for incentive pay, on about $490 million of tariffs paid. Acushnet put $12 million of $45 million into bonuses. Stanley Black & Decker's 10-Q says its $298 million refund gain was partly offset by variable incentive pay. In dual-entitlement terms, a price rise justified by a named cost is accepted on the understanding that the cost is being passed through. Keeping the price when that cost comes back turns an acceptable pass-through into a profit taken from the buyer. Bonuses make this worse, because they show where the money went. The proposed class actions now filed against Walmart, Target, Nike, Lululemon and Stanley Black & Decker make the same argument: the seller collects the tariff twice.

## 3. Portugal's DECO catches retailers raising prices weeks before their Black Friday "discounts"

**Source:** Euronews (Nov 18): [euronews.com](https://www.euronews.com/2026/11/18/black-friday-deals-under-scrutiny-as-portuguese-consumer-group-deco-warns-of-dubious-disco); NiT: [nit.pt](https://www.nit.pt/compras/lojas-e-marcas/deco-alerta-para-descontos-da-black-friday-estes-produtos-estavam-mais-baratos-antes)

DECO PROteste checked the online shops of Auchan, El Corte Inglés, Euronics and FNAC on Nov 2–3 and reported several breaches of the EU-derived rule that a discount must be measured from the lowest price of the previous 30 days. In one case, FNAC listed Samsung earbuds at €249.99 in its Black Friday campaign after selling them at €199.99 in mid-October. A DECO spokesperson said prices go up about a week to ten days before Black Friday. The 30-day rule is meant to stop sellers from manufacturing a reference price. Raising the price shortly before the sale gets around it in spirit, because a recent high price is exactly what a shopper uses to judge the deal. DECO also reports that 75% of Portuguese respondents already believe stores do this. If most shoppers already expect inflated reference prices, the "was" price may persuade them less.

## 4. "$1.50 per can" makes shoppers more deal-sensitive than "0.67 cans per dollar"

**Source:** Journal of Marketing, Nov 2026 issue (Villanova & Bagchi): [journals.sagepub.com](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/00222429251375403); summary: [marketbusinessnews.com](https://marketbusinessnews.com/why-the-way-unit-prices-are-shown-can-change-how-shoppers-judge-a-deal/456703/)

"Multiple Unit Offers and Rate Calculations" (now in print in JM 90(6), after appearing online in 2025) shows that the direction of a unit rate matters. Promotions were more effective when the rate was expressed as dollars per unit rather than units per dollar. This held whether the seller displayed the rate or the shopper worked it out. The authors' explanation is price salience: a dollars-per-unit rate puts money first. The paper also maps which rate shoppers calculate when none is shown. This matters for unit-price labelling rules, which fix the unit but not the direction, and for multi-buy offers such as "3 for $5", where the seller can choose which rate to make easiest to compute. I read only the abstract and the press summary, so effect sizes are not given.

## 5. Slack caps its AI assistant at 15 messages a week, then bills Salesforce credits

**Source:** The Revenue AI Report: [therevenueaireport.com](https://www.therevenueaireport.com/blog/slack-enterprise-plus-45-slackbot-flex-credits-order-form); Markaicode: [markaicode.com](https://markaicode.com/pricing/slack-ai-pricing/)

According to secondary sources that cite Slack's help center, Business+ members get 15 Slackbot AI messages a week, resetting on Monday. Further use draws on Salesforce Flex Credits through a connected Salesforce org. Enterprise+ has no weekly cap, and Free and Pro get only a trial. Salesforce prices its AI the same way elsewhere: a seat with an included allowance, and credits for anything beyond it, as with yesterday's Agentforce Coworker seat. A small weekly allowance is easy to count, so people start rationing it, which is usage anxiety built into the plan. The overflow is also billed in a currency that sits in a different budget, which matches the mental-accounting split this database tracks for credits. A $45 Enterprise+ price circulates online, but Slack sends Enterprise+ buyers to sales, so it is not treated here as a list price.

## 6. Xfinity announces December price rises without saying how much

**Source:** Xfinity bill-changes page (checked by trackers Nov 15): [xfinity.com](https://www.xfinity.com/billchanges); ModemGuides: [modemguides.com](https://www.modemguides.com/blogs/modemguides-blog/xfinity-price-increase)

Comcast's bill-changes page says some Xfinity prices will rise from December 2026. It gives the reason as rising costs and network investment, and says customers on promotional rates or term agreements are unaffected until their term ends. It does not give amounts, dates or the services affected. Announcing a rise before giving any numbers delivers the bad news twice, first as uncertainty and then as a bill. It also lets the "rising costs" explanation settle in before anyone can check it against the size of the increase. Exempting promotional and term customers means the existing customers who are no longer on a deal pay the increase. These are the least price-sensitive customers, and the ones most likely to compare their bill with a new customer's offer.

## 7. The Philippines lowers its regulated airline fuel surcharge as jet fuel falls

**Source:** Daily Tribune (Nov 18): [tribune.net.ph](https://tribune.net.ph/2026/11/18/cab-trims-fuel-surcharges-for-rest-of-november); GMA News: [gmanetwork.com](https://www.gmanetwork.com/news/money/economy/1009898/fuel-surcharge-downgraded-to-level-16-for-november-16-30-2026-flights/story/)

The Civil Aeronautics Board moved passenger and cargo fuel surcharges on domestic and international flights from Level 17 to Level 16 for Nov 16–30, citing lower jet-fuel prices. The peso amounts for the new period were not in today's sources. At Level 16 in late October, the surcharge was ₱525–₱1,535 on domestic flights. The usual complaint about surcharges is that they outlive the cost that justified them. In this regime they cannot: a regulator resets the surcharge every two weeks against a published matrix. That makes it a useful comparison for the airline and shipping surcharges set by the firms themselves, which this site will follow to see whether they come down when costs fall.

## Pick of the day

Item 1, the Australian café data. Because a surcharge ban removes a separate fee, it is a rare natural experiment in what happens to partitioned pricing. The first numbers suggest the cost doesn't move into base prices one for one. Instead, a minority of sellers use the ban as the occasion for much larger rises.
