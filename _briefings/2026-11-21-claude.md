## 1. A UK government audit of 87 online sellers finds personal prices in travel and tickets, rarely in retail

**Source:** GOV.UK (Nov 19): [gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/price-personalisation-evidence-from-the-uk-online-market); executive summary: [gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/price-personalisation-evidence-from-the-uk-online-market/price-personalisation-evidence-from-the-uk-online-market-executive-summary)

The UK government has published a measurement study of online price personalisation. Researchers collected prices for 144 products from 87 online sellers, first through a survey of 1,688 consumers and then with synthetic profiles, and tracked the same products over time. Retail prices barely varied. Transport, package holidays, hospitality and ticketing varied much more, and about a third of the platforms checked in those sectors showed high variability. Some differences tracked age, gender, device type and login status, and one taxi platform showed a strong sign of fully individual pricing. The study found no sign that prices were set using the vulnerabilities it tested: financial difficulty, mental health problems and neurodivergence. For consumer psychology, the useful finding is where the variation sits. It is concentrated in categories that already have dynamic prices, where buyers expect prices to move and have no fixed reference price. That makes personalisation hard to detect and, so far, hard for buyers to resent. Most of the debate about whether personal prices are fair has rested on surveys of what people say they would accept. This study measures what sellers actually do, so later work can test reactions against real prevalence. I read the summary only, not the size or direction of the gaps between profiles.

## 2. When Airbnb fees moved into the headline price, guests got pickier, yet nightly rates rose 4.8%

**Source:** Jin, Wagman & Xing, "Clearing the Fog," NBER Working Paper 35989 (Nov 2026): [nber.org](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35989); SSRN: [papers.ssrn.com](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=7290239)

From July 2024, a California law (AB 537) required short-term rentals to advertise prices that include all mandatory fees. The authors compare Airbnb listings in San Diego and San Francisco with listings in Seattle and Chicago. Once guests saw the full price up front, they became more price-sensitive, as theory predicts. But average nightly rates rose 4.8%, against the usual prediction that transparency pushes prices down. The reason is churn. Hosts who relied on cleaning fees left the market, better listings stayed, and new hosts came in with lower fees and lower quality. The welfare model finds the law removed a "welfare illusion" created by hidden fees and raised realised consumer surplus, mostly through that churn. The mechanism is partitioned pricing. A low base price with a separate cleaning fee lets the host compete on the number guests compare, and the all-in rule takes that advantage away. So the main effect of the rule falls on which sellers survive, not on the price each one charges. This is an unreviewed working paper, read at abstract level.

## 3. Valve drops demand-based pricing for Counter-Strike stickers, six months after players mocked it

**Source:** SteamDB (Nov 2026): [steamdb.com](https://steamdb.com/en/cs2/cs2-major-stickers-fixed-price); GamesRadar on the Cologne prices: [gamesradar.com](https://www.gamesradar.com/games/fps/thanks-valve-a-set-of-counter-strike-2-cologne-major-stickers-now-costs-nearly-usd20-000/)

In May, Valve replaced Counter-Strike 2's sticker capsules for the Cologne Major with a token shop. Sticker prices rose and fell with demand, so a popular player's sticker cost more, and buyers got a token refund if a price fell sharply within 24 hours. One tracker put a full set of the top 100 stickers at $19,447. By August, Valve said the system had measured interest in players but had not produced reasonable prices. On November 17, with the line "we've heard your feedback", it put the Singapore Major autograph stickers on sale at fixed prices by finish. The 24-hour refund shows Valve expected buyers to feel cheated by a price that dropped right after they paid, and it tried to insure against that. What it could not fix was the absence of a reference price. Fans could not tell whether a price was fair, and the gap between star and lesser players read as the platform ranking the players. This is a clean reversal in a market where price is the only lever and no cost can be cited. Valve has not repriced the Cologne stickers, and its post does not mention them.

## 4. OpenAI merges ChatGPT and Codex into one usage allowance, counted in tokens

**Source:** OpenAI Help Center: [help.openai.com](https://help.openai.com/en/articles/20001625-understanding-chatgpt-and-codex-usage-limits); Notebookcheck: [notebookcheck.net](https://www.notebookcheck.net/ChatGPT-and-Codex-will-share-one-usage-limit-starting-in-December.1440897.0.html)

_Announced around Nov 2, not covered here before; it takes effect in December._ OpenAI's Help Center says that from December, Plus, Pro and Business subscribers will draw on one shared allowance for ChatGPT and Codex. Usage will be counted in tokens, so stronger models and multi-step agent tasks use up more. Instant chats reportedly do not count. Prices are unchanged, but OpenAI has not published the pool sizes, the reset periods or how chat converts into allowance. In mental-accounting terms, OpenAI is merging two budgets into one. A heavy coder can now use up allowance that a light chatter would have left idle, and every chat now costs something against the coding budget. A shared pool counted in tokens also hides the exchange rate. Subscribers cannot tell how much a given task costs until the meter moves, which is the usage anxiety that credit pricing tends to produce. It also means the same subscription price can buy less without any price changing.

## 5. Samsung's finance chief reportedly says the firm must stop selling at "giveaway prices"

**Source:** Sammy Fans (Nov 20): [sammyfans.com](https://sammyfans.com/2026/11/20/samsung-plans-stop-heavy-discounts-amid-weak-business-performance); SamMobile (Nov 19): [sammobile.com](https://www.sammobile.com/?p=16689574)

After a weak year in Samsung's consumer-electronics division, its CFO, Park Soon-chul, reportedly told managers that the TV and appliance businesses cannot keep shrinking and that Samsung must never sell at giveaway prices. Finance leaders also called for weekly inventory tracking and argued that frequent deep discounts hurt margins and teach customers to wait for sales. Coming the week before Black Friday, this is a seller admitting the cost of training buyers. Repeated deep promotions lower the internal reference price, so the regular price starts to look like a markup and buyers wait for the next sale. Cutting promotions is the obvious remedy. The risk is the transition: buyers who expect a sale will read its absence as a price rise. Whether Samsung holds the line through the holiday season is the real test. There is a single source, the original Korean report was not found, and these are internal remarks as reported, not company policy.

## 6. Texas bills would bar retailers from using personal data to charge individuals more

**Source:** Texas Legislature, HB 1184 (introduced): [capitol.texas.gov](https://capitol.texas.gov/tlodocs/90R/billtext/pdf/HB01184I.PDF); Houston Chronicle: [houstonchronicle.com](https://www.houstonchronicle.com/news/houston-texas/trending/article/texas-lawmakers-dynamic-pricing-bills-22529447.php)

Rep. Julio Salinas has pre-filed HB 1184 for the 2027 Texas session. It would regulate personalised dynamic pricing by retailers, online and in stores, and limit their use of personal information to charge an individual more for the same good or service. A second bill, HB 1242, would add a chapter on personalised dynamic pricing to the state's Business and Commerce Code. These bills, and the earlier ones in New York City, Maryland and Michigan, are aimed at upward personalisation. The fairness complaint is about being charged more because of who you are, not about prices that change with demand. That fits the dual-entitlement view of fairness. A premium tied to who you are reads as the seller exploiting the buyer, while a discount tied to who you are reads as a favour. The test will be in the definitions. A rule that bans charging some people more but allows personalised discounts lets retailers do the same thing by raising the list price and discounting most people. These are pre-filed bills, with no committee action yet.
