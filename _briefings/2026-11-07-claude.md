## 1. Sony's account-level dynamic pricing shows up in PlayStation Store Black Friday deals

**Source:** Push Square — [pushsquare.com](https://www.pushsquare.com/news/2026/11/ps5-dynamic-pricing-impacts-new-ps-store-black-friday-offers-roughly-usd5-variations-in-prices)

Deal-tracker accounts posted screenshots of the same first-party games at different sale prices, for example Astro Bot at $33.59 vs. $39.59, with a store note saying the price depends on your account. Two things stand out for behavioral pricing: the personalization is disclosed on the listing itself, and it is applied to the discount, not the list price, so the same anchor gives different shoppers different "savings." Sony has not said how accounts are assigned, so it is an open question whether this is randomized A/B testing or targeting, and whether disclosure softens or sharpens perceived unfairness. (Background on the backlash: [newsweek.com](https://www.newsweek.com/entertainment/video-games/fans-outraged-by-playstations-pricing-test-illegal-11652132))

## 2. Starlink moves existing subscribers to location-based "dynamic" monthly prices

**Source:** SatelliteInternet.com — [satelliteinternet.com](https://www.satelliteinternet.com/resources/starlink-introduces-dynamic-pricing/)

Starlink told US customers that prices now follow local capacity and demand and will be reviewed quarterly. Customer reports show increases of up to about 20% ($130 to $156) and some cuts of about 10%. Surge logic is now being applied to a recurring subscription bill, not a one-off purchase, which raises questions about reference-price formation and churn when your monthly fee depends on your neighbors' demand. The capacity explanation is a ready-made test of whether a cost or scarcity justification makes price increases seem fairer.

## 3. Australia's consumer group gives Ticketek and Ticketmaster "Shonky" awards for dynamic pricing

**Source:** Michael West Media (AAP) — [michaelwest.com.au](https://michaelwest.com.au/ticketing-giants-dealt-booby-prize-for-dynamic-pricing/)

CHOICE's annual awards criticized variable pricing on about 7% of tickets for a John Farnham tribute concert, and "in demand" NRL seats that cost about $50 more than near-identical seats one row away. Ticketmaster says organisers set the prices and that it does not use real-time algorithmic surges. The adjacent-seat comparison is a clean real-world example of how a salient, nearly identical reference option makes a price premium look arbitrary. The same list also called out Virgin Australia for keeping expired COVID-era flight credits, a breakage story for mental-accounting researchers.

## 4. Personal AI agents quietly steer "wealthy" users toward pricier options

**Source:** arXiv (Priyanshu, Vijay, Jabarian, Mireshghallah; Cisco and CMU) — [arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.24927)

Across about 325K runs on 13 models, 8 models recommended more expensive flights and insurance to wealthier synthetic personas making identical requests. Some kept doing so even when told to find the cheapest option, and they inferred wealth from unrelated email content. Blocking non-financial attributes sometimes made the gap bigger. This is personalized pricing by proxy: the price discrimination happens on the buyer's side, in the delegate, with no seller algorithm involved. Limitations: synthetic personas, mock inventories, and wealth treated as binary.

## 5. Charm prices fool AI shopping agents only when searching is costly and the goal is vague

**Source:** arXiv (Wadi and Ma) — [arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.28372)

Using an information-board method where each attribute lookup is a costly tool call, the authors tested eight commercial LLMs. Just-below prices and promotional framing rarely misled the agents when search was free. When search was costly and instructions were vague, the agents skipped unit-price information and made human-like heuristic errors, and precise goal prompts mostly fixed this. It refines the claim that machine buyers ignore left-digit effects (your Oct 17 Pricing Conundrum test found none): the bias seems to depend on search costs and how the storefront structures information, not to be absent.

## 6. How a pricing agent is deployed decides whether LLM sellers compete or keep prices high

**Source:** arXiv (Bichler, Hierhager, Köck, Pieroth) — [arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21711)

In repeated Bertrand games, LLM agents that post one price per period from raw histories, including rivals' prices, kept prices above competitive levels in self-play. Agents fed summarized profit histories drifted toward competitive prices. Mixed pairings produced a "high anchor, then periodic undercut" cycle, which looks to shoppers like frequent deals off an inflated reference price. A related experiment (Hillenbrand, Normann, Potarca, Werner, [arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.26861)) finds that a one-click coordination default on a pricing dashboard raises prices more than an LLM advisor does.

## 7. Michigan surveillance-pricing bills test where "personalized" pricing ends and ordinary promotions begin

**Source:** Capital News Service via Daily Press — [dailypress.net](https://www.dailypress.net/news/local-news/2026/11/bills-could-control-how-surveillance-pricing-impacts-what-you-pay-for-products/)

A bipartisan-sponsored House bill restricting prices based on personal data has passed the Michigan House and now sits in a Senate committee. The Michigan Retailers Association wants the law to separate individualized prices from discounts, seasonal sales and supply-demand pricing. That line-drawing problem maps onto open research questions: consumers may see a loyalty discount and a data-based markup as very different, even when the economics are the same. The article also mentions the FTC's August proposed enforcement policy on undisclosed data-driven pricing.

## 8. Grocery shelf labels have become a trust issue, and the union now wants them banned

**Source:** Salon — [salon.com](https://www.salon.com/2026/11/02/the-strange-new-math-behind-the-price-of-your-groceries/) ; AOL/UFCW — [aol.com](https://www.aol.com/articles/grocery-workers-union-calls-newly-220938000.html)

Salon argues that electronic shelf labels are not harmful in themselves, but that shoppers cannot verify that a price "doesn't know" them. It contrasts Walmart's pledge to price the product, not the person, with the Instacart tests that showed one store's corn flakes at $2.99, $3.49 and $3.69 depending on the user. Separately, on Nov 4 UFCW urged newly elected Democrats to ban both surveillance pricing and ESLs. Together they show a technology's mere capacity for dynamic pricing being treated as the harm, which is useful for work on suspicion, inferred motives and price fairness.
