## 1. AI shopping assistants can soften price competition and leave some consumers worse off, even when they recommend the best option — arXiv (Guo, Xu & Zhang, "Artificial Shoppers and Attention Competition," Nov 6)

**Source:** [arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09399)

The model has salience-driven consumers, whose choices are swayed by whichever attribute (price or quality) stands out in the comparison. It compares a no-AI market with AI "assistance" (the AI curates the comparison set) and AI "delegation" (the AI picks and the consumer approves). Assistance can relax price competition: a mid-quality firm may stop courting low-valuation buyers rather than cut prices for everyone. The authors also find an "alignment paradox": a correctly recommended, cheaper option can still push salience-prone buyers toward lower quality whose loss outweighs the savings. This is a clean theoretical bridge between salience theory and agentic commerce, and it yields testable predictions about when AI curation helps or hurts consumers.

## 2. New York City passes one of the strongest US bans on "surveillance pricing" — Gothamist / NYC Council press release (Nov 12)

**Source:** [gothamist.com](https://gothamist.com/news/nyc-council-passes-ban-on-surveillance-pricing-that-relies-on-personal-data) · [council.nyc.gov](https://council.nyc.gov/press/2026/11/12/3339/)

Intro 891-A bars charging different customers different prices for the same good when an algorithm uses their personal data to set the price. It covers online and in-store sales, with fines of up to $1,000 per violating transaction. It follows Seattle's grocery-specific ban and a New York State law passed in June. Business groups warned that discounts and loyalty pricing could be caught. The Speaker says discounts are unaffected, but where the line falls between a targeted discount and a personalized price is unclear. That ambiguity is a live test of the gain/loss framing asymmetry, because consumers tend to accept personalized discounts and resent personalized surcharges.

## 3. EU unveils the Digital Fairness Act: virtual-currency prices in real money, a visible price ceiling in dynamic-pricing queues, and active consent before free trials convert — Lewis Silkin (Nov 11)

**Source:** [lewissilkin.com](https://www.lewissilkin.com/insights/2026/11/11/european-commission-publishes-long-awaited-digital-fairness-act-102o9b5)

Several provisions read like behavioral-pricing findings written into law. Traders would have to show what in-game currency purchases cost in real money, which targets the payment-decoupling and token effects. Sellers using dynamic pricing in virtual queues would have to show how the price is changing and the maximum it can reach. Free trials could convert to paid plans only after the consumer actively confirms, which ends default-driven conversion. Personalized pricing would be restricted mainly where the consumer is vulnerable and harm is likely, which comes close to treating price fairness as a moral-harm judgment. The proposal still has to pass the Parliament and the Council, so the text may change.

## 4. Starlink rolls out congestion-based residential pricing: 10–20% increases in crowded rural cells, steep discounts in big cities — TeslaNorth, citing PCMag (Nov 8)

**Source:** [teslanorth.com](https://teslanorth.com/2026/11/08/starlink-price-hikes-spread-us-backlash/)

Subscribers in congested areas got emails citing limited network capacity, and some bills rose from $130 to $156 a month. New customers in Boston, San Francisco and NYC were offered 30–50% off. This is a rare case of location-based surge pricing on a monthly subscription, applied to customers with few alternatives, and justified by a cost/capacity rationale. Kahneman-Knetsch-Thaler dual entitlement predicts that this kind of justification should soften fairness judgments. The early backlash, including accusations of exploiting rural customers who lack other options, suggests captivity may outweigh the cost justification.

## 5. A 3% restaurant "admin fee" leads diners to say they will cut their tips — WFMD, on a Reddit thread about Earls Kitchen + Bar in Denver (Nov 9)

**Source:** [wfmd.com](https://www.wfmd.com/2026/11/09/restaurant-admin-fees-spark-backlash-as-diners-question-what-theyre-paying-for/)

An 83-cent surcharge on a roughly $28 meal produced an outsized reaction. The diner and many commenters said they would reduce or skip the tip to offset the fee. Elsewhere in the story, a Florida chain dropped its 3% operations fee after a state inspection. This is anecdotal, but it suggests diners treat a fee and a tip as coming out of the same mental account. If that is right, partitioned surcharges may be partly cannibalized by lower tips, which is an empirical question worth testing with POS data.

## 6. Survey: 40% of US adults cancelled a subscription they wanted to keep because they couldn't pause or downgrade it — Bango / 3Gem via GlobeNewswire (Nov 12)

**Source:** [globenewswire.com](https://www.globenewswire.com/news-release/2026/11/12/3401696/0/en/more-than-one-in-five-americans-cancelled-a-subscription-after-a-price-rise.html)

The sample is 2,500 US adults, surveyed in late September. 22% cancelled after a price increase, and 49% say they now pay extra for features that used to be included, such as ad-free viewing, downloads or 4K. That second finding describes unbundling working as a hidden price increase, a "skimpflation" of subscriptions. The pause/downgrade result suggests the lack of a middle option, rather than the price itself, drives some churn. The sponsor sells subscription bundling, so treat the bundle-preference figures with caution.

## 7. Grocery Outlet tells investors that branded opportunistic deals, not promotions, drive its value perception — Q3 2026 earnings call transcript, The Motley Fool (Nov 11)

**Source:** [fool.com](https://www.fool.com/earnings/call-transcripts/2026/11/11/grocery-outlet-go-q3-2026-earnings-call-transcript/)

Management said it had phased out what it called "synthetic" promotions and will stop promoting like a traditional retailer. It credits its value image mainly to unpredictable, treasure-hunt-style deals on branded opportunistic buys, which are also more profitable than everyday items. Traffic rose 2.4% while the average basket fell 1.9%. For price-image research, this is a practitioner claim that irregular, discovery-based deals shape store price image more than scheduled promotions do, and that is testable.
