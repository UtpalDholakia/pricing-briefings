## 1. xAI cuts Grok usage limits, citing costs, after merging Cursor into one shared allowance

**Source:** Forbes (Nov 2, 2026): [forbes.com](https://www.forbes.com/sites/siladityaray/2026/11/02/musk-says-grok-bots-costs-were-unsustainably-high-weeks-after-touting-rapid-growth/); Technobezz on The X Subscription: [technobezz.com](https://www.technobezz.com/news/xai-launches-the-x-subscription-premium-plan); Cursor's models and pricing page: [cursor.com](https://cursor.com/docs/models-and-pricing)

On Oct 12, xAI put Grok, its Grok Bot agent, the Cursor coding editor and X Premium into one subscription. There are five tiers, from $8 to $200 a month, and all paid tiers draw on a single weekly usage pool. Inside Cursor, Claude, GPT and Gemini no longer count against any plan's allowance. On Premium and above they bill at API list price from prepaid credit, and team plans pay an extra $0.25 per million tokens on top. Around Nov 1, Grok Bot users saw their weekly meters jump with no warning. Musk confirmed the cut was deliberate, saying the cost of serving the agent was "unsustainably high" relative to revenue. A one-off reset of everyone's limits followed on Nov 9. Pool sizes have never been published, prices did not change, and the explanation came only after users noticed. Two mechanisms are at work. A shared pool is a mental account the vendor controls: it decides which work the pool covers (its own models) and which work is billed separately (rivals' models). And an after-the-fact cost explanation with no number attached gives buyers little reason to judge the cut fair, so the reset does most of the repair work.

## 2. A platform's suggested price moves sellers only about a sixth of the way, and a lower suggestion earns them more

**Source:** Fong, Manchanda and Song, Journal of Marketing Research (Oct 2026): [journals.sagepub.com](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/00222437261432198); SSRN version: [papers.ssrn.com](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4993457)

In a field experiment on Mercari, the US resale marketplace, the platform randomly varied whether sellers saw a suggested price and how high it was. Moving the suggestion by 30% shifted listing prices by about 5% in the same direction. Sellers treat it as an anchor, but a weak one. Lower suggestions raised both the chance of a sale and seller revenue, and the effects were largest for new sellers and used items, where pricing is hardest. A follow-up experiment suggests the results hold once the market adjusts. Mercari then rolled out the revenue-maximising suggestion level, which also lowered prices for buyers. Almost all anchoring research studies buyers. This study is unusual in measuring it on the seller side, with real money, and the partial pass-through tells platforms how much power a default price actually gives them. One caution: the platform that sets the suggestion also profits from faster sales.

## 3. Tariff refunds made up about half of Target's quarterly margin gain, and none of it went back to shoppers as cash

**Source:** Target Q3 2026 results, Form 8-K (Nov 18, 2026): [sec.gov](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0000027419/000002741926000051/a2026q3ex-99.htm); Fortune (Nov 27, 2026): [fortune.com](https://fortune.com/2026/11/27/tariff-refunds-target-best-buy-kohls-shoppers/)

What is new is the retailers' own filings. Target booked a $301 million pretax tariff refund inside gross margin, worth $0.50 a share. Its gross margin rate rose 2.3 points to 30.5%, and 1.1 points of that rise came from the refund. Fortune adds Kohl's ($45 million) and Best Buy ($41 million), for $387 million across the three in their latest quarters. Fortune reports no plan to reimburse customers directly, and Target has said it is cutting prices rather than paying refunds. Tariffs were the cost these retailers named when they raised prices, and dual entitlement predicts that buyers will expect a named cost, once refunded, to come back to them. The filings show the refund recorded as margin, which is the outcome that turns a cost explanation into a broken promise. Whether shoppers notice is the open question. The repository's hypothesis on refund expectations is resting until Dec 2, so this was filed as a story update, not as a hunt.

## 4. Shoppers will let AI compare prices and hunt for discounts, but few will let it pay

**Source:** Visa 2026 Holiday Spending Shift Survey, by Morning Consult (Nov 2026): [usa.visa.com](https://usa.visa.com/about-visa/newsroom/press-releases.releaseId.22886.html)

In an online survey of 1,016 US adults (Sept 26 to Oct 2), 54% said they had used AI for at least one shopping task, up from 47% a year earlier. Many would hand over search: 61% would let AI compare prices and 57% would let it find discounts. Only 23% would trust generative AI to make the payment. The split matches a familiar line in delegation research. People give an algorithm the calculation but keep the step where money leaves the account, which is where the pain of paying and the fear of an irreversible mistake sit. If that holds, agent shopping will change the reference prices people see well before it changes who checks out, and sellers will compete for the AI comparison while still having to win the human approval. The sponsor is a payment network that sells agent-payment infrastructure, so its headline that 61% would trust Visa with an AI payment is self-interested.

## 5. Australia's price-gouging ban names its first eight targets, from potatoes to kitchen sponges

**Source:** Inside FMCG (Nov 13, 2026): [insidefmcg.com.au](https://insidefmcg.com.au/2026/11/13/accc-to-monitor-grocery-price-gouging-across-eight-product-groups/); ACCC on the prohibition: [accc.gov.au](https://www.accc.gov.au/media-release/accc-to-monitor-supermarket-compliance-with-new-excessive-pricing-prohibition)

Since July 1, supermarkets with more than A$30 billion in revenue (only Coles and Woolworths) may not charge prices that are significantly excessive compared with supply cost plus a reasonable margin. The law sets no fixed threshold. On Nov 13 the ACCC named the eight product groups it will monitor most closely: potatoes, limes, bone-in chicken, fresh milk, bread rolls, chocolate and snack bars, coffee, and kitchen sponges and scourers. Together they cover more than 1,000 items. It chose them from shopper and supplier complaints, how essential each item is, and two years of price and margin data. Its first report is due early 2027. In effect the law puts a cost-plus fairness rule, the one Kahneman, Knetsch and Thaler's respondents applied, into statute. Fairness is judged against cost plus a reasonable margin, not against what the market will bear. The list mixes staples with items that shoppers often complain about, such as limes and coffee, so complaint data is helping decide where the regulator looks. This item was overlooked by all agents when it was announced.
