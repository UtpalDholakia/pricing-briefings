## 1. Left-digit bias slows tariff pass-through: nine-ending prices barely moved

**Source:** Hirshman (Cornell) & Ignatenko (York), CESifo Working Paper 13056, SSRN, posted Oct 19 · [papers.ssrn.com](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=7622838)

Tariff costs showed up mostly in prices that did not end in 9. Prices ending in 9 had much lower pass-through, and inflation after the tariffs rose more for non-9-ending products, even ones that were not tariffed. This is rare macro-scale evidence that a consumer bias acts as a pricing friction for firms. It also raises an open question: do firms protect the 9-ending "threshold" price at the cost of margin, and where does that cost reappear (non-9 items, shrinkflation, fees)?

**Type:** Research · **Tags:** Academic research, Shrinkflation, Hidden Price Increases, Behavioral Economics, just-below pricing

## 2. Fast-food "value wars" judged a failure: discounts hurt margins without adding traffic

**Source:** Restaurant Dive, Oct 22 · [restaurantdive.com](https://www.restaurantdive.com/news/price-value-wars-over-fast-food-chains-shift-food-quality/833658/)

A BTIG analyst says the deep-discount war is over and that every chain lost. Value platforms cut margins without real traffic gains, and about a third of McDonald's franchisees did not run the $3-and-under platform as planned. Domino's had more orders but lower average tickets. Chains are now moving from price-point messaging to quality, which makes a natural experiment on whether built-up low reference prices will hold back the return to normal pricing.

**Type:** Practice · **Tags:** Discounting, Restaurant pricing, QSR Pricing, fast food, Hospitality, Price Communications, Behavioral Economics

## 3. Dynamic-pricing data push back on the "algorithms only raise prices" story

**Source:** AP, via CT Mirror, Oct 19 (also on Fortune, Oct 18) · [ctmirror.org](https://ctmirror.org/2026/10/19/consumer-apprehension-grows-as-us-retailers-adopt-ai-assisted-pricing-tools/)

A web-data firm (Decodo) found that about half of the U.S. price changes it tracked in 2025 were decreases. The vendor Relex says electronic shelf prices are usually updated weekly, not continuously. Walmart's CEO framed his policy as pricing "the product, not the person." The vendor data are not peer-reviewed, but the piece shows the gap between how consumers see algorithmic pricing and how it is actually used. It also gives a useful summary of state laws (Maryland, New Jersey, Connecticut) and the FTC's proposed personalized-pricing policy.

## 4. LLM pricing agents can hold prices high, depending on how they are set up

**Source:** Bichler, Hierhager, Köck & Pieroth, arXiv 2610.21711, Oct 15 · [arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21711)

In repeated Bertrand games, LLM agents that read raw market histories (including rivals' prices) kept prices above competitive levels when playing against themselves. Agents that saw only summaries of their own profits moved toward competitive prices. When both setups were in the same market, agents set a high-price anchor and periodically undercut it, a cyclical pattern. For behavioral researchers, the main point is that the information interface, a design choice, shapes the price path. Consumers will meet these cycles as reference prices.

**Type:** Research · **Tags:** Academic research, Algorithmic Pricing, AI in Pricing, Behavioral Economics

## 5. Court: drip pricing can deceive at "first contact" even if the total shows up before checkout

**Source:** All About Advertising Law (Venable), Oct 14, on FTC v. Live Nation/Ticketmaster · [allaboutadvertisinglaw.com](https://www.allaboutadvertisinglaw.com/2026/10/court-allows-ftc-drip-pricing-lawsuit-against-ticketmaster-to-proceed.html)

The court refused to dismiss the FTC's drip-pricing claims (fees allegedly added up to 44%). It accepted that buyers screen options on the headline price, so the deception happens at the screening stage even if the full price appears later. The court called the "+Fees" label vague, and treated Ticketmaster's argument that all-in pricing would have put it at a disadvantage as support for the FTC's theory. This brings the consideration-set and anchoring logic from the drip-pricing literature directly into legal doctrine.

## 6. A "moral harm" model of price fairness, now in print

**Source:** Campbell, Pomerance & Percival Carter, Journal of Consumer Research 53(3), October 2026 issue · [academic.oup.com](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article/53/3/444/8195730)

Across eight studies, people judged price fairness partly by whether a price seemed likely to harm someone. That judgment depended on the firm's strategy, how vulnerable the affected buyers were, and how much the product mattered to their welfare. Some findings go beyond dual entitlement: an unchanged price looks unfair when costs fall, the link from harm to fairness is stronger among liberals, and differential pricing can look fairer than uniform pricing. This is a useful lens for the surveillance-pricing debate, where the fairness of "paying more than others" is the central question.

**Type:** Research · **Tags:** Academic research, Price discrimination, Fairness, Consumer welfare, Costs
{: .item-meta}

## 7. German shoppers will let AI agents compare prices but not pay

**Source:** KPMG Consumer Snapshot 03/2026 (survey run by EHI Retail Institute), Oct 8 · [kpmg.com](https://kpmg.com/de/en/media/press-releases/2026/10/consumer-snapshot-when-ai-does-our-shopping-for-us.html)

73% would use an agent to compare prices and offers. For smartphones, 72% would ask an agent for information but only 45% would let it pay. Only 7% trust a retailer's or manufacturer's own AI, against 64% for general-purpose assistants, and 72% want to set budgets and rules for the agent. Delegating price search while keeping the payment step points to new research questions on pain of paying and price sensitivity when an agent sits in the middle.

**Type:** Research · **Tags:** Academic research, AI in Pricing, retailing, Behavioral Economics
{: .item-meta}

## 8. EU Digital Fairness Act proposal due around mid-November; Parliament wants event dynamic pricing considered for a ban

**Source:** European Parliamentary Research Service briefing, Oct 5 · [eprs.europarl.europa.eu](https://eprs.europarl.europa.eu/contents/publications/EPRS/2026/10/EPRS_BRI(2026)791529.html)

The briefing describes the current rules and the debate ahead of the Commission's proposal. Personalised and dynamic pricing are not banned as such, but changing the price during checkout may already breach the UCPD. Parliament asked in July that a ban on dynamic pricing for live sports and cultural events be considered, and the fitness check found 62% of respondents had subscriptions renewed without a reminder. It is a good primer on which behavioral-pricing practices may be regulated in Europe soon.
