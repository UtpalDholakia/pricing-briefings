## Key takeaways

- The FTC’s fact-finding inquiry includes coupons and purchasing opportunities as well as prices.
- A low monthly price can draw attention away from the cost of ending a contract.
- A deeper advertised hotel discount may lose its advantage if the competing option costs less in total.

## What happened

### 1. The personalized price may be a coupon, not a different sticker

**Sources:** [FTC announcement](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/10/ftc-launches-personalized-pricing-compensation-inquiry) · October 28, 2026 · [NACS trade coverage](https://www.convenience.org/stay-current/news/2026/october/29/ftc-orders-major-consumer-firms-to-provide-information-on-pricing-practices) · October 29

The FTC ordered nine companies, including airlines, delivery platforms, Target, and Walmart, to explain their use of personal data in pricing. Its definition explicitly includes coupons, promotions, and purchasing opportunities. This is fact-finding, not a finding of misconduct.

**Tags:** Price discrimination, Regulation

### 2. Toast’s “$0” offer puts the cost of leaving in focus

**Sources:** [FTC complaint announcement](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/10/ftc-states-take-action-against-toast-inc-over-hidden-unauthorized-fees-deceptive-subscription) · October 29, 2026 · [Toast’s response](https://markets.financialcontent.com/wss/article/bizwire-2026-10-29-toast-responds-to-ftc-lawsuit) · October 29

The FTC and state partners allege that Toast’s pay-as-you-go offer obscured charges, including an early-termination fee of $150 per remaining contract month. Toast disputes the allegations and challenges the application of consumer-subscription law to negotiated business agreements. No court has resolved the claims.

**Tags:** Partitioned Pricing, Legal issues in pricing, subscription pricing

### 3. Berlin gives paying more a different social meaning

**Source:** [Bode-Museum announcement](https://www.smb.museum/en/whats-new/detail/german-unity-day-at-the-bode-museum-pay-what-you-wish-on-3-october-2026/) · October 1, 2026

For its first pay-what-you-wish day on October 3, the Bode-Museum presented free admission, a €7 reduced contribution, the €14 standard price, and a €20 solidarity contribution supporting access for others. All choices provided the same exhibition access.

**Tags:** Social processes, Price presentation

### 4. A bigger hotel discount can lose to a $5 cheaper bill

**Source:** [“The psychology of price discount depth”](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/13567667261473698) · August 4, 2026 · Research discovery

Across five experiments, larger advertised discounts improved preference when competing vacation options had identical final prices. But the advantage reversed when the deeper-discount option cost slightly more. Study 5 tested $180 versus $185. The evidence points to perceived price attractiveness rather than inferred quality. These were hypothetical scenarios with online-panel participants, not observed bookings.

**Tags:** Price Promotions, Hospitality

### 5. Redeeming a deep discount may not prevent its later downside

**Source:** [“From Inaction Inertia to the Postdiscount Effect”](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/bdm.70092) · September 2, 2026 · Research discovery

Tieyuan Guo’s three studies challenge the idea that post-promotion resistance mainly comes from regretting a missed bargain. An extremely low prior price reduced perceived value and increased anticipated regret at the later price, whether or not consumers had bought earlier. Describing the low price as a pricing error mitigated the effect.

**Tags:** Discounting, Consumer decision making

## Why it matters

**1. The personalized price may be a coupon, not a different sticker** The behavioral distinction matters: a targeted discount can feel like a reward, while a personalized markup feels extractive. Yet that distinction depends partly on the benchmark price. Practitioners should document offer eligibility and net prices, not assume an unchanged public price means pricing is unpersonalized.

**2. Toast’s “$0” offer puts the cost of leaving in focus** My interpretation: “$0 per month” can dominate evaluation while contingent obligations receive less attention. The lesson extends beyond consumer subscriptions. Compare acquisition, inactivity, and exit costs together. A low recurring fee is not necessarily a low total commitment.

**3. Berlin gives paying more a different social meaning** This is voluntary pricing with suggested amounts and social labels, not an entirely open-ended payment request. My hypothesis: the labels reduce uncertainty about an appropriate contribution while giving higher payments a prosocial purpose. Practitioners can test contribution framing separately from access. The announcement provides no attendance, payment-distribution, or revenue results.

**4. A bigger hotel discount can lose to a $5 cheaper bill** The practical implication is conditional: discount depth can differentiate matching prices, but may not compensate for even a modest final-price disadvantage. Test the competitive display, not the promotion in isolation.

**5. Redeeming a deep discount may not prevent its later downside** The implication is that successful redemption need not protect future demand from a deep promotional anchor. Practitioners should examine subsequent price acceptance among redeemers as well as nonbuyers. The accessible abstract does not establish a field-sales effect.

## Ideas to apply

- Audit net prices and the rules that determine which customers receive offers.
- Show both the recurring price and the financial obligation triggered by an early exit.
- After a deep promotion, compare later price acceptance among shoppers who redeemed it and those who did not.
