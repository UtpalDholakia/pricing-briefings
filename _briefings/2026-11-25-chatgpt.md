## Key takeaways

- Ask what customers believe they already own before charging for continued access.
- Evaluate a promotion's effect on the gap between tiers, not just each tier's discount.
- Treat an established free-shipping threshold as a reference point, not merely a cost-recovery rule.

## What happened

### 1. New York draws a line between an installed feature and an ongoing service
**Source:** [New York Legislature, A10411A, signed November 23, 2026](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/bills/2025/A10411/amendment/A).

The new law prohibits subscriptions for features that use hardware already installed when a vehicle is bought or leased and function without ongoing provider cost or support. It expressly excludes navigation updates, infotainment, cellular services and software-dependent driving assistance, among other services. This is not a ban on every car subscription. The text specifies commencement 90 days after becoming law. My reading: the distinction makes an ownership-based fairness argument concrete. Pricing teams should explain the continuing service being supplied, rather than treating installed capacity as sufficient justification for recurring revenue.

### 2. Nissan's cashback does not put cash in the buyer's pocket
**Sources:** [CarExpert, November 24, 2026](https://www.carexpert.com.au/car-news/nissan-black-friday-deals-brings-dollar2000-in-extra-savings); [Nissan Australia offer terms, November 25–30, 2026 window, checked November 25](https://www.nissan.com.au/offers.html).

Nissan adds A$2,000 to existing offers on eligible Australian models. For the Qashqai, CarExpert reports A$6,000 in total savings. Nissan's terms clarify the mechanism: cashback reduces the purchase price at the point of sale. It is not redeemable for cash. That distinction matters for mental accounting. A separate reward and a lower amount due may be economically equivalent but feel different. That is an interpretation to test, not a measured result of this campaign. Compare the cashback label with an equally prominent final-price presentation, holding the payable amount constant.

### 3. Disney's sale widens the gap between its two bundles
**Source:** [Disney+ Help Center, November 24, 2026](https://help.disneyplus.com/article/disneyplus-black-friday-deal-2026).

Through November 30, eligible new and returning US subscribers can get Disney+, Hulu With Ads for $4.99 monthly for 12 months, or Premium for $14.99. The offer page lists subsequent rates of $12.99 and $21.99, or then-current prices. Calculated from those figures, the promotional gap is $10 monthly, versus $9 at the listed regular rates. Both tiers are discounted, but the relative price of upgrading rises. Switching plans forfeits the promotion. My interpretation: acquisition discounts can also steer tier selection. Measure the resulting mix and contribution, not just sign-ups.

### 4. A free-shipping experiment needs a narrower headline
**Sources:** [Lee and colleagues, PLOS One, May 13, 2026](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0349032); [supporting Table S1](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article/file?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0349032.s001&type=supplementary).

Older discovery. In 38 analyzed participants, shoe offers always totaled KRW50,000. Purchase intentions averaged 2.57 with free shipping and 2.55 with KRW2,000 shipping on a four-point scale. The reported significant intention differences start at KRW4,000. Free shipping did beat the smallest fee on an intention-divided-by-response-time measure. These are different outcomes, neither actual sales. The authors also did not measure quality beliefs, leaving a price-quality explanation open. Brain responses do not by themselves establish that removing a small fee increases purchases.

**Tags:** "Academic research", "Partitioned Pricing", "Consumer decision making".

### 5. Raising the shipping goal can make customers buy less
**Source:** [Zhou, Jin, Gümüş and Animesh, working paper posted March 13, 2026, revised October 15](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=6297139).

Older discovery, read from the current indexed abstract; the full paper was inaccessible. A difference-in-differences study of a North American online grocer links an $80-to-$100 free-shipping threshold increase to 19.4% lower spending among continuing purchasers. The decline was greater among customers more accustomed to clearing the old threshold. The authors propose reduced perceived attainability and report a separate randomized online experiment supporting that mechanism. This is a working paper, not a universal elasticity. The practical question is whether a higher goal encourages topping up or makes the goal feel out of reach.

## Why it matters

My synthesis: the component charged, the alternative beside it and the customer's prior experience belong in the pricing decision. None can be inferred from the total price alone.

## Ideas to apply

- Test equal-payable-amount promotion labels before attributing a lift to cashback.
- Track tier contribution and later switching when discounts differ across plans.
- Pilot threshold changes by prior basket behavior. Count abandoned baskets, not only completed orders.
