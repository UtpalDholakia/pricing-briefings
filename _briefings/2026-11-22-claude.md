## 1. WWE prices its top seats for the rich and keeps its cheap seats just high enough to deter brokers

**Source:** F4W/WON (Nov 2026): [f4wonline.com](https://www.f4wonline.com/news/wwe/nick-khan-explains-why-wwe-cant-make-ticket-prices-too-family-friendly/); also Slam Wrestling: [slamwrestling.net](https://slamwrestling.net/news/nick-khan-addresses-high-wwe-ticket-prices/)

Fans have complained about being priced out, and WWE president Nick Khan answered by describing the policy at both ends of the house (at Bloomberg's Screentime conference). Premium seats are priced as high as the wealthiest buyers will go. Upper-bowl seats are meant to be affordable for families, but he argued that pricing them too low hands them to ticket brokers, who resell them at a markup. It is unusual for a seller to explain price discrimination this openly. The second half is the more interesting argument for fairness research: a price set below what the market will bear gets rationed by resellers, so a modest floor price is presented as a way to protect fans, not exploit them. Whether fans accept that reasoning, or read it as the seller keeping the resale margin for itself, is open. This is a dual-entitlement question that ticketing has never settled.

## 2. Disney raises Christmas-week tickets by $5 and adds a cheaper ticket that leaves out Magic Kingdom

**Source:** WDW News Today (Nov 2026): [wdwnt.com](https://wdwnt.com/2026/11/walt-disney-world-raises-ticket-prices-but-adds-new-ticket-option-for-week-of-christmas-2026/); also Disney Tourist Blog: [disneytouristblog.com](https://www.disneytouristblog.com/disney-world-raises-christmas-2026-ticket-prices-debuts-new-3-park-deal/)

For December 24-31, Walt Disney World raised one-day prices by $5 at all four parks (Magic Kingdom goes from $209 to $214). At the same time it added a 2- or 3-day ticket valid only at EPCOT, Hollywood Studios and Animal Kingdom. Fan sites put the saving at about $6 on two days and $8 on three, and Park Hopper can't be added. This is a damaged-goods version: the same product with its most valued part removed, sold slightly cheaper so that price-sensitive visitors sort themselves out. Disney fan sites called it a "deal" in scare quotes. Their coverage shows how a small saving against a just-raised price reads to buyers: as a fee for wanting the whole product, not as a discount. Some outlets also noted that families had already seen the lower rates, so the old price is now a reference point in their minds.

**Type:** Practice · **Tags:** Good-Better-Best Pricing, Price discrimination, price increases, Resort pricing, Variable pricing

## 3. The KPop Demon Hunters tour presale draws anger at concert pricing for a children's film

**Source:** Yahoo Entertainment (Nov 2026): [yahoo.com](https://www.yahoo.com/entertainment/music/concerts/articles/kpop-demon-hunters-world-tour-200347155.html); also Parade: [parade.com](https://parade.com/news/kpop-demon-hunters-world-tour-faces-backlash-over-ticket-prices)

Presales for Netflix and AEG's 2027-28 Golden World Tour opened November 18-19, and the general sale opened November 20. One fan said they watched seats climb from about $200 to $600 within minutes. Others reported lower-bowl seats above $700 and VIP packages from about $593 in Atlanta, €682 in Dublin and £635 in London. A Ticketmaster listing for the Belmont Park show gives face values of $97-$468 and warns that resale may exceed face value, so some of the highest figures are probably resale or premium tiers. For psychology, the audience matters. A family show makes the price feel like a moral breach, because parents measure it against what a children's outing should cost. Watching a price rise while you queue makes the higher price look like the seller exploiting demand. I have filed it as a pending backlash case.

## 4. Showing an off-peak discount as a percentage changes behaviour only on cheap trips

**Source:** arXiv (Wallimann et al., Nov 2026): [arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.12117)

This preprint ran a randomised stated-preference survey alongside a Swiss public-transport field test of off-peak discounts (1,082 respondents, a 39.6% response rate). Respondents saw fare differences either as percentages or as absolute amounts. Overall, the format made no significant difference to price sensitivity. For low-fare trips, percentages made respondents more price-sensitive than the equivalent currency amount. The authors suggest using percentage messaging for cheap products and for discounts shallower than about 47%. The result fits the classic rule of thumb that percentages look bigger on cheap items and absolute amounts on expensive ones. Here the advantage of percentages fades as fares rise and never turns into an advantage for currency amounts. The choices are stated, not observed in real travel, and the paper has not been reviewed.

**Type:** Research · **Tags:** Academic research, Price presentation, Discounting, Processing Prices, Variable pricing
{: .item-meta}

## 5. Teamwork.com bills AI from a shared monthly credit pool that expires and pauses when it runs out

**Source:** Teamwork.com blog (Nov 2026): [teamwork.com](https://www.teamwork.com/blog/teamwork-ai-credits/); change log: [pricingsaas.com](https://pricingsaas.com/companies/teamwork/diffs/2026W47)

From November 10, every paid Teamwork plan includes a monthly AI credit allowance: 1,000 on Basic, 2,500 on Accelerate and 7,500 on Optimize and Enterprise. The allowance is held at account level, so the whole team shares one balance, and it doesn't grow as seats are added. Unused credits expire at the monthly reset. Top-up credits cost $0.01 each and last six months. When the pool runs out, AI features stop until the next reset. Plan prices did not change. The design stacks three behavioural levers. A shared pool turns each user's AI use into a cost borne by colleagues, which can damp use the way a shared fuel card does. Monthly expiry creates breakage. A hard stop, rather than overage billing, swaps bill shock for usage anxiety. I couldn't find how many credits each AI action uses. That is the exchange rate the vendor controls, and it is the open question in the credits deep dive.

**Type:** Practice · **Tags:** Credit-based pricing, Pricing AI products, SaaS pricing, Usage-based pricing, Metered Pricing
{: .item-meta}

## 6. UK CMA opens drip-pricing cases against a gym chain and two travel sellers

**Source:** GOV.UK (Nov 18): [gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/travel-and-leisure-businesses-investigated-over-pricing-practices); also Lewis Silkin: [lewissilkin.com](https://www.lewissilkin.com/insights/2026/11/18/drip-pricing-cma-opens-three-new-investigations-into-holiday-and-gym-pricing-102oa5o)

New since the viagogo notice covered on November 20: the CMA has opened three more investigations under its direct-enforcement powers. Bannatyne Health Club is accused of adding joining fees and facility charges late in the sign-up process. Secret Escapes is questioned over ATOL fees, local taxes, resort fees and component prices that rise during booking. Caledonian Travel's case concerns indicative per-person prices. That brings the CMA to 23 investigations under the new powers, and no breach has been found yet. Gyms are a telling target. A government-linked review found drip pricing at 60% of gym sellers reviewed. A joining fee shown only at checkout works on buyers who are already committed to the sign-up, which is when they are least likely to walk away. The initial phase runs to April 2027.

**Type:** Regulation · **Tags:** Partitioned Pricing, Price transparency, Regulation, Pricing Laws, Membership

## 7. A pricing model in which a firm's ability to personalise prices is limited by who compares prices with whom

**Source:** SSRN (Chatterjea & Park, Nov 16): [ssrn.com](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=7717161)

This theory paper models consumers who judge whether a price is fair by comparing it with the prices paid by people they are socially connected to. Perceived unfairness grows with price gaps along the comparison network, and the firm maximises profit subject to a cap on total unfairness, which the authors call a "trust budget". The firm has a unique optimal set of personalised prices. Adding a comparison link can never raise the firm's maximum profit. The practical reading is that the limit on personal pricing is set by which customers compare prices with each other, not by data. Personalisation is cheapest across groups that never compare notes and most costly inside friend groups, families and online communities. It is a formal model with no data, but it gives a precise prediction for survey work on fairness in personalised pricing.

**Type:** Research · **Tags:** Academic research, Price discrimination, Fairness, Social processes, Algorithmic Pricing
{: .item-meta}
