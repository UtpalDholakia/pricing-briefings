## 1. Walmart's rollbacks fall from 11,000 to 7,700 as tariff refunds go to paying for diesel

**Source:** CNBC (Nov 19, 2026): [cnbc.com](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/19/walmart-wmt-q3-2026-earnings.html); rollback counts: [progressivegrocer.com](https://progressivegrocer.com/price-investments-accelerated-delivery-drive-walmarts-q3-results); the August refund pledge: [modernretail.co](https://www.modernretail.co/operations/walmart-gets-close-to-3-billion-in-tariff-refunds/)

Walmart U.S. ended its third quarter with about 7,700 price rollbacks. That is down from more than 11,000 at the end of the second quarter, though still above its usual 5,000 to 5,500. CEO John Furner told CNBC that diesel costs came in higher than expected and that tariff refunds helped offset them and keep prices low. Executives now expect more than $2 billion in extra diesel and transport costs this year. In August, the roughly $2.9 billion refund was presented as money for price cuts. Now it is presented as money that absorbs a different cost. No list price went up, so this isn't a price increase. The question is what shoppers had come to expect. Dual entitlement predicts that buyers accept prices that track costs, and a public pledge to pass on a refund makes the expected cut part of the reference transaction. A shrinking rollback count is a quiet way to take back that expectation. Whether shoppers notice a falling count, rather than a rising price, is an open question, and I have filed it as one.

## 2. Sellers think a price quoted by an AI varies less than one quoted by a person, and prefer the AI buyer for it

**Source:** Journal of Retailing, in press (2026): [sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0022435926000709)

The AI-pricing fairness debate so far asks whether an AI price is *fairer*. This paper asks whether it is *more predictable*. In a resale setting, consumers believed that quotes and trading prices produced by an AI agent fall in a narrower range than those produced by a human agent, across utilitarian and hedonic products. That belief drove a preference for selling to an AI buyer, most strongly among people who lack confidence in their own bargaining. The mechanism is perceived variance, not perceived intent. Someone who expects a tight distribution of offers has less reason to fear a lowball offer, so the AI counterpart reduces the cost of being a poor negotiator. It also hints at a risk. If AI-set prices are assumed to be consistent, buyers may check them less, even when the algorithm personalises. I read only the abstract, so the effect sizes are unknown. The paper is filed as a Boundary pointer on the AI-attribution claim.

## 3. Reference prices belong to the person who shops, and household-level models get them wrong

**Source:** Journal of Retailing (2026): [sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0022435926000291)

Most reference-price models are estimated on household scanner panels, as if the household were a single shopper. This study uses a panel that records which household member made each purchase. It estimates reference-price choice models for three categories at both levels and finds the household estimates substantially different from the individual ones. Within the same household, frequent shoppers are more price sensitive and respond differently to gains and losses against their reference price than infrequent shoppers. For theory, a reference price lives in someone's memory, and only the person who buys has seen the price history. For practice, a retailer that targets promotions on household-level estimates is pricing for an average shopper who doesn't exist, and the abstract says this has a cost. I could not read how large it is.

## 4. Mistral's pricing page quietly stops listing the API credits that came with its Pro plan

**Source:** AgentDeals pricing-change digest (Nov 16–22, 2026): [agentdeals.dev](https://agentdeals.dev/digest/2026-w47); change record: [github.com](https://github.com/robhunter/agentdeals/issues/3523)

A third-party tracker compared an archived copy of Mistral's pricing page from November 17 with its own reading on November 20. In that window, the line giving Pro subscribers $25.50 a month in API credits disappeared, along with the $15 credit on the Education plan. The plan prices did not change. The Pro credit had already been cut from $30 in October, and the Free plan lost its $10 credit at the same time. I found no announcement. This is unbundling by omission. The subscription price stays as a visible anchor while a bundled component worth about half of it stops being promised, which is the kind of change buyers notice least because nothing they see goes up. One caution: this rests on the tracker's comparison, not on a statement from Mistral. I will check Mistral's page at the December 1 snapshot before logging it as an allowance cut.

## 5. Ten more states join the FTC's case that Dollar General charges more at the register than on the shelf

**Source:** FTC press release (Nov 20, 2026): [ftc.gov](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/11/ten-additional-states-join-ftc-lawsuit-alleging-dollar-general-charged-consumers-more-advertised)

The amended complaint brings the case to the FTC plus 30 states. It alleges that from 2019 to March 2026, Dollar General routinely charged more at checkout than its shelf tags and ads showed, and knew it. Dollar General says it will fight the case, and no court has ruled. What is new is the scale. Eight days after Family Dollar's $63 million settlement, regulators are treating the posted price as a promise and a register mismatch as a deceptive practice, not a clerical slip. The psychology is in who loses. Shoppers on tight budgets, buying many low-priced items, are the least likely to compare receipts with shelf tags. A shelf price is a reference point that most shoppers never check against the receipt.
