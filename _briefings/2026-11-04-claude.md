## 1. Tips act as an effort contract, and firms pocket about a third of them

**Source:** Marginal Revolution, on Balla-Elliott & Norwich, "The Economics of Tips" (Norwich's University of Chicago job market paper) · [marginalrevolution.com](https://marginalrevolution.com/marginalrevolution/2026/11/the-economics-of-tips.html) · Paper: [pdfs.isaacnorwich.com](https://pdfs.isaacnorwich.com/Norwich_JMP.pdf)

Using matched POS and payroll data from about 20,000 US firms, the authors find tips are a roughly fixed share of revenue. When firms start collecting tips, they cut base wages by 23 to 33 cents per tip dollar. When local demand rises, revenue grows about twice as fast at tipping firms, with no change in prices or base wages. This recasts the tipping-screen debate: the tip is a revenue-share incentive that lifts firm profit by about 30% where customers tip, which helps explain why tipping keeps spreading. That is a supply-side counterpart to the consumer "tip fatigue" literature.

## 2. Carnival tags prepaid tippers with "Crew Love" stickers

**Source:** Fox News Travel (Nov 1) · [foxnews.com](https://www.foxnews.com/travel/cruise-passengers-criticize-new-program-identifying-early-tippers-board)

In a pilot, Carnival marks the cards of guests who prepaid gratuities, adds a thank-you note in the cabin, and shows the status to crew. A TikTok video set off a split reaction: some called it shaming guests who can't prepay, while others welcomed recognition and expect better service. This makes a normally private payment visible as a status signal. It's a ready-made field setting for studying the social-image motive in tipping, and whether visibility raises prepayment or causes reactance.

## 4. Why "we don't personalize prices" is cheap talk inside an app

**Source:** Business Model Analyst (Oct 26) · [businessmodelanalyst.com](https://businessmodelanalyst.com/walmart-pricing-patent-app-price-gap/)

Starting from the reported Walmart case where two phones in one household showed a $110 difference on the same hard drive (Walmart called it an error), the author argues that app prices are private while shelf prices are public. That makes price consistency a credence attribute that shoppers can't check. Denials and promises to let a patent lapse cost the firm almost nothing. The piece suggests verifiable app prices or price-match payouts as costly signals. For fairness researchers, it shifts the question from whether personalization happens to whether consumers can ever tell, and it fairly notes other explanations such as store selection and marketplace sellers.

**Type:** Opinion · **Tags:** Price discrimination, Fairness, Retailer Pricing Strategy, Platform pricing

## 5. Seattle's surveillance-pricing ban leaves loyalty discounts in a grey zone

**Source:** KIRO 7 (Oct 29); context: Sidley state-law roundup (Oct 26) · [kiro7.com](https://kiro7.com/news/local/kiro-7-investigates-seattle-bans-surveillance-pricing-will-shoppers-lose-discounts/XAJH2NM7EZCW5N436Q2QIFBA6U) · [sidley.com](https://www.sidley.com/en/insights/publications/2026/10/from-disclosures-to-prohibitions-the-new-legal-landscape-on-the-use-of-consumer-data)

Seattle's ordinance (effective Sept 2027) allows purchase-history discounts only if they are offered to groups rather than individuals. How large a group has to be is left to rulemaking around March 2027, and grocers warn they may pull discounts. Sidley lists the wider patchwork: bans in Maryland, New Jersey and Connecticut, and a Connecticut disclosure option that requires a label saying the price rose because of personal data. The law is drawing a line between personalized discounts and personalized surcharges, which matches the loss/gain asymmetry in fairness perception. It also leaves open how small a "segment" can get before it counts as one-to-one pricing.

## 6. German shrinkflation list: store brands shrink too, and a bigger pack can hide a price rise

**Source:** MarkenDetektive, on Verbraucherzentrale Hamburg's October list (Nov 4, German) · [markendetektive.de](https://www.markendetektive.de/2026/11/04/mogelpackung-oktober-2026-rewe-lorenz-lenor/)

October's flagged items include Netto's own-brand salad seasoning, cut from 10 g to 8 g at the same price (a hidden 25% increase), and Ültje trail mix, cut from 200 g to 150 g (33%). Lenor fabric softener went the other way: the bottle grew about 20%, but the price rose more, so the unit price went up about 11%. Across all 470 entries on the list, about one in nine is a private label, with hidden increases similar to brands. Two points are useful here. Store brands, often assumed to be the honest value anchor, use the same tactic. And the bigger-pack case shows that a package change can hide a price rise in either direction.

## 7. India will make airlines show a full fare breakdown and file cost data with the regulator

**Source:** The Week (India) (Nov 2) · [theweek.in](https://www.theweek.in/news/biz-tech/2026/11/02/flight-tickets-to-get-cheaper-governments-new-aviation-rules-bring-ticket-fares-under-focus.html)

Under the new Bharatiya Vayuyan Niyam rules (in force January 2027), airlines must show a breakdown of the total fare into service-provider fees, operating costs and taxes. They must also disclose cancellation terms and periodically submit cost data to the DGCA, which can order short-term fare changes in emergencies. Most drip-pricing rules require only all-in prices. Requiring a cost breakdown is a different approach, and it raises a testable question: does showing costs build trust through perceived cost-plus fairness (dual entitlement), or does it give buyers grounds to dispute each item?

**Type:** Regulation · **Tags:** Price transparency, Disclosure, Partitioned Pricing, Fairness, Regulation, Subscription Renewals

## 8. SaaS pricing tracker: vaguer free-tier limits and visible new-customer discounts

**Source:** PricingSaaS Pulse, Week 44 (Oct 19–25) · [pricingsaas.com](https://pricingsaas.com/)

The weekly tracker logged Wispr Flow replacing a specific free-plan cap ("1,000 words") with "limited use." It also logged Intercom raising its Essential plan to $29 per seat while showing a $19 "new customers save 35%" banner, which looks like a live test. Credit-based pricing reached 22% of tracked changes. Vague limits and openly lower prices for new customers are two practical tactics that research on ambiguity aversion and existing-customer fairness has not studied much in B2B and prosumer software.
