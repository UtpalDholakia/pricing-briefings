## 1. FTC goes straight to the price-setters: 6(b) orders on personalized pricing AND personalized pay — FTC press release (Oct 28)

**Source:** [ftc.gov](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/10/ftc-launches-personalized-pricing-compensation-inquiry)

The FTC sent compulsory study orders to Delta, United, JetBlue, Uber, Lyft, DoorDash, Instacart, Target and Walmart. The orders ask for the inputs, tools and procedures behind individualized prices (including loyalty-based ones) and, new here, behind individualized gig-worker pay offers that may draw on acceptance history or phone usage. The agency notes that its 2024 study only reached intermediary consultancies, so this is the first look inside actual pricing decisions. Two angles for researchers: the worker-side counterpart of surveillance pricing barely exists as a literature, and grocery groups (FMI, NGA) separately told the FTC that loyalty coupons, which lower prices for some shoppers, should be treated differently from data-driven price increases. That is a gain/loss framing distinction now being argued in regulatory filings (Food World News summary: [foodworldnews.com](https://www.foodworldnews.com/articles/81662/20261014/grocery-industry-asks-ftc-treat-loyalty-coupons-differently-personalized-pricing-what-store-app.htm)).

## 2. The US surveillance-pricing patchwork, now including a mandated "your data raised this price" label — Sidley Data Matters (Oct 27)

**Source:** [datamatters.sidley.com](https://datamatters.sidley.com/2026/10/27/from-disclosures-to-prohibitions-the-new-legal-landscape-on-the-use-of-consumer-data-for-personalized-pricing/)

A careful map of what is enacted versus pending. Maryland's food-pricing ban took effect Oct 1. New Jersey's law (effective Aug 2027) is the first with a private right of action. Seattle signed an ordinance on Oct 5 barring algorithmic price discrimination. Connecticut lets non-retail sellers keep personalizing prices only if they display a disclosure that the price was increased using the buyer's personal data. That Connecticut rule is a ready-made field setting for testing whether loss-framed, data-attributed price disclosure triggers fairness backlash, reactance or simple ignoring.

## 3. The EU Digital Fairness Act is due around mid-November, with personalized pricing and in-game currencies on the table — Eurobyte (Oct 25)

**Source:** [eurobytenews.com](https://eurobytenews.com/article/digital-fairness-act-proposal-due/)

Drawing on an Oct 5 European Parliament research briefing, the piece says the Commission's proposal is expected around mid-November. Unfair price marketing is one of six named focus areas, and personalized/dynamic pricing, subscription exit friction, and virtual currencies are all under discussion. For behavioral pricing scholars, the virtual-currency piece matters most: if Europe regulates token-denominated prices, the pain-of-paying and price-opacity effects of in-game currencies move from academic curiosity to compliance question. The briefing describes intended scope, not final legal text.

## 4. "Everyone lost": analysts declare the fast-food value war over — Restaurant Dive (Oct 22)

**Source:** [restaurantdive.com](https://www.restaurantdive.com/news/price-value-wars-over-fast-food-chains-shift-food-quality/833658/)

Analysts and executives say the deep-discount QSR value war that started in 2024 delivered weaker margins without the traffic gains. McDonald's ended national ads for its $3-and-under platform after about a third of franchisees did not run it as planned, and Wendy's CEO called its own bundles value-diluting. Chains are now moving to quality cues, while Cava benefits from both trade-up and trade-down. It is a live case for reference-price erosion (training customers to wait for deals) and for whether value perception can be rebuilt through quality instead of price.

## 5. The price of escaping basic economy keeps rising — Thrifty Traveler (Oct 19)

**Source:** [thriftytraveler.com](https://thriftytraveler.com/news/airlines/airlines-basic-economy-upcharges-2026/)

The premium to move from basic economy to a standard fare has grown from roughly $60-70 to $100+ on domestic round trips and from about $150 to about $240 transatlantic, with outliers like $360 on LAX-Haneda. The piece links the widening gap to change-fee replacement, the lighter tax treatment of ancillaries versus base fares, and fuel pass-through. For scholars, basic economy works as both a decoy and a fence. The open question is whether larger fences eventually turn the anchor fare into a perceived bait-and-switch, damaging fairness perceptions of the whole fare ladder.

**Type:** Practice · **Tags:** Fairness, Tiered Pricing, Good-Better-Best Pricing, Behavioral Economics, Premiumization

## 6. Halloween candy up ~15%, quietly re-engineered with less chocolate and more price points — Food Dive (Oct 22, updated Oct 26)

**Source:** [fooddive.com](https://www.fooddive.com/news/halloween-candy-inflation-2026-hershey-mondelez/833754/)

Citing a Groundwork Collaborative/Century Foundation analysis, the article puts Halloween candy price growth at about four times overall inflation, with some SKUs up 23-39%. Manufacturers are responding with price-pack architecture (more pack sizes at more price points), reformulation toward fillings and wafers, and, for Hershey, adding salty snacks to its Halloween lineup. Hershey's CEO has signaled the company is not leading with discounts or BOGOs. It is a clear example of firms using pack size, formulation and assortment to manage price perceptions instead of the shelf price, at an occasion where a social norm (you must hand something out) makes demand unusually inelastic.

## 7. Shrinkflation's newest trick: a lower shelf price on a smaller pack — Mouseprint (Oct 26) + Verbraucherzentrale Hamburg via t-online (Oct 6)

**Source:** [mouseprint.org](https://www.mouseprint.org/2026/10/26/here-we-shrink-again-fall-2026-part-2/) · [t-online.de](https://www.t-online.de/leben/aktuelles/id_101468062/mogelpackung-des-monats-nestle-versteckt-preiserhoehung-hinter-design.html)

Edgar Dworsky's fall list documents familiar cuts (Cheer 92 to 81 oz, Old Spice 3.3 to 3 oz, Fudgicles 20 to 18, Lucky Charms still labeled "family size"). The German case is more interesting: Nestle cut KitKat/Lion/Smarties mini multipacks from 13 to 10 pieces while also cutting the suggested price from EUR 4.79 to EUR 3.79, behind a "new format" flag, so the per-piece price still rose (about 3% per the consumer advocates). A visible price cut that hides a unit-price increase reverses the usual shrinkflation setup and directly tests how much shoppers rely on shelf price versus unit price.

## 8. AI shopping agents have wildly different price sensitivities, and weak ones can be steered by listing order — arXiv (PriceBench, Kireyev; Sept 25)

**Source:** [arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.31468)

In identical hotel-booking tasks, price sensitivity varied by more than an order of magnitude across LLMs (even within one model family), moving the average booked nightly rate from $247 to $393. More capable models held more consistent preferences, while weaker ones fixated on list position or chose almost at random. This is a month old but under-discussed: if agents increasingly do the buying, the "consumer" whose price psychology matters may be a model with its own idiosyncratic willingness-to-pay, and classic levers like position, anchors and framing may transfer, vanish, or be exploitable in new ways.

**Type:** Research · **Tags:** Academic research, AI in Pricing, Hospitality, Price presentation, Consumer decision making, Behavioral Economics

## 9. Bigger default tips raise tips but cost repeat visits, and showing amounts next to percentages doubled tipping — Int'l Journal of Hospitality Management (Warren & Hanson; Li & Warren; Aug 2026 issue)

**Source:** [sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0278431926001532) · [sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0278431926001271)

These two papers are older (online in April) but fit well with current tipping-fatigue coverage. In three experiments, higher default tip sets raised tip amounts but lowered word-of-mouth and repatronage, mediated by lower perceived control and concentrated among customers unsure of the tipping norm. In a Northern European natural experiment (5,169 transactions), adding currency amounts beside percentages on the POS screen raised the share of customers who tipped from about 18% to 40%, and some of the gain persisted after the change was reversed. Together they suggest prompt design shapes both how much people tip and how they feel about the business afterward.

**Type:** Research · **Tags:** Academic research, Behavioral Economics, Incentives
