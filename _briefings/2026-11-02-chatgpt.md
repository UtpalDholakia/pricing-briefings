## Key takeaways

- The case for a bundle can weaken when the seller can fill demand for scarce components separately. This is a model result.
- A small action can make a discount feel earned. The effect disappeared when redemption became burdensome.
- A survey about why customers accepted a price increase cannot tell us whether a different message would have changed their decision.

## What happened

### 1. Scarcity can remove the reason to bundle

**Sources:** [Kellogg Insight](https://insight.kellogg.northwestern.edu/article/when-bundling-hurts-the-bottom-line), November 1, 2026. [Underlying paper](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mnsc.2025.03120), August 10, 2026.

Bundling helps sellers by combining customers’ uneven valuations across products. But that advantage can fade when inventory is scarce relative to demand. Abdallah and Reed’s mathematical model finds that optimal mixed bundling converges toward separate component pricing as this scarcity intensifies.

**Tags:** Price Bundling, High demand

### 2. A promo code can make the same discount more effective

**Sources:** [University of Washington research feature](https://foster.uw.edu/news/research-nidhi-agrawal-token-effort-effect/), July 20, 2026. [Original JMR paper](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/00222437251338050), April 17, 2025.

In a Zoka Coffee field experiment, customers receiving a 15% discount through a promo code converted more often than customers receiving it automatically. Across eight studies, trivial redemption effort increased promotion effectiveness.

**Tags:** Price Promotions, Incentives

### 3. Small portions may need a different price-ending strategy

**Sources:** [Ohio State research notice](https://oric.ehe.osu.edu/2026/10/30/effective-price-framing-strategies-for-promoting-small-plates-2/), October 30, 2026; [journal article](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1447677026001282).

Smaller portions create a fairness problem when the price falls less than the quantity. Research highlighted by Ohio State finds that nonrounded prices increased perceived fairness and purchase intentions for small plates, but not regular plates.

**Tags:** just-below pricing, Fairness, Restaurant pricing

### 4. A seven-cent trial can look more worthwhile than a free one

**Source:** [Korean Management Review](https://kmr.kasba.or.kr/ko/viewer/article-view?id=kmr-2026-55-4-1671), August 2026.

A Korean experiment compared a free English-class trial with one costing 100 won, approximately seven cents. The nominal fee produced higher perceived value and participation intentions, particularly when attendance required a 25-minute walk.

**Tags:** Informational Value, Consumer decision making

### 5. Read the “58% rule” as survey evidence, not a messaging experiment

**Source:** [Chargebee, “The 58% Rule”](https://www.chargebee.com/blog/how-to-raise-prices-without-losing-subscribers/), updated June 26, 2026.

Chargebee argues that poor communication, rather than price increases, drives subscription churn. Its cited survey covered 1,454 consumers. But the detailed response says 58% accepted increases because they still valued the subscription. That does not establish that clearer messaging caused acceptance.

**Tags:** Price Communications, price increases, subscription pricing

## Why it matters

**1. Scarcity can remove the reason to bundle** The mechanism matters: sellers can serve customers who value each component most, without packaging it with something they value less. For practitioners, this challenges habitual package discounts during peak demand. It is a model-based result under specified conditions, not field evidence that unbundling always raises revenue.

**2. A promo code can make the same discount more effective** The proposed mechanism is perceived return on effort: a small action makes the saving feel earned. This is not an argument for difficult checkout. The advantage disappeared when redemption became burdensome. For pricing teams, the useful test is whether one easy action improves response to an unchanged offer, without creating abandonment elsewhere.

**3. Small portions may need a different price-ending strategy** The authors identify a shortcut: nonrounded prices can suggest a discount or carefully calculated price. This makes portion size an important boundary condition for price-ending decisions. The practical implication is to test endings alongside portion architecture. The reported outcomes concern judgments and intentions, not demonstrated restaurant sales or healthier eating.

**4. A seven-cent trial can look more worthwhile than a free one** The proposed mechanism is that a small payment supplies a value cue that helps justify nonmonetary costs. For experiential services, “free” may therefore be the wrong benchmark when participation itself demands effort. Important limits: researchers recruited 200 young adults, analyzed 192 responses, and measured hypothetical intentions rather than actual attendance.

**5. Read the “58% rule” as survey evidence, not a messaging experiment** The behavioral distinction is consequential: continuing to perceive value is different from being persuaded by an explanation. My takeaway is to test price-change communication separately from product improvements and existing customer attachment. Treat the statistic as descriptive evidence, not a transferable retention rule.

## Ideas to apply

- For a peak-demand window, compare a bundle with selling its components separately. Include the opportunity cost of the item likely to sell out first.
- Compare a single easy promo-code step with an automatic discount of the same value. Measure completion as well as abandonment.
- Test price endings by portion size. Do not infer actual restaurant sales from stated purchase intentions.
