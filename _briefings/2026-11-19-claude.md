## 1. Disney+ UK raises Standard to £11.99 for existing subscribers, while newcomers get six months at £9.99

**Source:** Cord Busters (Nov 18): [cordbusters.co.uk](https://cordbusters.co.uk/disney-plus-uk-existing-subscribers-price-hike); Selectra: [selectra.co.uk](https://selectra.co.uk/tv-broadband/news/disney-plus-uk-price-rise-18-november-channel-4-early-2027)

UK trade sites report that Disney+ Standard went from £9.99 to £11.99 a month on Nov 18 (a 20% rise). Standard with Ads went from £5.99 to £6.99 and Premium from £14.99 to £15.99. Monthly subscribers pay the new prices from their next bill, and annual subscribers from Dec 2. On the same day, a six-month "saver" offer gives new and eligible returning customers £5.99, £9.99 and £11.99, which are the old prices. So a current subscriber's reference price is now being charged to strangers, and the only way to get it back is to cancel and rejoin later. That is the loyalty penalty in its most visible form. In dual-entitlement terms, the rise breaks the subscriber's entitlement twice: once through the rise itself and once by showing someone else getting the old price. I could not find Disney's own UK announcement, so these prices come from the trade press.

## 2. One in three US electronics got more expensive before Black Friday, up from one in seven last year

**Source:** Decodo (Nov 2026): [decodo.com](https://decodo.com/blog/black-friday-2026-price-hikes)

Web-data vendor Decodo collected more than a million price points from 37 US retailers, six times a day. It compared early-September prices with late-October prices. About one product in eight rose in price over that window. In electronics the share was one in three, against one in seven in 2025, and in fashion it was under one in twenty. For last year, it reports that six in ten marked-up products were no cheaper on Black Friday than in September. Raising a price weeks ahead lets the later "deal" be measured from a reference point the seller built. This is the US version of what Portugal's DECO found yesterday, now measured as a share of products rather than as single cases. Two limits apply. The data come from a vendor, and they don't separate tariff- or cost-driven rises from rises made to build up a reference price.

## 3. A private-equity landlord settles with the FTC for $49.75 million over "smart home" fees left out of advertised rent

**Source:** FTC consumer alert (Nov 2026): [consumer.ftc.gov](https://consumer.ftc.gov/consumer-alerts/2026/11/ftc-says-progress-residential-charged-renters-hidden-fees); Colorado AG: [coag.gov](https://coag.gov/press-releases/attorney-general-weiser-announces-48-million-multistate-settlement-with-progress-residential-for-false-advertising-charging-hidden-fees/)

The FTC and four states (Arizona, Colorado, Indiana and Utah) say Progress Residential advertised monthly rents that left out mandatory fees, among them a "smart home" fee and a "utility maintenance" fee. Under the proposed settlement, filed Nov 6, Progress and its owner Pretium Partners pay $49.75 million, of which $48.5 million goes to renter redress. They must also advertise the total monthly price. The firms admit no wrongdoing, and a court must still approve the deal. Rent is a recurring price that people budget for monthly, so a fee left out of the headline rent changes the number tenants plan around, not just a one-off checkout total. The fee names matter too. A "smart home" fee sounds like an optional upgrade, which makes a mandatory charge read as a benefit. The order uses the same all-in remedy as the hotel and ticketing rules, now applied to housing.

## 4. A field experiment with 853,521 customers: letting buyers keep the item for a partial refund paid off, and invited opportunism

**Source:** Marketing Science, Articles in Advance (Sept 2026; Fang, Wu, Yuan & Zhang): [pubsonline.informs.org](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mksc.2025.0618)

_Older than this week, included because it has not been covered here._ A cross-border e-commerce platform randomly offered some customers who asked to return an item a partial refund to keep it instead. A multi-agent AI system priced each offer from the customer's photos and claim. The policy raised net transaction value by $1.44 per customer over 30 days and $4.41 over 90 days. Refund payouts went up, but return costs fell and later spending rose. Later refund requests also rose, in patterns that fit opportunistic claims. The authors put the revenue-maximising average refund at about 41% of the price. A returnless refund works as a price cut made after the sale and fitted to each buyer, so the platform has effectively built personalized after-sale pricing into its returns process. It also teaches customers that a complaint can lower the price. I read only the abstract.

## 5. Vimeo moves legacy subscribers onto new plans at renewal, and it decides who counts as a business

**Source:** Livid (updated Nov 5): [livid.com](https://livid.com/blog/vimeo-price-increase-2026-a-complete-breakdown-of-the-new-plans-and-forced-upgrades/); PetaPixel: [petapixel.com](https://petapixel.com/2026/09/04/vimeo-can-decide-to-change-your-plan-and-force-you-to-pay-5x-more/)

Vimeo is retiring its old self-serve plans (Starter, Standard, Advanced, Plus, Pro, Business). Legacy subscribers move to the new Creator, Core and Professional plans at their next renewal, with an email about 30 days before. Vimeo itself can reclassify an account as "Studio," the business plan, which reportedly costs $400 a month billed annually. Third-party breakdowns disagree on the new individual prices. The 34–180% increases quoted online come from renewal emails users have posted, not from Vimeo. This differs from an ordinary price rise in two ways. The buyer never chooses the new plan, so there is no choice to anchor on. And the seller decides which price category the buyer belongs to. That makes the price feel imposed rather than chosen. It also invites customers to compare against competitors right at renewal, the moment when switching is easiest.

## 6. A Texas ticket bill would freeze the price once a seat is selected, but not before

**Source:** TicketNews (Nov 2026): [ticketnews.com](https://www.ticketnews.com/2026/11/texas-ticketing-bill-dynamic-pricing-fees/); Dallas Express: [dallasexpress.com](https://dallasexpress.com/state/texas-bill-targets-surprise-ticket-fees-but-leaves-high-prices-untouched/)

A bill proposed for Texas's 2027 session would require ticket sellers to show the total price, including mandatory fees, in the listing. It would also bar them from raising the total after a buyer selects a ticket. The one exception is a previously disclosed charge for non-electronic delivery that the buyer chooses. TicketNews notes the gap: the freeze starts only at selection. Demand-based prices can still differ widely between buyers who never see each other's checkout. The bill fixes the "price creep" that feels like a betrayal mid-transaction. It leaves alone the dynamic pricing that makes two neighbours pay different amounts for similar seats, which is what drove the Australian "Shonky" awards earlier this month. The bill number was not confirmed in the sources I read.
