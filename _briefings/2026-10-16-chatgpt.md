## Key takeaways

- The typical increase among Australian cafés that raised their price was much larger than the average change across the whole sample.
- A fee can alter what an offer communicates even when the amount is tiny.
- The value of a pricing mechanism can change after customers learn how to use it.

## What happened

### 1. A tiny fee disappears. A much larger price increase arrives

**Source:** [Small Flat White’s surcharge tracker](https://smallflatwhite.com/surcharge-tracker) · Updated throughout October

This wonderfully narrow source calls Australian cafes to track coffee prices. Among 1,508 cafes checked before and after October’s card-surcharge ban, 17% raised their small flat white price. Among those raising prices, the median increase was 50 cents, or 9.8%. Across the entire sample, the average increase was 1.5%. The sample is unweighted and still developing.

**Tags:** Changing price, Regulation

### 2. One cent can change the meaning of hospitality

**Sources:** [“When size doesn’t matter”](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbusres.2026.116046) · *Journal of Business Research*, April 2026 · [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0148296326000809?dgcid=rss_sd_all)

Across five restaurant studies, charging for something normally complimentary produced negative reactions even when the surcharge was just one cent. The researchers connect this response to a violation of communal norms: customers read the charge as inconsistent with goodwill. An advertising field study also found lower click-through rates when a surcharge appeared.

**Tags:** Price presentation, Social processes, Restaurant pricing

### 3. More precise pricing can make a seller worse off

**Sources:** [“Consumer-Driven Class Pricing”](https://doi.org/10.1287/mksc.2023.0133) · *Marketing Science*, published online March 12, 2026 · [Marketing Science](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mksc.2023.0133)

Why do businesses assign a handful of prices to many different products? Zuhui Xiao develops a model in which reference dependence and loss aversion can make grouping products into price classes profitable. Differences in product costs encourage different prices. Consumers’ asymmetric reactions to those differences can undermine the gains. The paper opens with eight draft beers sold at only three prices.

**Tags:** pricing structures, Pricing models

### 4. Charging customers for the chance to save works until they learn

**Source:** [“Profitability of two-part tariffs in name-your-own-price markets”](https://epub.ub.uni-muenchen.de/131751/) · *Journal of Retailing*, September 2026 issue

Two laboratory experiments and a field experiment examine a peculiar offer: pay a nonrefundable fee to submit your own price. Modest bidding fees can increase seller profits. But experience with the mechanism, or a calculation aid, reduces participation enough that charging the fee may cease to pay. The authors see potential for short promotions and membership benefits.

**Tags:** Partitioned Pricing, Assessing profitability

### 5. Smaller portions may need a different price ending

**Sources:** [“Effective price framing strategies for promoting small plates”](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jhtm.2026.101520) · *Journal of Hospitality and Tourism Management*, September 2026 · [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1447677026001282)

Smaller portions invite a proportionality judgment: has the price fallen enough relative to the quantity? This research finds that nonrounded prices improve perceived fairness and purchase intentions for small plates, with no equivalent benefit for regular plates. The authors interpret nonrounded prices as cues of discounted or fair pricing.

**Tags:** just-below pricing, Fairness

## Why it matters

**1. A tiny fee disappears. A much larger price increase arrives** Owners describe accumulating cost pressures and then raising prices in one step. A useful hypothesis for price communication: an external event can give sellers an occasion to act on increases they have postponed. The tracker does not establish how customers interpret that explanation.

**2. One cent can change the meaning of hospitality** The revenue from a small charge can be negligible while its relational meaning is substantial. This gives a sharper explanation for some fee backlash than simple price sensitivity. Revisit intentions, however, should not be treated as observed repeat purchases.

**3. More precise pricing can make a seller worse off** This supplies a consumer-side argument for restraint as AI makes granular pricing easier. It is a theoretical result under specified assumptions, offering a hypothesis to test rather than a universal prescription for fewer prices.

**4. Charging customers for the chance to save works until they learn** The durability of a pricing mechanism deserves its own analysis. A profitable first encounter may depend on how customers initially understand the offer. Learning can change the economics.

**5. Smaller portions may need a different price ending** The interesting finding is the boundary condition. Price endings gain meaning from the quantity comparison customers are making. For downsizing and smaller-pack strategies, that comparison may deserve as much attention as the final price itself.

## Ideas to apply

- After removing a surcharge, compare the previous checkout total with the new all-in price, not just the new and old posted prices.
- Before charging for something customers expect to be complimentary, test whether the charge changes what they infer about the business.
- Evaluate a bid fee or a portion-specific price ending with repeat customers as well as first-time buyers.
