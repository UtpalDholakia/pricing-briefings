## Key takeaways

- Do not value a future-use credit as an unconditional reduction in today's outlay.
- Separate access priority from the price customers can actually buy at today.
- Diagnose the barrier to purchase before choosing a retargeting incentive.

## What happened

### 1. Apple's gift card is a separately charged promotion product
**Source:** [Apple US Black Friday terms, November 27–30, 2026 promotion, checked November 27](https://www.apple.com/shop/gifts/black-friday-event/terms-conditions).

Apple offers gift cards of up to $300 with eligible purchases. The accounting is more interesting than the headline. The product receives a discount equal to the card's value, but the customer is charged for the card. The card is usable only for future purchases. For a full refund, both must be returned; a missing or redeemed card reduces the product refund by its original value. My interpretation: the offer creates a separate mental account for future Apple spending. Compare its value with a cash discount using expected redemption and additional spending, not face value alone.

**Tags:** "Price Promotions", "Price presentation", "Customer loyalty".

### 2. Shakhtar sells premium access first, then opens lower starting prices
**Source:** [FC Shakhtar ticket announcement, November 26, 2026](https://shakhtar.com/en/news/2026/november/26_news/26_shakhtar_real_tickets/).

For January's Real Madrid match at Stamford Bridge, November 26–December 3 sales are VIP-only and open to anyone. Prior London-match buyers and Chelsea Members get access December 4–7. General sale starts December 8, subject to availability. Published starting prices are £85 standard and £350 VIP. Prices may change as seats in a category become scarcer. This combines access segmentation with inventory-linked pricing. It does not mean an £85 ticket is available to everyone today. My inference: early certainty becomes part of the premium product. Audit that distinction in advertisements and monitor perceived fairness across purchase windows.

**Tags:** "Sports ticket pricing", "Dynamic pricing", "Price presentation".

### 3. Cash App distinguishes stopping the payment from ending the subscription
**Source:** [Cash App US terms, updated November 25, 2026, section VIII.a.6](https://cash.app/us/en/legal/tos).

The current terms describe a right to stop preauthorized Cash App Card payments. Requests must arrive at least three business days before the transfer. Blocking a merchant stops all future card transactions from that merchant, but does not automatically cancel the underlying subscription or agreement. I verified the current provision, not the date this capability first became available. The pricing implication is a distinction between payment retention and customer retention. A stopped charge is not necessarily a completed cancellation. Track these states separately and explain their consequences clearly rather than treating continued billing as evidence of continued preference.

**Tags:** "subscription pricing", "Subscription Renewals", "Price transparency".

### 4. A functional claim does not guarantee a functional-drink premium
**Source:** [Bigeye, WORTH IT., November 12, 2026; marketplace capture November 11](https://www.bigeyeagency.com/research/worth-it-food-beverage-2026).

Earlier discovery. Bigeye recorded logged-out marketplace listings. Nine of Amazon's top fifty soft-drink listings were prebiotic or fiber sodas. Their median listed price was $0.18 per fluid ounce, versus $0.05 for the other forty-one. Yet Pepsi Prebiotic Cola appeared at $0.07. These are observed listings, not verified receipts, sales weights or a causal estimate of what the claim earns. My interpretation: the cheapest credible product carrying the same benefit may reset the comparison customers make. Test the premium against that substitute, not only against the average specialty brand.

**Tags:** "Premiumization", "Consumer decision making", "Price presentation".

### 5. Removing a barrier is different from reminding someone to buy
**Source:** [Luo, Wang, Wu and Moriguchi, MSOM, first online May 21, 2025](https://doi.org/10.1287/msom.2024.0779).

Older discovery, read from the publisher's abstract. In a field experiment, simple product reminders did not significantly increase purchases. Late-stage free-shipping and free-return retargeting increased purchases and targeting profitability. Reported heterogeneity differed: free returns worked especially well for younger, less experienced customers and higher-value abandoned products; free shipping for older, longer-tenure customers and lower-priced products. The abstract does not supply sample sizes or full comparator details. My interpretation: risk and immediate cost are different barriers. Test which one explains abandonment before applying the same incentive to everyone.

**Tags:** "Academic research", "Price Promotions", "Consumer decision making".

## Why it matters

My synthesis: a headline price can hide a different commitment, a different purchase date or a different source of uncertainty. Pricing analysis should make each explicit before evaluating the offer.

## Ideas to apply

- Compare gift cards with equal-value cash discounts on total contribution and redemption.
- Display both the sales window and current eligibility beside starting ticket prices.
- Test shipping and return offers separately, with a no-offer holdout and profit measurement.
