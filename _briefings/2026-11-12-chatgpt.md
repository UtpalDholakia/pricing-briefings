## Key takeaways

- A fare floor can make the advertised maximum discount least attainable on lower-priced tickets. See item 1.
- A one-time charge is easy to describe. An FAQ that still talks about renewals makes it harder to trust. See item 2.
- Who recommends a discount and who sets a price are two different questions for shoppers. See item 4.

## What happened

### 1. Brightline’s cheapest eligible fares get less than 20% off

**Source:** [Brightline email via Milled · Nov 10, 2026](https://milled.com/brightline/cheers-to-the-season-save-up-to-20-1C-3Ajyf6WV9dfa7)

Brightline’s holiday offer advertised up to 20% off eligible Orlando fares, with minimum prices of $39 for SMART SAVER and $59 for SMART SELECT. A hypothetical $45 SMART SAVER fare would fall to $39 rather than $36, a saving of about 13%. The fare floor can make the headline discount unavailable on cheaper tickets.

**Tags:** Price Promotions, Price legibility

### 2. Notch Metrics drops the subscription, but its FAQ still talks about subscribing

**Sources:** [Happy Valley · Nov 11, 2026](https://happyvalley.dev/price-changes) · [Notch Metrics pricing](https://notchmetrics.com/#pricing)

The Mac app’s founder originally advertised $9 a month or $49 a year. The pricing page now offers a seven-day trial without a card, followed by a single $5 payment. Yet the FAQ still describes subscribing, cancellation, and failed payments. The public pages do not establish what happens to existing subscribers.

**Tags:** SaaS pricing, subscription pricing, Price transparency

### 3. SharkNinja publishes the gap between new and returning customers

**Source:** [SharkNinja Australia · offer dated Nov 12, 2026](https://sharkninja.com.au/blogs/sales-offers/black-friday-2026-17-off-a-first-order-12-off-for-returning-customers)

For its one-day Australian promotion, SharkNinja offered newsletter subscribers 17% off a first order and 12% off a returning customer’s order. Both codes appeared on the same page. Neither required a minimum spend, and both excluded bundles and gift cards. The terms describe the offer, not how customers responded.

**Tags:** Price discrimination, Fairness

### 4. A discount recommendation worked better with an AI label

**Source:** [Chen et al., Information & Management · Nov 1, 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.im.2026.104464)

In a WeChat field experiment, shoppers bought more often when a discount-focused recommendation was labeled as coming from AI rather than from a person. The result reversed when the message emphasized personal fit. A follow-up experiment suggested that shoppers credited AI with analytical skill and people with a better understanding of individual tastes.

**Tags:** Academic research, AI in Pricing

### 5. A product passport was more persuasive when shoppers knew why to open it

**Source:** [Dorner et al., Environmental and Resource Economics · Aug 19, 2026](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10640-026-01107-z)

In a lab experiment with real purchase stakes, participants valued a toothbrush after seeing either no extra information, a link to its environmental details, or that link plus a short explainer. Final willingness to pay was not significantly different in the link-only and control groups. The explainer group spent more time with the passport and had higher final willingness to pay than either group.

**Tags:** Informational Value, Consumer decision making

## Why it matters

**1. Brightline’s cheapest eligible fares get less than 20% off** A percentage headline gives shoppers a number to expect before they see their actual fare. If you use a floor, check how many customers receive the headline saving and how many see a smaller discount at checkout.

**2. Notch Metrics drops the subscription, but its FAQ still talks about subscribing** The offer removes both a recurring charge and a choice between billing periods. Contradictory language can bring uncertainty back at checkout. Test whether a reader of the FAQ can correctly explain what they will owe.

**3. SharkNinja publishes the gap between new and returning customers** A returning buyer can evaluate 12% as a saving or as five points less than a newcomer gets. Publishing both offers makes that comparison easy. Test a distinct returning-customer benefit against a smaller version of the same discount.

**4. A discount recommendation worked better with an AI label** Who recommends a deal is a different question from who sets the price. Shoppers may welcome an algorithm finding a bargain without wanting one to decide what they should pay. Test those roles separately and label them accurately.

**5. A product passport was more persuasive when shoppers knew why to open it** Making information available is different from giving shoppers a reason to look. Before pricing in a sustainability premium, check whether buyers actually open and understand the evidence.

## Ideas to apply

- Calculate the effective discount for each eligible fare. Compare what shoppers see in the headline with what they actually pay.
- Ask a first-time visitor to explain a new one-time offer after reading the FAQ. Check whether they expect another charge.
- When testing a product passport, compare a link alone with a short explanation of why the information is worth opening.
