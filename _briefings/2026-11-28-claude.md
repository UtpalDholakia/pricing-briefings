## 1. AI pricing tools are talking brands out of deeper Black Friday discounts

**Source:** Reuters, via BNN Bloomberg (Nov 23, 2026): [bnnbloomberg.ca](https://www.bnnbloomberg.ca/business/artificial-intelligence/2026/11/23/ai-tools-help-brands-curb-black-friday-deals-from-clothes-to-skincare/)

Reuters interviewed five vendors of AI pricing tools. Kapnova's CEO said its software led an unnamed billion-dollar beauty brand to cut a planned 40% skincare discount to 30%, and the vendors said their models often find that discounts can shrink without losing demand. Centric Market Intelligence's data, published by LSEG, point the same way: average Black Friday-week discounts fell to 15% from 18% at department stores and to 23% from 31% at specialty retailers. The behavioural question is what the models are learning. If demand is flat between 30% and 40% off, the deal is probably working through the badge and the "was" price (transaction utility) rather than the extra ten points, so retailers now have software that finds the shallowest discount that still triggers the deal response. The caveats matter: the 40-to-30 example is a vendor's own claim about an unnamed client, and an earlier LSEG note put last year's specialty average at 24%, not 31%, so the year-on-year drop may be smaller than it looks.

## 2. Tip screens that show dollar amounts beside percentages doubled the share of customers who tip, and most of the gain stayed

**Source:** Li and Warren, *International Journal of Hospitality Management* (2026): [sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0278431926001271)

A counter-service restaurant in a Nordic country changed its payment-screen tip options twice over nine months: percentages only, then percentages with dollar amounts, then percentages only again. Across 5,169 card transactions, showing the amounts raised both the chance of tipping and the average tip. A secondary summary gives the share tipping as 17.6%, then 39.6%, then 32.2% after the amounts were removed. Showing the amount turns a ratio the customer has to work out into a concrete, easy choice, a processing-fluency effect that is separate from the size of the defaults. What stands out is that most of the effect remained after the amounts were taken away, which suggests the screen established a norm in a market that had none. It is one venue with no control site, so the persistence could also reflect a trend or a change in who was buying.

## 3. A field experiment on a Chinese platform finds that the more data it holds on a shopper, the more price discrimination that shopper faces

**Source:** Quan, Zheng, Wang and Hu, *Production and Operations Management* (2026): [journals.sagepub.com](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/10591478251408177)

No platform admits to data-driven price discrimination, so it has been hard to measure. These authors ran a field experiment with a leading Chinese e-retailer and found that customers on whom it held more data faced more price discrimination. They pair this with a natural experiment around China's 2020 rule banning the practice, which lets them ask whether the platform complied. Most of the fairness research the trackers hold (for example, on "your price is personal" labels) measures how people judge personalised prices. This study measures how much personalisation happens and who bears it: it is heaviest for the platform's most engaged users, the customers who have given it the most data. I read only the abstract, so the effect sizes and the compliance result are not yet in the tracker.

## 4. Connecticut answers shrinkflation with consistent unit prices, not downsizing notices

**Source:** Connecticut Attorney General and Department of Consumer Protection, via EIN Presswire (Nov 2026): [einpresswire.com](https://www.einpresswire.com/article/958772862/attorney-general-tong-and-dcp-commissioner-cafferelli-praise-passage-of-updated-regulations-to-combat-shrinkflation-by-equipping-consumers-with)

From January 1, 2027, Connecticut food retailers, both in-store and online, must show unit prices in one unit across similar products (pounds for dry bulk goods; pints, quarts or gallons rather than litres for most liquids). The unit price must be bold, in a contrasting colour, and at least half the size of the selling price. Small family-run stores are exempt. The rule replaces the Attorney General's 2025 bill requiring notice when a pack shrinks, which stalled after food-industry opposition. Connecticut is betting on fixing how shoppers process unit prices rather than on telling them about each size cut. That addresses inattention, one of the four reasons in [Why Shrinkflation Works So Well](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/why-shrinkflation-works-so-well-and), but not indifference or inertia. Mixed units and tiny unit-price labels are well-documented reasons shoppers ignore unit prices, so the size and consistency rules target the right problem.

## 5. OpenAI extends a promotional API price to November 30 without saying what the regular price will be

**Source:** OpenAI developer docs, GPT-5.6 Sol model page (Nov 2026): [developers.openai.com](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/models/gpt-5.6-sol)

GPT-5.6 Sol is listed at a promotional $4 per million input tokens and $20 per million output tokens, down from $5 and $30. The promotion was first said to run at least to November 21 and now ends on November 30. As read, the model page does not give a post-promotion price. One open-source cost tracker had to patch its code because calls after November 21 would otherwise have had no price. A promotion normally gets its pull from the reference price it is measured against. Here the reference price is the old list price, but the price buyers will actually pay after the deadline is unpublished, so the deadline creates urgency without a known cost of missing it. For API buyers who budget by the month, an expiry date with no follow-on price is a forecasting problem more than a deal. The December 1 snapshot will read the page.

## 6. Pricing bots that keep the same rival learn higher prices, even when they cannot see or punish it

**Source:** Arslan, Kim and Xiao, arXiv preprint 2609.35402 (Sep 28, 2026): [arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.35402)

In a pre-registered experiment with Q-learning agents in a Bertrand duopoly, agents paired with one permanent rival learned higher prices than agents rotating among about three rivals, in all twenty paired runs. The effect held when the rival's price was hidden and messaging was off, so it is not only learned retaliation, the usual explanation for algorithmic collusion. Untrained LLM agents showed it only in some prompt setups. For consumers, the point is that a stable competitive pairing (the same two apps, the same two airlines on a route) can push prices up without any communication or punishment strategy. That makes "tacit collusion" much harder to prove than the current debate assumes. This is an unreviewed simulation, and the three-rival comparison is labelled exploratory.
