## 1. Three in ten Norwegian Black Week products still cost more than in September

**Source:** Godpris press release via NTB (Nov 2026): [kommunikasjon.ntb.no](https://kommunikasjon.ntb.no/pressemelding/19139360/black-week-i-gang-en-av-fire-varer-er-billigere-men-tre-av-ti-er-fortsatt-dyrere-enn-i-september?publisherId=17848964); Prisjakt data via Tek.no: [tek.no](https://www.tek.no/i/mKmPkq)

The US pre-sale price data covered here on Nov 19 measured price rises before Black Friday. Norway now shows what those rises look like once the sale starts. As Black Week opened, the price-comparison service Godpris found that 29% of about 1,000 popular products were still at least 5% dearer than in early September, against about two in ten a year earlier. Only about one in four were at least 5% cheaper, while more than half were marked down during last year's Black Week. Godpris's managing director said many of the "deals" simply return to the September price. Prisjakt's data, reported by Tek.no, shows the same 29% share (18% a year earlier). The mechanism is transaction utility built on a manufactured reference point. A "before" price that was genuinely charged in October can be lawful and still overstate the saving against what the product cost a few weeks earlier. Elkjøp, Power and Komplett told Tek.no that the rises are unrelated to the sale. Power pointed to earlier campaigns ending.

## 2. Ofgem confirms a 26% rise in Britain's energy price cap, and stresses that it does not cap bills

**Source:** Ofgem (Nov 25, 2026): [ofgem.gov.uk](https://www.ofgem.gov.uk/press-release/energy-price-cap-rise-26-january-2027); Octopus Energy: [octopus.energy](https://octopus.energy/blog/january-2027-energy-price-cap/)

New since Meta AI's Nov 22 item on the forecast: the regulator has decided. From January 1 to March 31, 2027, the cap for a typical dual-fuel household paying by Direct Debit will be £2,166 a year, up from £1,723. That is a 26% rise, or about £443 a year by Octopus's count. Ofgem blames wholesale gas, which it says has roughly tripled since the Middle East conflict began, and notes that electricity rises less than gas. Ofgem's announcement also repeats that the cap limits unit rates and standing charges, not total bills. That matters psychologically. The word "cap" invites households to read £2,166 as a ceiling, when it is only an illustration based on typical use. The cost explanation is the kind dual entitlement predicts buyers will accept, but it comes from the regulator, not from the sellers who collect the money.

## 3. Telling buyers that tipping is customary beats appeals to reciprocity, without losing repeat business

**Source:** Kim, Amir & Wilbur, Marketing Science, Articles in Advance (Oct 30, 2026): [pubsonline.informs.org](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mksc.2025.0392)

The authors describe these as the first academic field experiments to vary the wording of a tip request. They covered 436,969 transactions on a global freelance marketplace and compared an injunctive-norm message (tipping is customary) with personalised and impersonal reciprocity appeals. On the web, the norm message raised tipping by 11.8 percentage points for new buyers and 6.8 for repeat buyers. In the app the gains were 2.5 and 1.4 points. Effects grew with the order price. The worry that tip prompts drive customers away did not show up. The norm and personalised-reciprocity messages increased later orders and spending on the web and left app repatronage unchanged. For tipping research, which mostly studies default amounts, this shows that the stated reason for tipping is a separate lever. A norm statement tells buyers what is expected of them, while a reciprocity appeal makes the tip feel like a personal favour. I read only the abstract.

## 4. Pricing a new LLM service when users are still learning what it is worth

**Source:** Du & Lu, arXiv preprint 2611.03199 (Nov 2026): [arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.03199)

AI launch pricing is usually discussed as a competitive move. This preprint treats it as a learning problem on both sides. The provider has no demand history, and users' willingness to pay changes as they use the service. The authors derive matching upper and lower bounds on how much revenue a pricing rule must lose while it learns, and find two regimes that depend on serving cost. In simulations their algorithms beat standard online-learning baselines. Behaviourally, it gives a formal reason why introductory AI prices are temporary. Early users are still forming their reference price, so a low launch price shapes the very willingness to pay the provider is trying to measure. That cuts against the doubling of introductory API rates now scheduled for Dec 1. It is theory and simulation, unreviewed, with no field data.

## Pick of the day
