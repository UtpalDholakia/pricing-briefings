## Key takeaways

- Reducing the number of plans can lower the price at the top and raise the entry price for new customers.
- A stack of discounts can make an offer feel unusual, but complexity can undermine the effect.
- A packaging change can make categories feel cheaper and new products feel more expensive at the same time.

## What happened

### 1. A pricing simplification that raises the entry price

**Source:** [Sharetribe’s public pricing changelog](https://www.sharetribe.com/pricing/pricing-changelog/) · October 2026

Sharetribe is replacing three operating plans with one. Customers on its most expensive plan get a lower base price. But the cheapest operating plan disappears for new customers. Existing customers on that plan can keep it indefinitely. The company is also replacing transaction charges with infrastructure usage credits.

**Tags:** SaaS pricing, Usage-based pricing, Tiered Pricing

### 2. Beehiiv asks customers to change the comparison

**Source:** [Beehiiv’s announcement](https://www.beehiiv.com/blog/beehiiv-pricing-2026) · October 1, 2026

Beehiiv explains its new prices by emphasizing its expansion beyond newsletters into websites, podcasts, community, and digital products. It also stresses that it takes no percentage of subscription revenue. Existing customers transition at renewal, with notice and protection for their current annual term.

**Tags:** Price Communications, value-based pricing

### 3. A complicated bargain can be valuable because it feels personal

**Sources:** [“One of a kind: How stacked discounts make customers feel smart”](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jretai.2025.12.004) · *Journal of Retailing*, June 2026 · [ideas.repec.org](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jouret/v102y2026i2p333-352.html)

Wenting Zhong and Lan Xia find that stacked discounts can outperform a single discount with the same face value. Their studies attribute the appeal to perceived uniqueness and smart-shopper feelings. Computational errors do not explain the effect. Complexity can weaken it, and the advantage is stronger for smaller overall discounts.

**Tags:** Price Promotions, Processing Prices

### 4. A smaller pack can make the category look cheaper and a new product look dearer

**Sources:** [“How Downsizing a Typical Product … Recalibrates Price Perceptions”](https://doi.org/10.1002/mar.70134) · *Psychology & Marketing*, March 31, 2026 · [Wiley Online Library](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/mar.70134)

Three online experiments examine smaller packages accompanied by proportional price reductions, leaving unit prices unchanged. The lower ticket price makes the category assortment seem less expensive. Yet a newly introduced product within that assortment can look more expensive. Unit-price disclosure attenuates several of the effects.

**Tags:** Shrinkflation, Price legibility

### 5. Letting customers choose their promotion can reduce purchasing

**Source:** [“Menu-Induced Preference Cannibalization”](https://gsb.skku.edu/publications/37) · SKK GSB research summary, April 2026

Should customers choose between a guaranteed discount and a chance-based promotion? Across two field experiments and a preregistered online study, offering a promotion menu reduced purchase incidence relative to a single promotion. Among purchasers, however, the menu was associated with greater subsequent consumption. The researchers interpret the findings through preference cannibalization and sunk-cost effects.

**Tags:** Price Promotions, Lottery, Consumer decision making

## Why it matters

**1. A pricing simplification that raises the entry price** Simplification produces different reference points for different customers. My interpretation: grandfathering protects existing customers’ expectations, while usage credits introduce a new challenge. Customers must learn what activity costs before they can judge whether the new arrangement is fair.

**2. Beehiiv asks customers to change the comparison** My reading is that beehiiv wants customers to compare its bill with the combined cost of several tools and revenue-sharing fees. That comparison could justify a higher price. Its effectiveness depends on whether customers value and use the additional capabilities. The announcement provides the argument, not evidence that customers accept it.

**3. A complicated bargain can be valuable because it feels personal** Part of a promotion’s value comes from the customer’s sense of having secured an unusual deal. This suggests testing whether a promotion creates that feeling alongside measuring perceived savings. The research does not establish that adding more discount layers always helps.

**4. A smaller pack can make the category look cheaper and a new product look dearer** One product can influence the perceived affordability of an entire assortment while creating an unfavorable comparison for a launch. The managerial implication is to evaluate package changes across the assortment. These findings come from visual scenarios involving chocolate and soft drinks, rather than observed store purchases.

**5. Letting customers choose their promotion can reduce purchasing** Adding an option can change how customers evaluate the existing offer. For practitioners, the study also raises a measurement issue: purchase conversion and subsequent engagement may move in opposite directions. A promotion should be assessed across both stages.

## Ideas to apply

- Audit a plan consolidation separately for new buyers, grandfathered subscribers, and customers on the former highest tier.
- Test a single discount and a stack with identical savings. Ask how unusual the deal feels and how easy it is to understand.
- Measure how a package-size change alters the reference price for products you have not resized.
