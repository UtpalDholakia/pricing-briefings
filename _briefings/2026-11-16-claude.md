## 1. ChatGPT gives its shopping agents a wallet as JPMorgan blocks agents from its cards

**Source:** TechCrunch (Nov 13): [techcrunch.com](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/13/chatgpts-new-wallet-lets-ai-agents-shop-and-check-out-for-you); PYMNTS, on the WSJ's report (Nov 13): [pymnts.com](https://www.pymnts.com/news/2026/jpmorgan-stops-ai-agents-from-using-customer-credit-cards/)

On the same day, OpenAI launched ChatGPT Wallet and JPMorgan was reported to block agents that shop with a Chase card number. Wallet lets Pro users store a card once. The agent checks out with a payment token, and each purchase is approved with a fingerprint or Face ID after the user reviews the items and total. OpenAI says spending limits and category or time rules are coming, so users can pre-approve purchases. Pricing research has studied checkout friction mainly as pain of paying. These products turn it into a design variable: a biometric tap on every purchase keeps some pain, while a pre-approved category budget turns the agent into a mental account with a cap. The bank's block shows a second lever. Whoever controls the payment credential can set how hard it is to let an agent spend, and merchants say Stripe's virtual-card workaround costs them more to accept.

## 2. Lyft swaps instant ride discounts for 8% back in expiring credits

**Source:** Lyft (Nov 5): [lyft.com](https://www.lyft.com/blog/posts/lyft-announces-lyftgo-membership-doordash); View from the Wing (Nov 5): [viewfromthewing.com](https://viewfromthewing.com/?p=259359)

Lyft Go (USD 9.99 a month, DashPass included) replaces Lyft Pink's percentage discounts with 8% back in Lyft credits, earned on the base fare only and capped at USD 40 a month. The credits expire after 60 days, must be selected by hand, and lapse if the membership ends. DashPass-linked ride discounts ended on Nov 11, and paid Pink members move to Go at their next renewal at their current price. The headline rate went up (8% vs 5%), but the benefit moved from a price cut to a rebate in a captive currency. Mental accounting predicts riders will value that currency below cash. Expiry adds breakage, and credits that can only be spent on the next trip pull riders back to the app. Gary Leff's point that a discount saves money now while a credit only pays off if you ride again is the consumer's version of the argument.

## 3. Magic: The Gathering's Collector Boosters shrink from 15 to 12 cards at the same price

**Source:** MTG Insider (Nov 15): [mtginsider.com](https://mtginsider.com/mtg-nauctis-shrinkflation-commander-bundles-collector-boosters/)

For the February set *Nauctis*, Collector Boosters stay at USD 26.99 but drop from 15 cards to 12. The rare and mythic slots stay at five, so the cut falls on lower-rarity cards, and the price per card rises 25% (USD 1.80 to 2.25). Borderless foil commanders leave the USD 49.99 Commander decks and move to a new USD 79.99 Premium deck. Bundle full-art lands are no longer foil, and the headline chase card is no longer serial-numbered. Two pricing mechanisms are at work at once. Keeping the rare count intact protects the part of the pack buyers value most, while the cut falls on cards they barely count. A 1-in-300 "God Pack" adds a lottery element that makes the per-card price harder to judge. Moving a feature from the standard deck to a new premium tier also makes the standard deck look worse next to it.

## 4. Tesla raises the entry price for an all-wheel-drive Model Y by $8,000 without changing a price

**Source:** The Supercar Blog, citing Electrek (Nov 15): [thesupercarblog.com](https://www.thesupercarblog.com/tesla-quietly-raises-the-entry-price-for-model-y-awd-by-8000-in-the-us/)

Tesla removed the USD 41,990 Model Y AWD from its US configurator, about nine months after launch. The cheapest all-wheel-drive Model Y is now the Premium AWD at USD 49,990, 19% more, and Tesla made no announcement. No listed price changed. What changed is the menu: the bottom rung of the AWD ladder is gone. Buyers who remember USD 41,990 as the reference price for AWD now face a forced trade-up, not a price rise they can complain about. This is the same mechanism as the Collector Booster cut. In both cases the seller raised what customers pay by changing what is offered, not the price tag.

## 5. Borrowers who think rates barely vary don't shop around, and showing them peers' rates changes that

**Source:** Kellogg Insight, on Berwart, Higgins, Kulkarni & Truffa, "Search and Negotiation with Biased Beliefs in Consumer Credit Markets" (Nov 1): [insight.kellogg.northwestern.edu](https://insight.kellogg.northwestern.edu/article/for-loans-it-pays-to-compare)

The study ran a randomized field experiment with 112,063 Chilean loan applicants, linked to the regulator's loan-level data. Most borrowers expected a lower rate than they got (73.2%) and underestimated how widely offers varied (74.8%). A tool showing the rates similar borrowers had received raised negotiation by 38.7%, raised offers received by 13.1%, and cut interest rates by 3.5%. Among borrowers who had underestimated dispersion, negotiation rose 78%. A simpler tool that only estimated the savings from one more quote had no significant effect. The mechanism is a belief about dispersion, not the cost of searching. People search when they think the price could be very different elsewhere, and a credible display of peers' prices is what corrects that belief. The paper is an unreviewed working paper.

**Type:** Research · **Tags:** Academic research, Price transparency, Consumer decision making, Behavioral Economics, Price discrimination

## 6. "Pay now or wait for free" lifts spending by impatient readers more than it costs from patient ones

**Source:** Yale Insights, on Wang, Sudhir & Lee, "Consumer Engagement with Sequential Content" (Nov 4): [insights.som.yale.edu](https://insights.som.yale.edu/insights/to-get-users-to-pay-give-them-the-option-not-to)

The authors use 15 months of data from a serial-fiction platform (more than 10,000 novels, over 1 million users). After some free chapters, readers can pay for the next chapter or wait 1 to 72 hours for it free. In counterfactual simulations of their model, adding the wait option halves spending by patient readers but raises spending by impatient readers, who make nearly two-thirds of purchases, by 36%. The revenue-maximizing design makes about 30% of chapters, the weakest ones, wait-for-free. Cliffhangers and faster pacing push impatient readers to pay. The free option works as a screening device that sorts readers by impatience, and the price of waiting is time, not money. These are model outputs from an unreviewed working paper, not a field test.

**Type:** Research · **Tags:** Academic research, Price discrimination, Pricing models, Hedonic Value, Consumer decision making, Platform pricing

## 7. In per-resolution AI support pricing, the definition of a "resolution" sets the price

**Source:** Rajesh Beri, The D\*AI\*LY Brief (Nov 2026): [beri.net](https://www.beri.net/article/per-resolution-pricing-intercom-fin-vs-zendesk-vs-agentforce-resolution-definition-2026)

Beri compares how three vendors define a billable outcome. Intercom's Fin (USD 0.99) bills "assumed resolutions," which include customers who leave without asking for more help. Zendesk (USD 1.50 to 2.00) bills only resolutions an LLM check confirms. Salesforce's Agentforce Help Agent (USD 2) says abandoned sessions are not billed, though mainly in press statements rather than on its pricing page. On his assumptions, if 15 of Fin's 60 billed resolutions per 100 conversations come back to a human agent, cost per conversation rises from about USD 5.99 to 8.02. Outcome-based pricing is sold as paying only for value. In practice the seller controls the unit's definition, much like a credit's exchange rate, and here silence is counted as success. The buyer's protection is auditability, the same thing OpenAI's API customers asked for when they turned down per-job pricing.

## Pick of the day

#5, the Chilean loan experiment. It isolates a mechanism that price-transparency tools usually assume: people search when they believe prices vary, and a peer-price display is what changes that belief. That bears directly on comparison tools like Mexico's Profeco price-history site.
