## 1. Left-digit bias slowed tariff pass-through: nine-ending prices barely moved

**Source:** Hirshman & Ignatenko, CESifo WP 13056 (SSRN, posted Oct 19): [papers.ssrn.com](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=7622838)

Using the 2025–26 tariffs as a cost shock, the authors find that pass-through went mostly into prices that did not end in 9. Nine-ending prices absorbed much less of the shock, and inflation after the tariffs was higher for non-9-ending products, including ones that were not tariffed. This takes the left-digit literature from demand-side choice to macro price rigidity: a consumer bias acting as a friction that shapes how firms respond to aggregate shocks. It also raises a question about whether retailers "spend" price increases on items whose endings cost them less.

## 2. LLM pricing agents can sustain supracompetitive prices, depending on how they are wired

**Source:** Bichler, Hierhager, Köck & Pieroth, arXiv 2610.21711 (Oct 15): [arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21711)

In repeated Bertrand games, LLM agents that see raw price histories, including rivals' prices, can hold prices high in self-play. Agents fed only summarized profit feedback drift toward competitive prices. In mixed markets, LLMs set a high anchor and then periodically undercut it, which keeps both firms above cost. The finding that interface design determines market outcomes is relevant to the surveillance and algorithmic-pricing debate, and to anyone studying how reference prices form when the "seller" is a model.

## 3. Two Canadian courts narrow what counts as "drip pricing"

**Source:** Bennett Jones (Oct 23), Weinstein v. Sirius XM Canada: [bennettjones.com](https://bennettjones.com/Insights/Blogs/2026/10/Ontario-Court-Rejects-Drip-Pricing-Class-Action) · Bennett Jones (Oct 8), Kuling v. Ticketmaster: [bennettjones.com](https://www.bennettjones.com/Insights/Blogs/2026/10/Federal-Court-Interprets-Drip-Pricing-No-Drip-No-Class)

Canada's Federal Court held that the Competition Act's drip-pricing provision does not require all-in pricing. Its core is concealment plus sequencing (fees revealed later in the funnel), so a fee displayed next to the face value is not drip pricing. Separately, an Ontario court declined to certify a class over Sirius XM's base-price-plus-asterisk advertising, reasoning that even a credulous consumer can read a qualifier like "plus fees." Together, the rulings separate partitioned pricing from drip pricing, a line behavioral researchers rarely draw sharply, and they rest on assumptions about consumer attention that are testable.

## 4. Sam Fender disowns his own stadium-tour prices, then reprices around fairness norms

**Source:** Brit Brief (Oct 24): [britbrief.co.uk](https://britbrief.co.uk/entertainment/music/sam-fender-cuts-ticket-prices-and-removes-front-standing.html) · Far Out (Oct 25): [faroutmagazine.co.uk](https://faroutmagazine.co.uk/sam-fender-reveals-new-ticket-prices-for-2027-tour-condemning-own-prices/)

After fans compared new stadium prices (about £98–£131 for standing) with last year's £65–£88, the artist said he was unhappy with them and that price rises should track only the cost of staging the show. He then scrapped premium "front standing" and set flat tiers: £85 standing, and £55/£75/£95 for seats. The detail to notice is that the old prices were quoted including fees and the new ones excluding fees, so the size of the "cut" is partly a framing effect. The case shows a cost-based fairness heuristic stated by the seller himself, and tier segmentation (split standing) as a fairness trigger in its own right.

## 5. ChatGPT Pro halves its usage allowance at the same price: shrinkflation in abstract units

**Source:** PCWorld (Oct 2; change takes effect Oct 30): [pcworld.com](https://pcworld.com/article/3249113/chatgpt-pros-biggest-perk-just-got-cut-in-half-meet-the-new-ai-subscription-math.html)

OpenAI's $200 Pro plan drops from 20x to 10x the usage of the Plus plan, with a grace period and one-time credits for existing subscribers. Other reports (e.g., [dsebastien.net](https://www.dsebastien.net/2026-09-29-chatgpt-pro-500-the-500-plan-that-came-with-a-pro-200-cut/)) describe a new $500 tier at about 25x. The company argues that efficiency gains will offset the cut. This is a live case of quantity reduction in units consumers cannot easily measure (multiples of another plan), combined with what may act as an upward anchor or decoy tier. Expect churn-and-backlash data after Oct 30.

## 6. Dynamic pricing data cut against the "surge" narrative: about half of price changes were decreases

**Source:** Fortune/AP (Oct 18): [fortune.com](https://fortune.com/2026/10/18/dynamic-pricing-surveillance-prices-consumer-data-digital-labels-algorithmic/)

Web-scraping firm Decodo reports that roughly half of the U.S. price changes it tracked in 2025 were cuts. Separate grocery research found almost no surge pricing after electronic shelf labels were introduced, and vendors say ESL prices are usually updated weekly. The gap between consumer fear and observed behavior is the interesting part: backlash appears to be driven by perceived capability and loss of predictability, not realized price paths. (The Decodo methodology is not disclosed, so treat the figure as suggestive.)

## 7. UK CMA at 18 months of direct fining powers: drip pricing settlements and a delivery-fee review

**Source:** CMA blog, Emma Cochrane (Oct 14): [competitionandmarkets.blog.gov.uk](https://competitionandmarkets.blog.gov.uk/2026/10/14/direct-consumer-enforcement-18-months-on/)

Seven of the eight online-pricing probes opened in November 2025 have settled, and the latest (Gold's Gym, Wayfair, Buy It Direct) carried more than £6m in penalties. The CMA is now reviewing how retailers disclose delivery fees, is watching for misleading discounts, and starts a new subscription-contracts regime in January. Relatedly, a European Parliament research brief (Oct 5: [eprs.europarl.europa.eu](https://eprs.europarl.europa.eu/contents/publications/EPRS/2026/10/EPRS_BRI(2026)791529.html)) expects the EU Digital Fairness Act around mid-November, with dynamic pricing at live events and subscription cancellation as live issues.

## 8. Lyft's subscription experiments: salience failures nearly wipe out the gains from nonlinear pricing

**Source:** Bodoh-Creed, Hickman, List, Muir & Sun, NBER WP 35003 (revised Sept 2026): [nber.org](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35003)

Using large-scale Lyft experiments, the authors model subscription sign-up as distorted by salience failures, forecast errors and impulsivity, while usage stays rational. A frictionless subscription would raise profits by about 5.7% over optimal linear pricing, but the estimated frictions nearly erase that gain. This reverses the usual story that inattention benefits subscription sellers: here, consumers who fail to subscribe when they should is the costly error. This one is slightly outside the recency window but worth knowing.

## 9. Tipping: the attitude–behavior gap persists

**Source:** Salon (Oct 26): [salon.com](https://www.salon.com/2026/10/26/americans-hate-tipping-so-why-havent-they-stopped/)

The essay sets widespread complaints about tipping against Toast data showing full-service tips flat at about 19.1% (15.8% at quick-service) for two years. It argues that tip prompts at the point of sale, plus uncertainty about where the money goes, drive the resentment rather than any change in behavior. The new evidence is thin, but the stable tip rates during peak "tipflation" discourse are a useful data point for research on norm persistence and on whether resentment ever turns into defection.
