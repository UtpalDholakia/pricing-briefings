## Key takeaways

- A shelf-to-register discrepancy can cost customers time as well as money. The Dollar General claims are allegations.
- The appeal of a delayed discount changed when consumers could see what they would pay at renewal.
- Advertised savings can affect whether customers keep looking for a better offer.

## What happened

### 1. A low-price promise depends on what happens at the register

**Source:** [FTC announcement and complaint](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/10/ftc-state-partners-sue-dollar-general-charging-consumers-more-advertised-many-products) · October 26, 2026

The FTC and 20 states sued Dollar General over alleged shelf-to-register overcharges. The complaint reports 29,265 customer complaints categorized as “Did Not Honor Marked Price” between January 2023 and March 2026. These are allegations, not adjudicated findings.

**Tags:** Price transparency, Legal issues in pricing

### 2. A delayed discount can imply a cheaper future

**Source:** [“Discount now or later?”](https://myscp.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/jcpy.70027) · May 13, 2026 · Research discovery

Consumers sometimes preferred a later discount when every payment appeared in dollars, rather than mixing dollar prices with percentage discounts. The proposed mechanism is extrapolation: an improving payment sequence suggests that lower prices might continue. Importantly, disclosing the renewal price removed the delayed-discount advantage. Four main studies included a simulated advertisement with an actual interest-click measure, not completed purchases.

**Tags:** Price Promotions, Subscription Renewals

### 3. “27% off” and “free pillows” can count the same benefit twice

**Source:** [ASA ruling: Origin Sleep](https://www.asa.org.uk/rulings/origin-sleep-uk-ltd-a26-1329295-origin-sleep-uk-ltd.html) · July 15, 2026 · Older discovery

Origin advertised savings of up to 27% with two free pillows worth £196. The percentage represented the saving attributable to the pillows, rather than a separate mattress discount. The ASA concluded that consumers would likely understand the offer as providing both benefits. Explanations behind an information icon or on another page did not correct that impression.

**Tags:** Price Bundling, Disclosure

### 4. One cent can cross a relationship boundary

**Sources:** [“When size doesn’t matter”](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbusres.2026.116046) · April 2026 issue · Research discovery · [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0148296326000809)

Across five restaurant studies, charging for normally complimentary items reduced advertising engagement, perceived fairness, or revisit intention. Even a one-cent surcharge reduced revisit intention, including among consumers who did not select the charged item. The researchers identify violated communal expectations as the mechanism: a small courtesy becomes an explicit transaction. Activating exchange norms mitigated the negative response.

**Tags:** Partitioned Pricing, Social processes

### 5. “Was/now” prices can discourage the next comparison

**Sources:** [Citizens Advice: reference pricing and consumer vulnerability](https://www.citizensadvice.org.uk/policy/publications/online-choice-architecture-and-vulnerability-understanding-the-impact-of/) · December 11, 2025 · Older discovery · [images.ctfassets.net](https://images.ctfassets.net/mfz4nbgura3g/6fPQe104S3SrIqtoceJX6J/91ce6e68071d92dab0faae12c52ac0be/OCAxVulnerability_The_Behaviouralist_Final_V3__1_.pdf)

A preregistered simulation with 8,046 adults tested broadband, gym, and hotel choices. Reference pricing alone reduced correct choices by 1.8 percentage points and reduced visits to a competing site. Participants could earn prize-draw entries for correct decisions.

**Tags:** Processing Prices, Consumer welfare

## Why it matters

**1. A low-price promise depends on what happens at the register** The behavioral issue extends beyond the discrepancy itself. Checking receipts, challenging a cashier, and returning for a refund impose effort. My inference: small errors can persist when recovery costs exceed the refund. Practitioners should audit promotion execution and correction friction, not just intended prices.

**2. A delayed discount can imply a cheaper future** For practitioners, this is a warning about inference as much as an acquisition opportunity. Test what customers expect after the promotion, not only which offer attracts attention.

**3. “27% off” and “free pillows” can count the same benefit twice** The mechanism is perceived benefit addition. Practitioners should distinguish mathematically defensible bundle savings from the extra saving the headline appears to promise. This ruling is an interpretation of likely understanding, not a consumer experiment.

**4. One cent can cross a relationship boundary** The practical distinction is between recovering a negligible cost and changing what the relationship signifies. These findings do not establish that every small restaurant fee reduces actual repeat visits.

**5. “Was/now” prices can discourage the next comparison** The useful mechanism to consider is premature search closure: an apparent saving may make further comparison feel unnecessary. That interpretation should be separated from the measured effects. Practitioners should evaluate whether promotional displays improve selection of suitable offers, rather than treating reduced comparison shopping as an unqualified success.

## Ideas to apply

- Audit both price accuracy at checkout and the effort involved in getting a correction.
- Ask customers to state the expected renewal price when comparing discount schedules.
- Test whether a bundle’s headline reads as one saving or as two separate benefits.
