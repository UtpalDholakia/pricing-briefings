## 1. In Japan, a price increase still calls for an apology, and some firms back down — Malay Mail (AFP)

**Source:** [malaymail.com](https://malaymail.com/news/money/2026/11/09/in-japan-raising-prices-isnt-just-business-its-something-to-apologise-for/242444)

Uniqlo reversed a sock price increase within weeks after a backlash, and Saizeriya's president apologized publicly for a 20-yen rise on a menu item while promising to look for ways to cut prices again. University of Tokyo economist Tsutomu Watanabe thinks it will take another three to four years for consumers to accept ongoing inflation. This is a natural case of reference prices and fairness norms formed during three decades of deflation, which suggests that tolerance for price increases is learned and slow to change, not a fixed trait.

## 2. Diners answer a 3% "admin fee" by withholding the tip — Fox News via WFMD

**Source:** [wfmd.com](https://www.wfmd.com/2026/11/09/restaurant-admin-fees-spark-backlash-as-diners-question-what-theyre-paying-for/)

A Reddit post about an 83-cent admin fee on an Earls Kitchen + Bar bill in Denver drew many replies saying such fees lead people to cut or skip the tip. Separately, a Florida Duffy's location dropped its 3% "operations charge" after a state inspector cited it under Florida's new rule, in effect since July, which requires the fee's amount and purpose to be disclosed before ordering. The fee was already on the menu but was not properly shown on the receipt. The tip-retaliation response suggests diners keep a single mental budget for discretionary payments, so a small surcharge can cost the server far more than it earns the restaurant.

## 3. Australia's ACCC widens its Black Friday sweep, and the past fines show what fake urgency looks like — ABC News (Australia)

**Source:** [abc.net.au](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2026-11-10/shoppers-warned-dodgy-discounts-black-friday-sales/107365588)

The ACCC's annual check of Black Friday ads now covers social posts and marketing emails as well as retailer websites. The article lists earlier penalties: Vuly's countdown timer reset to a larger discount instead of ending, digiDirect showed strike-through prices it had rarely charged, and James Lane planned in advance to extend a sale past its advertised end date. These cases are useful real-world examples of reference-price inflation and false deadlines, and Australia's new unfair-trading law takes effect next July.

## 4. Two seats one row apart, priced A$125 and A$175: CHOICE gives its Shonky to Ticketmaster and Ticketek — TicketNews

**Source:** [ticketnews.com](https://www.ticketnews.com/2026/11/australian-consumer-group-ticketing-giants-in-demand-pricing/)

The Australian consumer group CHOICE singled out "In Demand" pricing in which nearly adjacent seats differed by A$50, BTS prices that fans say were disclosed only once they reached the virtual waiting room, and queue-and-countdown checkout flows that it says pressure buyers into paying more. Ticketmaster says organizers set these prices and that it does not use automated real-time surge pricing. Price gaps between almost identical units are a clear test of when price discrimination feels unfair, and the waiting-room design raises a question about committing to a purchase before seeing the price.

## 5. Grocery workers' union asks newly elected lawmakers to ban electronic shelf labels — AOL

**Source:** [aol.com](https://www.aol.com/articles/grocery-workers-union-calls-newly-220938000.html)

The UFCW says lawmakers should ban both surveillance pricing and digital shelf tags. New Jersey has already banned surveillance pricing and imposed a one-year moratorium on electronic labels, and Maryland requires retailers to hold a price for at least one business day. Walmart and Kroger argue that the labels improve accuracy. Neither side cites data showing that the labels raise prices, so the policy is moving ahead of the evidence. Whether frequent shelf-price changes damage price trust or weaken reference prices is an open and testable question.

## 6. AI shopping agents can steer buyers without changing a single price — Chat2Cart (Substack)

**Source:** [chat2cart.substack.com](https://chat2cart.substack.com/p/ai-shopping-agents-can-steer-you)

The author (who discloses that he founded a firm that tracks agent recommendations) argues that the FTC's proposal to require disclosure of personalized pricing misses the main lever, which is the five-or-so-item shortlist an agent shows instead of roughly 50 search results. He cites a Princeton experiment in which 61% of people chose a sponsored product the agent pushed, compared with 22% in ordinary search, and 55.5% still chose it when told it was sponsored. The argument shifts the behavioral-pricing question from what the price is to what gets considered at all.

## 7. A 1-euro opening-day kebab draws a crowd that turns violent in Dortmund — piqmarkets

**Source:** [piqmarkets.com](https://piqmarkets.com/story/dortmund-police-attacked-as-crowd-storms-kebab-shop-for-1-doner)

A new shop promoted a one-off 1-euro doner (normally 7.50 to 9 euros). Somewhere between 300 people (police estimate) and 1,000 (the owner's estimate) turned up, broke through private security, and threw objects at police. About 100 officers and a helicopter were sent in, and several people were arrested. This is an extreme field example of a near-zero price acting as a social event as much as an economic offer, and of the risk that a launch promotion damages the brand it was meant to build.

## 8. Bichler et al. find LLM pricing agents can set a high-price anchor and undercut it periodically — arXiv (posted Oct 15, older than this digest's usual window)

**Source:** [arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21711)

In repeated Bertrand competition, LLM agents that see raw price histories that include rivals' prices kept prices above competitive levels when competing against copies of themselves. Agents that output price distributions based on summaries of their own profits moved toward competitive prices. When the two designs competed, the result was a cycle: one agent set a high anchor and periodically undercut it, and both stayed above marginal cost. For behavioral pricing scholars, this suggests that the reference-price patterns consumers see (cycles, anchors, "sales") may increasingly depend on how pricing agents are set up, not on managerial intent.

**Type:** Research · **Tags:** Academic research, Algorithmic Pricing, AI in Pricing, Price Promotions, Behavioral Economics, Marginal Costs

## 9. A UK "are the Black Friday discounts real?" study with a pre-registered method — WEM

**Source:** [wem3.ai](https://wem3.ai/press/methodology)

WEM, an affiliate commerce site, pre-registered its rules in August: a discount counts as genuine only if the price is below what the retailer charged over the previous 90 days, with minimum observation thresholds and a commitment to publish nothing if the sample falls short. Results are due between November 10 and 17. The study has an obvious conflict of interest, because WEM earns commission on the shopping traffic it studies, but the pre-registered, price-history test is a useful model for checking claimed reference prices, and the results are worth watching.
