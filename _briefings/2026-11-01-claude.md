## 1. FTC opens a formal study of personalized pricing at nine firms across airlines, ride-hail, delivery and big-box retail — Captain Compliance (summary of the Oct 28 FTC 6(b) orders)

**Source:** [captaincompliance.com](https://captaincompliance.com/education/ftc-6b-orders-personalized-pricing-compensation-delta-uber-walmart/)

Delta, United, JetBlue, Uber, Lyft, DoorDash, Instacart, Target and Walmart have 45 days to explain whether prices vary person to person, what data inputs reach a price, and how loyalty-program membership is used. The orders also ask whether driver pay offers depend on personal data such as acceptance history, which treats price and wage personalization as one problem. For researchers, the eventual report could be the first regulator-compelled description of what firms actually feed into individualized prices, something field studies have only been able to infer.

## 2. FTC and 20 states sue Dollar General over shelf prices that don't match the register — Grocery Dive

**Source:** [grocerydive.com](https://grocerydive.com/news/dollar-general-sued-ftc-alleged-deceptive-pricing/872885)

The complaint cites more than 29,000 price-honoring complaints and undercover buys in which every purchase rang up higher than advertised; one Ohio visit found 13 of 58 items mismatched. The agency notes that fast-scrolling register screens make discrepancies hard to catch. That framing turns a mundane operations failure into a question about limited consumer attention and trust in posted prices, and it falls hardest on low-income shoppers who depend on posted prices to budget.

## 3. Altman says OpenAI almost dropped per-token pricing, then backed off after customers objected — The New Stack

**Source:** [thenewstack.io](https://thenewstack.io/openai-token-pricing-reversal)

On a podcast with Stripe's John Collison, Altman described testing a single per-job price in place of token metering. Buyers pushed back because they wanted to see what they were paying for, even though he calls tokens a "fake unit." This is a live example of a buyer preference for an auditable, granular unit over a simpler flat price, which runs against the usual flat-rate bias and pain-of-paying arguments for bundled pricing. It also raises the open question of when partitioning signals fairness rather than obfuscation.

**Type:** Practice · **Tags:** AI pricing, Token pricing, Fairness, Price Bundling, Metered Pricing, Flat-rate pricing

## 4. Card data on 174M consumers: only about 2.9% pay for generative AI, and they are very price sensitive — NBER Working Paper 35953 (Egan, Russel, Shi, Yao)

**Source:** [nber.org](https://nber.org/papers/w35953)

Almost all paying users sit on basic plans at $30 a month or less. Annual retention runs 20–45%, multihoming is falling, and switching spikes around major model releases. A structural model finds the services are becoming closer substitutes, which limits pricing power. For subscription-pricing scholars, this is rare revealed-preference evidence on churn in a category where a free tier anchors the reference price at zero.

**Type:** Research · **Tags:** Academic research, AI in Pricing, AI pricing, subscription pricing, Subscription Renewals, Tiered Pricing, Behavioral Economics

## 5. A Sony job posting for "intelligent pricing" on the PS Store revives fears about personalized discounts — Push Square

**Source:** [pushsquare.com](https://www.pushsquare.com/news/2026/10/ps5-dynamic-pricing-concerns-revived-by-new-sony-job-listing)

The senior monetisation role covers expanding a targeted-offers program that earlier in 2026 quietly gave some users deeper discounts than others, with no explanation of who qualified. Players reacted to discount-only personalization as if it were a price hike. That supports the idea that fairness judgments are relative to other buyers, not only to a past price. Gaming is a clean setting: a single walled storefront, a digital-only future, and an engaged community comparing notes.

## 6. From disclosure to prohibition: how state and city surveillance-pricing laws differ — Sidley Data Matters

**Source:** [datamatters.sidley.com](https://datamatters.sidley.com/2026/10/27/from-disclosures-to-prohibitions-the-new-legal-landscape-on-the-use-of-consumer-data-for-personalized-pricing/)

This is a careful map of Maryland (in force Oct 1), Connecticut, New Jersey (the first private right of action), New York, and Seattle's new ordinance against algorithmic price discrimination. The laws split on whether they target the algorithm, the personal-data inputs, or price variability itself. Connecticut's mandated disclosure text, a notice that the price was raised using your personal data, is in effect a ready-made experimental stimulus. How consumers respond to that kind of disclosure is an open empirical question.

## 7. EU Digital Fairness Act expected mid-November; one lawyer argues most of its targets are already illegal — Lawgent

**Source:** [lawgent.se](https://lawgent.se/the-digital-fairness-act-arrives-in-november-most-of-what-it-targets-is-already-unlawful/)

The argument is that the Act responds to enforcement difficulty, not missing rules. Personalised pricing stays legal with disclosure, and the Commission's real target is personalization that exploits identified vulnerabilities. The one genuinely new obligation is likely to be cancellation as easy as sign-up (69% of consultation respondents reported trouble cancelling). It is useful context ahead of a proposal that may define "vulnerability-based" pricing in law, a construct behavioral researchers will be asked to operationalize.

**Type:** Opinion · **Tags:** Price discrimination, Disclosure, Fairness, Regulation, Pricing Laws, Legal issues in pricing, Subscription Renewals

## 8. Fall shrinkflation roundup: detergent jugs lose 10%, and "family size" stays on the box while the box shrinks — Mouseprint

**Source:** [mouseprint.org](https://www.mouseprint.org/2026/10/26/here-we-shrink-again-fall-2026-part-2/)

Edgar Dworsky's long-running catalog adds Cheer (92 to 81 oz, with the dosing cap unchanged), Old Spice, Sara Lee bagels (6 to 5) and Welch's. ## 9. BNPL spreads into groceries, and a third of grocery BNPL users have missed a payment — Marketplace

**Source:** [marketplace.org](https://www.marketplace.org/story/2026/10/29/more-people-to-turn-to-buy-now-pay-later-for-holiday-purchases)

TSG survey data suggest about half of consumers will use BNPL this holiday season, with 20% planning to use it more versus 9% less. Urban Institute research finds roughly 1 in 10 working-age adults buy groceries this way. Splitting a recurring, low-ticket necessity into installments is a different mental-accounting problem from financing a gift, and the missed-payment rate suggests the decoupling of payment from consumption is costing these users.
