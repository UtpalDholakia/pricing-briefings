## 1. FTC opens a 6(b) study of personalized prices AND personalized pay — FTC press release

**Source:** [ftc.gov](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/10/ftc-launches-personalized-pricing-compensation-inquiry)

On Oct. 28 the FTC sent compulsory study orders to Delta, United, JetBlue, Uber, Lyft, DoorDash, Instacart, Target and Walmart, asking for the inputs, tools and procedures behind prices and worker compensation, including whether loyalty status or personal data make prices vary person to person. The agency frames it as correcting its 2024 study, which only looked at pricing consultancies rather than the firms that set prices. For researchers, the new twist is the symmetry: the same opaque, data-driven "price" logic applied to gig workers' pay offers (e.g., based on past acceptance history), which opens a fairness-perception question on the labor side that the consumer literature has barely touched.

## 2. LLM pricing agents can sustain high prices — or not — depending on how they are deployed — arXiv (Bichler, Hierhager, Köck, Pieroth)

**Source:** [arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21711)

In repeated Bertrand simulations, LLM agents that see raw market histories (including rivals' prices) and post one price per period sustain supracompetitive prices in self-play, while agents that see only summarized own-profit histories and output price distributions drift toward competitive prices. In mixed markets, an LLM can hold a high "anchor" price and undercut it periodically, producing price cycles that keep both firms above cost. Pair it with Wadi & Ma's "Shopping by algorithm" (arXiv, Sept. 2026, [arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.28372)), which finds LLM shopping agents fall for just-below prices and promotional framing only when attribute search is costly and the goal prompt is vague; together they suggest that both sides of the agentic market inherit "psychology" from interface and deployment design rather than from the model itself.

## 3. Behavioral frictions nearly wipe out the profit from subscription (nonlinear) pricing at Lyft — NBER WP 35003 (Bodoh-Creed, Hickman, List, Muir, Sun; revised Sept. 2026)

**Source:** [nber.org](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35003)

Using Lyft subscription experiments, the authors estimate a structural model in which the decision to subscribe is distorted by salience failures, forecast errors and impulsivity, while usage after subscribing is rational. A frictionless program would lift profit about 5.7% over optimal linear pricing, but the estimated frictions erase nearly all of that gain, and recovering it would require cross-team coordination to reduce the frictions. This is a useful inversion of the usual "subscription traps" story: here consumer inattention mostly hurts the firm by causing people who would benefit to not subscribe.

## 4. OpenAI halves its $200 plan's allowance (effective tomorrow) while adding a $500 tier — The New Stack

**Source:** [thenewstack.io](https://thenewstack.io/openai-halves-200-plan/)

Starting Oct. 30, ChatGPT's $200 Pro plan drops from 20x to 10x the Plus usage allowance and from 200 to 100 premium-model messages per week, at an unchanged price, while a new $500 plan offers 25x. OpenAI argues that more efficient models mean users still get more done than before, and it is softening the blow with a one-time credit grant (reported at roughly $2,500 in value, expiring Dec. 31); developers' reaction has been largely negative. It is a clean live case of shrinkflation applied to an intangible, usage-metered service, combined with a new top tier that recasts the old middle option. It also raises the question of whether consumers treat efficiency gains as theirs by entitlement, a reference-dependence point worth testing.

## 5. Michael Burry cancels a ~41% Substack price hike after reader pushback — Stocktwits

**Source:** [stocktwits.com](https://stocktwits.com/news-articles/markets/equity/michael-burry-cancels-substack-price-hike-plan-pushback-denies-money-grab/cZUdA8eRByd)

Burry planned to raise his newsletter from $49 to $69 a month for new subscribers only, arguing a higher price would protect community quality by deterring drive-by joiners who leak content; existing subscribers were already grandfathered. He reversed within days, citing accessibility and continuity. The interesting wrinkle for fairness research is that the backlash came even though current payers were insulated, and it centered on whether price can legitimately be used as a screening or gatekeeping device rather than as a revenue lever.

## 6. Chipotle deliberately prices below cost inflation and finds lower-income guests respond best when promos are lightest — Chipotle Q3 2026 earnings call (via The Motley Fool)

**Source:** [fool.com](https://www.fool.com/earnings/call-transcripts/2026/10/29/chipotle-cmg-q3-2026-earnings-call-transcript/)

Management said pricing will run about 2% for 2026 against roughly 3% cost inflation (an intentional ~100bp gap) and that price increases are evaluated pre/post against control restaurants so they can be rolled back. Two details stand out: lower-income cohorts outperformed higher-income ones for the first time this year, most strongly in the month with the least promotion, and a 1.4-point mix drag came from smaller group sizes as Rewards engagement rose. Both are field hints that loyalty enrollment changes basket composition and that "value" for price-sensitive segments may not be driven by discounts.

## 7. Personalized-pricing law shifts from disclosure to prohibition; Seattle joins the states — Sidley Data Matters

**Source:** [datamatters.sidley.com](https://datamatters.sidley.com/2026/10/27/from-disclosures-to-prohibitions-the-new-legal-landscape-on-the-use-of-consumer-data-for-personalized-pricing/)

Sidley's Oct. 27 roundup notes that Maryland's ban on data-driven dynamic pricing of certain foods and delivery took effect Oct. 1; that Seattle signed an ordinance on Oct. 5 prohibiting "algorithmic-based price discrimination" (effective Sept. 2027); and that New Jersey's grocery and necessities ban carries a private right of action. The FTC's proposed enforcement policy statement is still pending. The patchwork (bans vs. disclosure, loyalty-program and consent carve-outs, exemptions for discounts to veterans, teachers and seniors) codifies a lay theory of which price differences feel fair, and those carve-outs map closely onto the dual-entitlement and justification literatures. Note: sources disagree on New York's status, so check before citing.

## 8. Court lets FTC's drip-pricing case against Ticketmaster proceed on the theory that the first price seen matters — All About Advertising Law (Venable)

**Source:** [allaboutadvertisinglaw.com](https://www.allaboutadvertisinglaw.com/2026/10/court-allows-ftc-drip-pricing-lawsuit-against-ticketmaster-to-proceed.html)

A California federal court denied Live Nation/Ticketmaster's motion to dismiss, accepting the FTC's theory that a fee-excluded price can be deceptive because it induces first contact and drives filtering, even if the full price appears before checkout; it called a bare "+Fees" label vague and ambiguous. A Maryland court reached the opposite result under state law in a Vivid Seats case, because reliance was not shown. This is effectively a legal adoption of anchoring and initial-exposure effects, and the split over reliance highlights an evidentiary gap behavioral researchers could fill.

## 9. Shrinkflation's newest tricks: unchanged dosing instructions, legacy "family size" labels, and "new format" with a lower RRP — Mouseprint (US) and t-online/Verbraucherzentrale Hamburg (Germany)

**Source:** [mouseprint.org](https://www.mouseprint.org/2026/10/26/here-we-shrink-again-fall-2026-part-2/) · [t-online.de](https://www.t-online.de/leben/aktuelles/id_101468062/mogelpackung-des-monats-nestle-versteckt-preiserhoehung-hinter-design.html)

Mouseprint's fall roundup flags Cheer detergent cut from 92 to 81 oz with the same dosing directions and cap (so fewer loads per jug), and Lucky Charms continuing to shrink under an unchanged "family size" label. In Germany, consumer advocates named Nestlé's KitKat/Lion/Smarties mini multipacks "misleading package of the month": 13 pieces became 10 under a "new format" flag, and Nestlé pointed to a lower suggested retail price even though the per-piece price still rose. Together they show firms now managing not just the size cue but the reference cues around it (usage instructions, size descriptors and a visible lower list price) to blunt unit-price detection.
