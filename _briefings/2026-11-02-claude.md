## 1. The FTC asks nine firms to show whether loyalty status and browsing data move individual prices (and gig pay)

**Source:** MarketScale; airline angle from The Points Party · [marketscale.com](https://www.marketscale.com/industries/retail/ftc-orders-nine-companies-to-explain-how-personal-data-shapes-prices-and-pay) · [thepointsparty.com](https://thepointsparty.com/articles/ftc-orders-delta-united-jetblue-personalized-pricing-inquiry)

On Oct 28 the FTC voted 2-0 to send 6(b) study orders to Delta, United, JetBlue, Uber, Lyft, DoorDash, Instacart, Target and Walmart. The orders ask whether prices differ across customers based on loyalty-program membership or analysis of personal data, and whether driver pay depends on opaque signals such as acceptance history. Responses are due in 45 days. This is a study, not an enforcement action. What stands out for researchers is that loyalty membership is now treated as a possible input to personalized pricing rather than a safe harbor. That puts the old line between rewarding loyal customers and exploiting captive ones in front of a regulator.

## 2. Sony moves to expand "intelligent pricing" on the PlayStation Store, and heavy users may be getting the worse deals

**Source:** iXBT.games (Russian gaming press, English edition) · [ixbt.games](https://ixbt.games/en/news/2026/10/30/playstation-store-nacnet-naznacat-individualnye-ceny-s-pomoshhiu-ii.amp.html)

A new Sony job listing for a senior director of digital game monetization calls for building a smart pricing system and widening "targeted offers," personalized discounts aimed at specific user segments. Players have already reported that the same game is priced differently across accounts, with active owners sometimes seeing worse offers than lapsed ones. This is a clean real-world case of a loyalty penalty in a digital storefront where prices are easy to compare across accounts. That makes it a natural setting for studying how fast perceived unfairness spreads once people notice the differences.

## 3. Independent grocers pledge "no surge pricing by person" and recast electronic shelf labels as plain price tags

**Source:** The Shelby Report (grocery trade press); see also Salon's Nov 2 commentary · [theshelbyreport.com](https://theshelbyreport.com/2026/10/27/nga-sets-forth-independent-grocers-principles-on-pricing-technology/) · [salon.com](https://salon.com/2026/11/02/the-strange-new-math-behind-the-price-of-your-groceries)

The National Grocers Association board approved principles that reject using a customer's personal data to charge that person more. The principles also require loyalty programs to be opt-in with visible benefits, and describe ESLs as tools for quick accurate updates, not for pricing based on who is in the aisle. The pledge echoes Walmart's "price the product, not the person" line and comes right after Maryland's ban on personalized grocery pricing took effect Oct 1. Salon's piece argues that the real problem is the information gap between store and shopper, not price levels. The open question is whether public product-versus-person pledges actually rebuild trust when shoppers can't check them.

## 4. India's insurance regulator says dynamic pricing belongs in airlines but not in insurance

**Source:** Business Standard (BFSI Insight Summit, Mumbai) · [business-standard.com](https://business-standard.com/amp/specials/bs-events/irdai-chief-dynamic-pricing-insurance-sector-bs-bfsi-summit-2026-126102700559_1.html)

On Oct 27, IRDAI chairman Ajay Seth said digital insurance platforms should leave no room for dynamic pricing and should focus on transparent, comparable pricing instead. The remark is a regulator stating outright that acceptable price variation depends on the category: fine for seats, objectionable for protection products. That raises a researchable question about which product features (essentialness, risk, regularity of purchase) set consumers' fairness boundaries for variable pricing.

## 5. Halloween candy shows shrinkflation and price stickiness together: per-ounce prices doubled while cocoa costs fell

**Source:** Marketplace · [marketplace.org](https://www.marketplace.org/story/2026/10/28/how-much-more-expensive-has-candy-really-gotten)

Comparing archived listings, Marketplace found Hershey's Kisses party bags shrank from 35.8 to 30 oz while the shelf price rose. The per-ounce price went from 25 to 50 cents, and Brach's candy corn also doubled per ounce after its bag shrank. Cocoa futures have fallen by more than half from their peak, but shoppers haven't seen relief because candymakers buy inventory months ahead. The piece is a useful classroom case on asymmetric pass-through ("rockets and feathers") and on how downsizing hides per-unit increases that a list-price comparison would expose.

## 6. LLM pricing agents can sustain supra-competitive prices, depending on how they're deployed

**Source:** arXiv (Bichler, Hierhager, Köck, Pieroth), Oct 15 · [arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21711)

In repeated Bertrand simulations, LLM agents that set one price per period from raw market histories, including rival prices, kept prices high in self-play. Agents that output price distributions from summaries of their own profits moved toward competitive prices. In mixed markets the LLMs learned a high-price-plus-periodic-undercut pattern that kept both firms above cost. The finding that the interface shapes the outcome matters for behavioral pricing: what consumers end up facing may depend more on prompt and data design choices than on any particular "algorithm."

## 7. AI shopping agents fall for human-style pricing heuristics, but only when search is costly and the goal is vague

**Source:** arXiv econ.GN (Wadi and Ma), posted late September · [arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.28372v1)

Using a process-tracing setup that puts product attributes behind costly tool calls, the authors tested eight deployed LLMs from three providers. With free information, pricing cues rarely misled them. When information cost something and the goal was vague, the models skipped the attributes needed to compute unit price and made heuristic-like mistakes. A specific goal prompt largely fixed this. The implication is that whether behavioral pricing tactics still work on machine buyers depends on storefront information design, not on the models being immune or naive.

**Type:** Research · **Tags:** Academic research, AI in Pricing, Behavioral Economics, Processing Prices

## 8. Courts split on what counts as drip pricing: a US judge says "+Fees" is too vague, while a Canadian court rejects mandatory all-in pricing

**Source:** All About Advertising Law (Manatt); Cassels on Competition (Canada) · [allaboutadvertisinglaw.com](https://www.allaboutadvertisinglaw.com/2026/10/court-allows-ftc-drip-pricing-lawsuit-against-ticketmaster-to-proceed.html) · [cassels.com](https://cassels.com/insights/cassels-on-competition-october-2026/)

In the FTC's case against Ticketmaster, a California federal court let the deception claims proceed. It relied on the "first contact" doctrine (a misleading initial price can deceive even if the total appears before purchase) and held that a "+Fees" label doesn't tell consumers that the cost may rise as much as 44%. In Kuling v. Ticketmaster, Canada's Federal Court found that showing a base price and a separate fee at the same checkout stage doesn't breach Canada's drip-pricing provision. The two rulings disagree on whether the initial price, used as a reference point and for filtering listings, is itself the harm. That is a legal version of a debate behavioral researchers have been having about drip pricing.

## 9. NYC sues CarGurus because its "Great Deal" badges were computed on prices that left out mandatory fees

**Source:** NYC Department of Consumer and Worker Protection · [nyc.gov](https://www.nyc.gov/site/dca/news/71-26/mamdani-administration-sues-cargurus-deceiving-new-yorkers-ai-chatbot-hidden-junk-fees)

DCWP alleges more than 2,000 violations: deal ratings and an AI chatbot's "deal" lists were based on prices that excluded fees, down payments and trade-in requirements. In one example, a car labeled $2,212 below market came out $1,473 above market once fees were added. Across 40,000+ listings, fees added about $1,100 on average. This case moves the junk-fee issue from the price itself to an algorithmic reference-price signal (the deal badge) and to AI assistants that pass the distortion along.
