## 1. Prices ending in 9 absorbed far less of the tariff shock — Hirshman & Ignatenko, SSRN / CESifo WP 13056

**Source:** [papers.ssrn.com](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=7622838)

Using retail price data around the recent US tariffs, the authors find that pass-through went mostly to prices not ending in 9. Prices ending in 9 moved much less, and inflation rose more for non-9 items, even untariffed ones. This is a macro-scale sign that firms' beliefs about left-digit bias limit how they respond to cost shocks. It raises a good question: do 9-endings act as a self-imposed price ceiling, and does that pressure show up later as shrinkflation or larger jumps across a digit threshold?

## 2. Personalized grocery prices built from loyalty data: most shoppers gain, total consumer surplus falls — Durrmeyer, Fournel & Samano (working paper), via Market Business News

**Source:** [marketbusinessnews.com](https://marketbusinessnews.com/grocery-loyalty-data-personalized-pricing/452198/)

The authors estimate demand from one year of loyalty-card purchases (about 20,700 households, 24 categories) and simulate individual pricing. Profits rise about 7% and roughly 84% of shoppers come out ahead, but large losses for a minority outweigh those gains. This complicates the fairness debate: the median shopper benefits while the losses are concentrated. It also suggests that backlash may depend more on who visibly loses than on the average effect. These are simulations from one store with no competition modeled.

## 3. LLM pricing agents can set a high "anchor" price and undercut it periodically, keeping prices above cost — Bichler, Hierhager, Köck & Pieroth, arXiv

**Source:** [arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21711)

In repeated Bertrand games, standard learning algorithms settle near marginal cost. LLM agents can sustain higher prices, and the result depends on deployment details: whether they see rivals' raw price histories or only summaries of their own profits. In mixed markets, LLMs set high-price anchors and periodically undercut them. This is a reference-price dynamic produced by machines, and it matters for how consumers learn prices in agent-priced markets.

**Type:** Research · **Tags:** Academic research, Algorithmic Pricing, AI in Pricing, Legal issues in pricing, Behavioral Economics, Marginal Costs

## 4. A "moral harm" account of price fairness, now in print — Campbell, Pomerance & Percival Carter, Journal of Consumer Research (Oct 2026 issue)

**Source:** [academic.oup.com](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article/53/3/444/8195730)

Across eight studies, the authors argue that fairness judgments come from inferences about whether a price harms people. Those inferences depend on the pricing strategy, how vulnerable the buyers are, and how much the product matters to their welfare. One finding beyond dual entitlement: holding a price steady after costs fall is judged unfair, especially when the buyers are vulnerable. The harm-to-unfairness link is also weaker among conservatives. This is a useful lens for current fights over grocery prices and personalized pricing.

**Type:** Research · **Tags:** Academic research, Price discrimination, Fairness, retailing, Consumer welfare, Costs

## 5. The fast-food value war is over, and analysts say every chain lost — Restaurant Dive

**Source:** [restaurantdive.com](https://www.restaurantdive.com/news/price-value-wars-over-fast-food-chains-shift-food-quality/833658/)

McDonald's $3-and-under menu stalled (about a third of franchisees didn't run it as planned), and Wendy's now calls its bundles "value diluting." Chains are shifting to quality messaging. Cava, which has priced below inflation since 2019, kept rising value scores even as its customers' incomes fell. This is field evidence that deep-discount tiers don't buy traffic once reference prices have reset, and that perceived value tracks restraint over time more than headline deals.

## 6. Carnival brings dynamic pricing to drink packages and Wi-Fi — CP24 / CTV News

**Source:** [cp24.com](https://www.cp24.com/news/world/2026/10/20/major-cruise-line-to-introduce-dynamic-pricing/)

Starting Dec. 1, Carnival's beverage and Wi-Fi packages (sold alone or in new "FunPacks" bundles) will vary in price by sailing, itinerary and season, with the price locked once bought. It is also a natural experiment in whether price locks reduce perceived unfairness.

As Gov. Hochul weighs the One Fair Price package, advocates (EPIC, CDT) want location-data and purchase-data loopholes closed. ## 8. EU consumer authorities take on in-game virtual currencies at nine game companies — Lewis Silkin

**Source:** [lewissilkin.com](https://www.lewissilkin.com/insights/2026/10/15/eu-consumer-protection-network-launches-action-involving-nine-video-games-compani-102o5do)

The CPC Network is applying its principles on virtual currencies: show real-money prices, don't hide costs behind layered currencies or exchanges, don't force purchases of unwanted currency bundles, and honor 14-day withdrawal rights for unused currency. In effect, regulators are treating token-based payment as a known psychological device for reducing pain of paying and price transparency. If firms comply, the change could serve as a test of how much token framing raises spending.

## 9. Court: a "+Fees" label may not cure drip pricing, and deception can occur at first contact — All About Advertising Law (Manatt)

**Source:** [allaboutadvertisinglaw.com](https://www.allaboutadvertisinglaw.com/2026/10/court-allows-ftc-drip-pricing-lawsuit-against-ticketmaster-to-proceed.html)

In denying Ticketmaster's motion to dismiss, the court accepted that luring initial interest with partitioned prices can be deceptive even if the full price appears before checkout. It called "+Fees" too vague to signal a markup of up to 44%. Ticketmaster's argument that going all-in early would have hurt it competitively was read as confirming that low headline prices pull buyers in. The law is moving toward the psychology of anchoring on the headline price.

## 10. Fall shrinkflation roundup: count cuts, store brands, and the largest packs — Mouseprint

**Source:** [mouseprint.org](https://www.mouseprint.org/2026/10/17/here-we-shrink-again-fall-2026-part-1/)

Edgar Dworsky's latest list covers Hershey's Kisses (10 to 8.7 oz), Ziploc freezer bags (16 fewer per box), Stop & Shop oatmeal (10 to 8 packets), Laughing Cow, Dixie plates, and a cut to Eight O'Clock's largest coffee bag. The pattern worth noting is downsizing of count-based goods and of the biggest "value" sizes, where consumers may rely most on habit and least on checking unit prices. Read alongside item 1, it fits the idea that firms protecting a price point push the adjustment into quantity.
