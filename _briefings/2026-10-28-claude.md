## 1. FTC and 20 states sue Dollar General over shelf-vs-register price gaps

**Source:** FirstAlert4/Gray (also CNBC, Oct 26): [firstalert4.com](https://www.firstalert4.com/2026/10/27/ftc-20-states-accuse-dollar-general-overcharging-shoppers-register/)

The complaint says shoppers were routinely charged more at the register than the shelf, tag or ad price. Missouri's earlier undercover checks across 147 stores found baskets averaging about $7 above shelf totals. Dollar General says the redress demand is far out of line with actual discrepancies. The behavioral question for researchers: low-price-image formats depend on shoppers not checking the receipt. Who audits the "price you see," and do shelf-price errors hit low-income, low-attention shoppers hardest?

## 2. Personalized grocery prices make most shoppers better off but lower total consumer surplus

**Source:** Market Business News, on a new working paper by Durrmeyer, Fournel & Samano (Oct 22): [marketbusinessnews.com](https://marketbusinessnews.com/grocery-loyalty-data-personalized-pricing/452198/)

Using loyalty-card data on about 20,700 shoppers in 24 categories, the authors simulate targeted pricing. Profits rise about 7%, and 84% of shoppers come out ahead, yet aggregate consumer surplus falls because a minority loses a lot. This complicates the "surveillance pricing hurts everyone" framing that is driving new state laws. It also raises a fairness-perception question: does the public judge the median shopper or the worst-hit one? The paper is a simulation from one store's 2018 data, has not been peer-reviewed, and ignores competition between stores.

## 3. Whether LLM pricing agents keep prices high depends on how they are set up

**Source:** arXiv, Bichler, Hierhager, Köck & Pieroth (Oct 15): [arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21711)

In repeated Bertrand games, LLM agents that set one price a period from raw histories that include rivals' prices sustained above-competitive prices with each other. Agents that output price distributions from summaries of their own profits drifted toward competitive prices. When the two setups were mixed, markets cycled between a high-price anchor and periodic undercutting. For behavioral pricing, that is a machine version of anchoring: what the agent is shown shapes the price level, not just the algorithm.

**Type:** Research · **Tags:** Academic research, Algorithmic Pricing, AI in Pricing, Behavioral Economics

## 4. Different AI shopping agents have very different price sensitivity

**Source:** arXiv, Pavel Kireyev, "PriceBench" (Sept 25; slightly older, but it pairs with item 3 from the buyer side): [arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.31468)

Fitting a logit choice model to 28 LLMs booking among 179 real NYC hotels, the author finds price sensitivity varies more than tenfold across models. On identical tasks, the mean booked nightly rate ranged from $247 to $393, and preferences differed even within one model family. If agents do the buying, "the consumer's price sensitivity" partly becomes a property of whichever model the consumer happens to use. Tasks, code and responses are public, so the setup can be reused for anchoring, decoy or partitioned-price tests.

**Type:** Research · **Tags:** Academic research, AI in Pricing, Partitioned Pricing, Good-Better-Best Pricing, Hospitality, Behavioral Economics, Variable pricing

## 5. Japanese shoppers will tolerate smaller packs but reject raised container bottoms

**Source:** Macromill survey, resurfaced in Nikkei / Nikkei MJ (Oct 19 and 26); original release: [prtimes.jp](https://prtimes.jp/main/html/rd/p/000000686.000000624.html)

In a 3,000-person survey, 90% say they notice "stealth price hikes." The split is nearly even: 53% would rather keep the volume and pay more, 47% would rather keep the price and get less. But about 60% call raised container bottoms or double-walled packaging unacceptable, well above the share objecting to a smaller pack or fewer pieces. That points to a useful distinction for shrinkflation research: an honest-but-smaller pack seems to read as a price change, while a pack built to look full reads as deception.

## 6. US surveillance-pricing laws are moving from disclosure to outright bans

**Source:** Sidley Data Matters (Oct 27): [datamatters.sidley.com](https://datamatters.sidley.com/2026/10/27/from-disclosures-to-prohibitions-the-new-legal-landscape-on-the-use-of-consumer-data-for-personalized-pricing/)

A useful map of how the states differ. Maryland's food-retail ban took effect Oct 1. New Jersey is the first state to give consumers a private right of action. Connecticut requires sellers outside its banned sectors to disclose when personal data raised a price. Seattle's new ordinance reaches electronic shelf labels. The laws define the target three different ways: by mechanism (algorithms), by input (personal data) or by price variability. That matters for anyone measuring how consumers react to "personalized" versus "dynamic" pricing. Separately, the EU Parliament's research service says the Commission's Digital Fairness Act proposal is expected in mid-November: [eprs.europarl.europa.eu](https://eprs.europarl.europa.eu/contents/publications/EPRS/2026/10/EPRS_BRI(2026)791529.html)

## 7. Independent grocers commit not to use personal data to raise prices

**Source:** Supermarket News (Oct 26): [supermarketnews.com](https://supermarketnews.com/grocery-technology/nga-sets-forth-principles-on-pricing-technology)

The National Grocers Association published voluntary principles. Members pledge not to use a shopper's personal data to set higher individual prices, to keep loyalty enrollment voluntary and easy to leave, and to use electronic shelf labels as price tags rather than tools that react to who is nearby. Note what the pledge rules out (data-based markups) and what it keeps (data-based discounts). This is the same higher-vs-lower distinction the research and legislation keep stumbling over. A good case of an industry using fairness norms to head off regulation.

## 8. Shrinkflation catalog, fall 2026: Cheer cut the jug but kept the cap

**Source:** Mouseprint, Edgar Dworsky (Oct 26): [mouseprint.org](https://www.mouseprint.org/2026/10/26/here-we-shrink-again-fall-2026-part-2/)

Mouseprint's fall entries include Cheer (92 to 81 oz), Old Spice (3.3 to 3 oz), Welch's jam, Sara Lee bagels (cut in steps) and Lucky Charms still labeled "family size." The Cheer case is clever. The dosing cap and instructions are unchanged, so the cost per load rises while each wash feels exactly the same. Shoppers notice the shrinkage only when the jug runs out early, which makes it hard to detect at purchase.

## 9. Priced out of arena shows, UK fans are turning to tribute acts

**Source:** Irish News / PA, on a Night Time Industries Association survey (Oct 26): [irishnews.com](https://irishnews.com/news/uk/arctic-numpties-to-daft-funk-fans-priced-out-of-concerts-turn-to-tribute-acts-DRDJ4RN5PVLCROIFHB7EOBZ7OA)

About a fifth of UK adults went to a tribute show last year, typically at £20–30. Over two-thirds say seeing major acts has become too expensive, and price is the main reason given by those planning more tribute nights. A neat substitution story to set against dynamic-pricing debates. If headline tickets anchor fans' sense of "a night out" too high, part of demand moves to a cheaper substitute rather than vanishing. This is survey data from an industry body, so read the numbers with caution.

**Type:** News · **Tags:** concert ticket pricing, Behavioral Economics, Affordability

## 10. Halloween chocolate stays expensive even after cocoa fell about 70%

**Source:** WJLA (Oct 20): [wjla.com](https://wjla.com/newsletter-daily/halloween-candy-prices-2026-trick-or-treating-chocolate-costs-cocoa-shortage-inflation-grocery-hersheys-reeses-pieces-shrinkflation-consumer-tariffs-money-saving-tips-shopping-deals)

Chocolate retail prices are still climbing (analysts put them up about 11–12%) despite the collapse in cocoa futures, while shoppers trade down to gummies and hard candy. Spending is still forecast at a record. Read this next to Campbell, Pomerance & Percival Carter's JCR paper in the current issue (Oct 2026, "Painful Prices"; [academic.oup.com](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article/53/3/444/8195730)). They find that keeping a price unchanged after costs fall is judged less fair than keeping it unchanged when costs are flat. Chocolate is a live test of whether that moral reaction ever shows up in behavior or only in surveys.
