## 1. FTC compels nine firms, from airlines to grocery, to explain how they use personal data to set prices, and also worker pay

**Source:** Skift: [skift.com](https://skift.com/2026/11/04/what-the-ftc-wants-to-know-about-how-airlines-set-fares/) · Holland & Knight analysis: [hklaw.com](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/11/ftcs-section-6b-orders-on-personalized-pricing-and-pay-what-businesses)

On Oct 28 the FTC voted 2-0 to send compulsory 6(b) orders to Delta, United, JetBlue, Uber, Lyft, DoorDash, Instacart, Target and Walmart. The orders ask for every method that uses personal information to set prices, plus A/B tests, segmentation schemes, disclosure wording (with screenshots) and, for platforms, how consumer prices feed into worker pay. Responses are due mid-December. For behavioral researchers, the request list shows what regulators think the relevant variables are, and the A/B-test archive could eventually become the largest field record of personalized-price experiments. The airlines' denials hinge on the line between "dynamic" and "personalized" pricing, which consumers may not see the same way.

## 2. PlayStation Store shows different Black Friday discounts on the same game to different accounts

**Source:** Push Square: [pushsquare.com](https://www.pushsquare.com/news/2026/11/ps5-dynamic-pricing-impacts-new-ps-store-black-friday-offers-roughly-usd5-variations-in-prices)

Sony appears to be testing account-level prices on first-party titles, with gaps of about $5 (Astro Bot at $33.59 vs. $39.59, for example). One user reported a 60% discount on a main account and 30% on a secondary one. Sony has not commented, and a late-October job post for "intelligent pricing" suggests the practice may grow. This is a clean natural case of personalized discount depth, as opposed to personalized list price. Comment threads split between calling it discrimination and comparing it to loyalty or student discounts, which makes it useful for studying how framing as a discount changes perceived fairness.

## 3. Australia's Choice gives Ticketek and Ticketmaster "Shonky" awards over dynamic ticket pricing

**Source:** Michael West Media (AAP): [michaelwest.com.au](https://michaelwest.com.au/ticketing-giants-dealt-booby-prize-for-dynamic-pricing)

Choice said fans of a John Farnham tribute concert saw fluctuating, inflated prices on about 7% of tickets. NRL fans reported "in demand" seats costing $50 more than comparable seats one row away. Ticketmaster's defence is that organisers set the prices and that no real-time surge algorithm is involved. That raises an open question for attribution research: do consumers assign blame for a price to the platform they see or to the party that actually set it? The case also shows how a nearby seat becomes the reference point that makes a premium feel unfair.

## 4. Germany's October shrinkflation list: store brands shrink too, and a bigger bottle costs more per millilitre

**Source:** MarkenDetektive (German), summarizing Verbraucherzentrale Hamburg's Mogelpackungsliste: [markendetektive.de](https://www.markendetektive.de/2026/11/04/mogelpackung-oktober-2026-rewe-lorenz-lenor/)

Five new cases show hidden price increases of 5.6% to 33.3%, including two private labels (Rewe, Netto). An analysis of 470 listed cases finds store-brand shrinkflation runs about as deep (around 20%) as brand-name shrinkflation, which cuts against the idea that private labels protect value-seeking shoppers. The most interesting case runs the other way: Lenor softener grew from 1,239 to 1,491 ml, but its unit price rose 10.6%. This "upsizing with a markup" exploits the bigger-pack-is-cheaper heuristic and has been studied far less than downsizing.

## 5. JetBlue raises bag fees using a 2x2 grid of peak/off-peak and advance/airport prices

**Source:** Upgraded Points: [upgradedpoints.com](https://upgradedpoints.com/news/jetblue-checked-bag-fee-increase-november-2026/)

For tickets bought from Nov 3, the first checked bag costs $50 to $70 depending on travel date and on whether it is paid in advance or at the airport. The second bag costs $70 to $85. The advance price works as a framed discount ("save up to $10") on top of a $11 increase. The interesting part is how temporal price discrimination and partitioned pricing are layered on the ancillary rather than the fare. It raises the question of whether consumers fold these conditional fees into their mental total when comparing fares across airlines.

## 6. Grocery price opacity: an essay on algorithmic pricing after Walmart's pledge to "price the product, not the person"

**Source:** Salon: [salon.com](https://www.salon.com/2026/11/02/the-strange-new-math-behind-the-price-of-your-groceries/)

The essay connects Walmart's September pledge, its rollout of electronic shelf labels and the Instacart price-test episode. It argues that the core harm is losing the ability to understand why you pay what you pay, rather than any proven price surge. It is careful to note that the Instacart tests used random assignment, not personal profiling. That distinction matters for research, because consumers may react to random price dispersion with the same unfairness judgments they would apply to targeted pricing. The firm's pledge itself is now a positioning claim that could be tested.

## 7. The EU's Digital Fairness Act is expected in mid-November, and one lawyer argues most of what it targets is already illegal

**Source:** Lawgent: [lawgent.se](https://lawgent.se/the-digital-fairness-act-arrives-in-november-most-of-what-it-targets-is-already-unlawful/)

Citing an Oct 5 European Parliament briefing, the piece expects the Commission's proposal around mid-November. Its argument is that the Act responds to weak enforcement rather than missing rules. Personalised pricing stays legal with disclosure, and the real concern is personalisation that exploits identified vulnerabilities. The only clearly new obligation it sees is making cancellation as easy as signup. For behavioral pricing scholars, the coming text matters because "exploiting vulnerabilities" will need an operational definition, and that is a question consumer psychology can help answer.
