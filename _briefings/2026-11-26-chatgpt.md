## Key takeaways

- Check the period behind an advertised monthly equivalent. It need not match the renewal cycle.
- Treat cancellation of the old subscription as part of an upgrade's design.
- Separate participation, intended payment and actual payment when evaluating voluntary pricing.

## What happened

### 1. Dropbox divides its annual purchase price by fifteen
**Source:** [Dropbox plans, undated live promotion ending December 1, checked November 26, 2026](https://www.dropbox.com/plans).

Dropbox's promotion gives new subscriptions three extra months. Its footnote says the displayed monthly price is the annual purchase price divided by fifteen. After those fifteen months, renewal is every twelve months at the annual price shown at checkout. This is an extension of service, not monthly billing at the displayed rate. Calculated over the initial period, fifteen months for the price of twelve means 20% less per service month, not 25% off. My interpretation: the denominator helps sell the offer. Show the amount due now, service end date and renewal interval together.

**Tags:** "SaaS pricing", "Price presentation", "Price Promotions".

### 2. A lifetime upgrade does not automatically end the annual subscription
**Source:** [Perttu Lähteenlahti, RevenueCat, November 18, 2026; campaign window November 25–December 1](https://www.revenuecat.com/blog/engineering/run-a-black-friday-sale-for-your-app).

The developer's Netli.fyi campaign offers new customers a $12.49 first year, renewing at $24.99. Existing subscribers are offered lifetime access for $49.99 instead of $99.99. The important operational detail: buying lifetime does not cancel the annual subscription. The guide tells developers to explain this and provide a subscription-management button. Customers could reasonably interpret lifetime as replacing recurring payment. That expectation is my inference, not a measured complaint outcome. This is a documented campaign plan, not reported sales results. Test whether buyers understand both the new entitlement and the action needed to stop renewal.

**Tags:** "subscription pricing", "Subscription Renewals", "Price Promotions".

### 3. Baltimore's ticket-pricing suit includes a quantity condition
**Source:** [Music Business Worldwide, November 25, 2026](https://www.musicbusinessworldwide.com/city-of-baltimore-sues-vivid-seats-over-alleged-bait-and-switch-ticket-pricing/).

Baltimore filed suit against Vivid Seats on November 23. Beyond allegations about added fees, the city alleges that some per-ticket prices required buying at least two tickets, with the minimum disclosed late. Vivid Seats denies the allegations and says it has displayed all-in pricing nationwide since May 2025. These are competing assertions, not adjudicated findings. The city's linked release timed out during verification. The pricing issue deserves attention regardless of the outcome: an inclusive per-unit price can still conceal a minimum commitment. Audit the advertised quantity as well as the advertised amount.

**Tags:** "Sports ticket pricing", "Price transparency", "Price Bundling".

### 4. A museum's payment flexibility is available at the counter, not online
**Sources:** [Museum of the City of New York admission terms](https://www.mcny.org/visit); [Kids Art Studio ticket terms](https://www.mcny.org/kids-art-studio). Undated current pages, checked November 26, 2026.

Current-practice discovery, not a newly announced change. MCNY lists pay-what-you-can admission for New York City residents. Its program page says online ticket buyers agree to full admission, while pay-what-you-can is available only at ticket counters. Free Wednesday tickets are also counter-only under the admission terms. My interpretation: the channel becomes part of the price. Asking a person for a flexible payment may feel different from choosing an amount privately online. The pages do not establish embarrassment, deterrence or revenue effects. Test those possibilities rather than assuming that nominal eligibility produces equal access.

**Tags:** "Price discrimination", "Consumer decision making", "Fairness".

### 5. Voluntary pricing has three decisions, not one
**Source:** [Bihari, Awasthi and Sharma, Marketing Intelligence & Planning, September 11, 2026](https://doi.org/10.1108/MIP-12-2025-0939).

Older discovery. Four studies distinguish adopting pay-what-you-want, deciding willingness to pay and making the payment. The abstract reports that external reference prices did not affect adoption in study 1, while higher reference prices raised willingness to pay for nonprofit sellers in study 2. Study 3 reports that descriptive norms increased actual payments when payment was publicly visible. I read the publisher's abstract, not the full methods. Sample sizes and effect sizes remain unchecked. The useful mechanism is stage-specific influence: a cue that changes intended payment need not attract more participants or increase receipts.

**Tags:** "Academic research", "Social processes", "Consumer decision making".

## Why it matters

My synthesis: customers buy a commitment, not just a number. Its duration, replacement rules, quantity and purchase channel all deserve explicit treatment.
