## 1. EU's Digital Fairness Act lands today with a pricing-specific blacklist (Euronews; CEA Digital Law)

**Source:** [euronews.com](https://www.euronews.com/2026/11/09/eu-to-blacklist-ten-manipulative-online-design-traps-leaked-draft-shows) · [ceadigilaw.org](https://ceadigilaw.org/en/blog/digital-fairness-act-addictive-design-pricing/)

The Commission presents its proposal today. Per the leaked draft, it bans checkout-only drip fees, bans was/now discounts computed against a manufacturer's recommended price (only real competitor prices could serve as the reference), requires in-game currencies to show their real-money value, and requires sellers using dynamic pricing to disclose how many units remain at the advertised price. For reference-price research, this is a rare case of a regulator deciding which anchors count as legitimate. The stock-disclosure rule is an untested scarcity cue: will consumers read it as transparency, or as pressure? Germany has already objected, and application is unlikely before 2028.

## 2. PlayStation Store quietly shows different prices by account (Push Square)

**Source:** [pushsquare.com](https://www.pushsquare.com/news/2026/11/ps5-dynamic-pricing-impacts-new-ps-store-black-friday-offers-roughly-usd5-variations-in-prices)

During Sony's Black Friday sale, store listings for first-party titles such as Astro Bot and Death Stranding 2 were labeled as having account-dependent prices, with spreads of about $5 (for example $33.59 vs. $39.59). Sony hasn't commented. This is personalized pricing in a closed ecosystem where buyers can easily compare screenshots, so it works as a live test of how quickly peer comparison turns a modest price gap into a fairness grievance. Commenters said they would buy less, and that boycott intent is worth checking against actual behavior.

## 3. Starlink makes a monthly subscription price float with local congestion (TeslaNorth, summarizing PCMag reporting)

**Source:** [teslanorth.com](https://teslanorth.com/2026/11/08/starlink-price-hikes-spread-us-backlash/)

After a late-October test in Kansas, Missouri and Nebraska, Starlink is emailing existing residential customers in more states that their price can change as local capacity tightens. Reported increases are 10–20% (e.g., $130 to $156/month), while new customers in some big cities get 30–50% off. Surge logic usually applies to one-off purchases, so applying it to an ongoing contract after installation is unusual. It runs against the reference price set at signup and hits a captive rural base, and broadband experts quoted by PCMag said they had never seen an ISP price this way.

## 4. FTC's surveillance-pricing 6(b) orders: sample design and what's being asked (Arnold & Porter; WBTV)

**Source:** [arnoldporter.com](https://www.arnoldporter.com/en/perspectives/blogs/consumer-products-and-retail-navigator/2026/11/the-ftc-ramps-up-its-pricing-related-inquiries) · [wbtv.com](https://www.wbtv.com/2026/11/06/ftc-did-not-request-surveillance-pricing-information-charlottes-largest-air-carrier/)

The Oct. 28 orders to nine airline, ride-share and delivery firms (Delta, United and JetBlue among the airlines) ask how loyalty status and algorithmic analysis of personal data feed individual prices, and what inputs and tools are used. WBTV reports the FTC chose a representative sample per industry rather than surveying everyone. Two things for researchers: loyalty-tier pricing is explicitly in scope, which blurs the line between rewarding customers and discriminating among them, and the eventual 6(b) report could become a rare source of firm-side data on how prices are personalized.

## 5. Theory paper: AI shopping assistants can soften price competition and leave some well-served consumers worse off (arXiv)

**Source:** [arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09399)

Guo, Xu and Zhang (posted Nov. 6) model platforms whose AI assistants shortlist products for consumers. Wider access does not necessarily cut prices: a mid-quality firm may stop serving low-valuation buyers, and prices can rise for some product pairs. They also describe an "alignment paradox": the assistant ranks accurately, but salience nudges some consumers to a cheaper, lower-quality option, and the quality loss outweighs the savings. A useful companion is Buecheler, Berbeglia and Guo's price-equivalent audit (arXiv 2610.25843, Oct. 19; [arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.25843)). It converts the effect of seller cues such as social proof on 7 LLM shoppers into dollar premiums across 113,472 trials, which gives a usable metric for agent susceptibility to cues.

## 6. BNPL makes splurges easier to justify to a spouse, in the spouse's eyes too (CU Boulder Today)

**Source:** [colorado.edu](https://www.colorado.edu/today/2026/10/28/buy-now-pay-later-could-make-your-splurge-easier-justify-your-spouse)

Amozegar, Lynch and Barasch (under review at Journal of Retailing) find that married consumers expect $100–$400 splurges to seem cheaper, easier to justify and less conflict-prone when paid with BNPL rather than a credit card. The non-buying spouse shows the same bias, so this isn't only the buyer rationalizing. It adds an interpersonal layer to pain-of-paying and payment-decoupling accounts: installments seem to work as a social justification device inside the household budget, not only as a liquidity tool. In the exploratory survey, 52% of frequent BNPL users said they used it partly to reduce spousal conflict. (Released Oct. 28, slightly outside the usual window, but novel.)

## 7. A 3% restaurant "admin fee" goes viral and diners respond by withholding tips (WFMD/Fox News)

**Source:** [wfmd.com](https://wfmd.com/2026/11/09/restaurant-admin-fees-spark-backlash-as-diners-question-what-theyre-paying-for)

An 83-cent, 3% admin fee on a Denver Earls receipt prompted a Reddit pile-on, with many saying they would offset such fees by tipping less. A Florida operator argued that fees appearing only on the bill feel like a gotcha and depress tips. Florida now requires pre-order disclosure, and a Duffy's location was cited and dropped its fee. This is partitioned pricing with a feedback loop: consumers treat the surcharge and the tip as one fungible "extra" mental account, so the fee may partly cannibalize staff income rather than add to the restaurant's revenue.

## 8. Small hotel audit: resort fees now appear upfront, but the member rate shown first anchors low (Everything-PR Research)

**Source:** [everything-pr.com](https://everything-pr.com/hotel-price-transparency-audit)

A purposive Nov. 10 audit of 12 U.S. fee-charging hotels found all 11 priced views included mandatory fees in the headline price, as the FTC rule requires. Only 2 itemized the fee next to the rate, though. Checkout totals ran a median 16% above nightly rate times nights, mostly from taxes, and every Hyatt page led with a member rate about 4% below the public rate. The sample is small (10 of 12 on one booking platform), but it suggests where the anchoring has moved after the rule: from the hidden fee to taxes and to member-rate framing, both still legal.

**Type:** Practice · **Tags:** Price transparency, Partitioned Pricing, Regulation, Membership, Hospitality, Resort pricing, Price presentation

## 9. Ticketing: resale caps don't touch primary-market price shock, and Australia hands out "Shonky" awards (TicketNews; Michael West Media)

**Source:** [ticketnews.com](https://www.ticketnews.com/2026/11/noah-kahan-presale-prices-resale-caps-2027/) · [michaelwest.com.au](https://michaelwest.com.au/ticketing-giants-dealt-booby-prize-for-dynamic-pricing/)

Noah Kahan, who backs a Massachusetts cap on resale at 110% of face value, saw fans balk at $300–$500+ presale tickets (one checkout showed $405.50 plus $81.10 in fees). A promoter pointed out that fans are priced out before resale even enters the picture. In Australia, consumer group Choice gave Ticketek and Ticketmaster Shonky Awards for dynamic and "in demand" pricing, citing seats about $50 dearer than near-identical seats one row away. Ticketmaster's defence, that prices don't rise just because more fans join the queue, separates demand-based pricing from surge pricing in a way consumers likely won't perceive.

**Type:** News · **Tags:** Dynamic pricing, concert ticket pricing, Affordability

## 10. Car makers keep charging subscriptions for hardware buyers already own, and willingness to pay is falling (Fortune)

**Source:** [fortune.com](https://fortune.com/2026/11/04/car-subscriptions-automakers-drivers-pushback/)

With average new-car payments at a record $787/month, automakers keep adding subscriptions for built-in features (VW's horsepower unlock, Subaru remote start). S&P Global Mobility data show stated willingness to pay for connected-car services fell from 86% to 68% in a year. Drivers interviewed separate paying for new capability from paying to unlock hardware they already bought. That distinction lines up with entitlement- and ownership-based fairness theories, and few studies have tested it directly.
