## 1. Sony's PS Store appears to show different Black Friday prices to different accounts — Push Square

**Source:** [pushsquare.com](https://www.pushsquare.com/news/2026/11/ps5-dynamic-pricing-impacts-new-ps-store-black-friday-offers-roughly-usd5-variations-in-prices)

Deal trackers posted screenshots of first-party games (Astro Bot, Death Stranding 2) listed at two prices about $5 apart, with the store labeling the price as account-dependent. Readers reported discount depths of 30% versus 60% on the same title across accounts. Sony has said nothing. This is account-level price discrimination presented through the discount itself, so the "sale" frame and the reference price differ by person. It is a clean natural setting for studying how people react when they find out a friend got a deeper deal.

## 2. Starlink starts congestion-based pricing, and the first increase lands days after sign-up — TeslaNorth (summarizing PCMag)

**Source:** [teslanorth.com](https://teslanorth.com/2026/11/08/starlink-price-hikes-spread-us-backlash/)

Starlink told subscribers in several states that prices will move with local network demand. Residential increases of 10–20% were reported (one plan went from $130 to $156 a month), while some new customers in big cities were offered 30–50% off. The company's notice justifies the increase with a capacity/market rationale. That links to Damavandi, Antia and Kopalle's forthcoming Journal of Marketing field experiment, which found market justifications cut churn by about 30% while cost and quality justifications did nothing ([haslam.utk.edu](https://haslam.utk.edu/news/ut-haslam-researcher-finds-messaging-matters-in-price-increase-notifications/)). The open question is whether that still holds when the "market" is a monopoly in rural areas and customers see it as exploitation.

## 3. Theory paper: AI shopping assistants can make prices fall and some consumers still end up worse off — arXiv (Guo, Zhang & Xu)

**Source:** [arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09399)

Posted November 6. The model has salience-distorted shoppers and compares an AI that recommends (the consumer still chooses) with one that buys for the consumer (delegation). Whether assistance lowers or raises prices depends on which quality tiers the platform carries. The authors also find an "alignment paradox": even with the best option recommended and cheaper, salience can push some buyers to an inferior product. Companion empirical work: Wadi & Ma (arXiv 2609.28372) find LLM shopping agents fall for just-below prices and promotional framing only when getting information costs something and the goal is vague, so the weakness depends on how the storefront is designed.

**Type:** Research · **Tags:** Academic research, AI in Pricing, Price Promotions, Tiered Pricing, Price presentation, Consumer welfare, Consumer decision making

## 4. Lab experiment: small dashboard nudges make people's pricing algorithms more collusive — arXiv (Hillenbrand, Normann, Potarca & Werner)

**Source:** [arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.26861)

About 1,000 Prolific participants built rule-based pricing algorithms that competed in a Bertrand duopoly. A warning about price wars raised prices; adding a one-click tit-for-tat preset raised them the most. LLM advice on its own did nothing. Reasoning-enabled LLMs that designed algorithms on their own reached similarly high prices. Posted in late September, so it is outside the usual window, but it matters for behavioral pricing: tool vendors' choice architecture, not just the algorithm, may drive tacit collusion.

**Type:** Research · **Tags:** Academic research, Algorithmic Pricing, AI in Pricing, Collusion, price increases, Consumer decision making

## 5. What the FTC's personalized-pricing 6(b) study asks the airlines — Skift

**Source:** [skift.com](https://skift.com/2026/11/04/what-the-ftc-wants-to-know-about-how-airlines-set-fares/)

Delta, United and JetBlue are among the nine companies ordered to report how they set fares. Holland & Knight's summary ([hklaw.com](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/11/ftcs-section-6b-orders-on-personalized-pricing-and-pay-what-businesses)) says the orders request A/B tests, segmentation schemes, vendor tools and disclosure screenshots going back to 2021–23, and also cover how gig platforms set worker pay. Delta denies any individualized pricing despite AI pricing via Fetcherr, and the line it draws between "dynamic" and "personalized" is the distinction consumers find hardest to see. Responses are due mid-December, and if any of the underlying A/B evidence becomes public it would be unusually rich data.

## 6. Map of price-transparency enforcement: Toast, auto dealer fees, healthcare, plus Michigan's surveillance-pricing bills — Kirkland & Ellis; Manchester Mirror

**Source:** [kirkland.com](https://www.kirkland.com/publications/kirkland-alert/2026/11/ftc-and-states-signal-price-transparency-is-a-top-priority) · [themanchestermirror.com](https://themanchestermirror.com/2026/11/02/bills-could-control-how-surveillance-pricing-impacts-what-you-pay-for-products/)

Kirkland's November 4 alert collects recent actions: the October 30 FTC/state suit against Toast over subscription misrepresentations to restaurants, a dealer-fee settlement, warning letters to 24 healthcare companies, and the August draft policy saying undisclosed personalized pricing may be deceptive where buyers expect a single price. Separately, Michigan House bills restricting surveillance pricing are now in the Senate. The retailers' association wants them to explicitly exempt discounts and supply/demand changes. Defining "personalized" in law is where the behavioral question of which price differences people see as fair becomes concrete.

## 7. German shrinkflation watch: private labels are shrinking too, and one "bigger bottle" raised the unit price — MarkenDetektive (from Verbraucherzentrale Hamburg data)

**Source:** [markendetektive.de](https://www.markendetektive.de/2026/11/04/mogelpackung-oktober-2026-rewe-lorenz-lenor/)

October's cases include Netto's private-label salad seasoning cut from 10g to 8g at the same price (a 25% hidden increase) and Ültje trail mix cut from 200g to 150g. Lenor fabric softener grew its bottle but raised the price more, so the per-ml price rose about 11%; this "upsizing" is the reverse of shrinkflation and is rarely studied. Across 470 entries on the list, about 1 in 9 are private labels, with typical hidden increases near 20%, which undercuts the idea that store brands are a refuge from shrinkflation.

## 8. A 3% "admin fee" on a Denver burger, and Florida's new disclosure rule for restaurant operations charges — WFMD / Fox News

**Source:** [wfmd.com](https://www.wfmd.com/2026/11/09/restaurant-admin-fees-spark-backlash-as-diners-question-what-theyre-paying-for/)

A Reddit receipt showing an 83-cent admin fee drew a large reaction for its size. Florida now requires restaurants to disclose the amount and purpose of such fees before ordering, and a state inspection cited one chain because the fee was on the menu but did not appear correctly on the receipt (the chain then dropped the fee). The outrage is far out of proportion to the money, which supports the view that fee backlash is driven by fairness and unclear labeling, not cost.

## 9. An affiliate site pre-registered its Black Friday fake-discount study — WEM

**Source:** [wem3.ai](https://wem3.ai/press/methodology)

WEM (a commission-earning comparison site) pre-registered rules in August for judging UK Black Friday deals against 90-day price histories. Every product gets one of four verdicts (genuine cut, matches an earlier price, never moved, or insufficient data), with minimum data thresholds and a commitment to publish nothing if the sample falls short. Results are due November 10–17. Pre-registration by an interested commercial party is new, and the method is worth borrowing for academic studies of reference prices.

**Type:** Practice · **Tags:** AI in Pricing, Regulation, Price Promotions, Discounting, Behavioral Economics, price realization
