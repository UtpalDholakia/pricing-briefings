##### ***Summary.*** Why do some prices live for decades while others die within hours or even minutes? The answer has less to do with technology and more with what a price means to customers. Familiar, iconic prices become a core part of the customer’s relationship with a brand, meriting longer lives as their value grows. Changing them will fray relationships with customers and lose their price-image creating power, even when such a price is reduced. However, keeping the price unchanged while enshittifying the product will leave only a zombie price in place, devoid of meaning and potency.

#### **A pricing conundrum for the Pricing Conundrum**

When Amazon launched its Prime membership program in 2005, it had far fewer benefits than it does today, but it only cost \$79. Prime’s [price increased](https://www.cnbc.com/2022/02/03/amazon-increases-the-price-of-prime-nearly-17percent-to-139-per-year.html) to \$99 in 2014, to \$119 in 2018, and to \$139 in 2022, where it stands today. That’s an average life of approximately 5 years for the annual price of a Prime membership. But not all Amazon prices are this long-lived. In [one study that scraped prices daily](https://www.nber.org/papers/w25138) from websites of major US retailers between 2008 and 2017, the life of an average price decreased from 6.5 months to 3.7 months. A separate analysis in the same study found that Walmart’s online prices had shorter lives when the products were also available on Amazon. In [another study of five large anonymized online retailers](https://pubs.aeaweb.org/doi/pdfplus/10.1257/mic.20210158) from 2023 (so we’re not certain whether Amazon was included), more than one-third of the prices at the fastest price-changing retailer changed every day, and many changed every hour.

So the pricing conundrum is this: We have a retailer, like many others today, capable of changing its prices frequently. Yet one of its most important offerings (i.e., the Prime membership that forms the foundation for its retail pricing strategy) hasn’t changed in price for the past four years, while the prices of the assortments of products and services on its site change far more quickly. (Even in the assortment, there are many long-lived prices, many controlled by third-party sellers; for instance, I haven’t changed the price of my books listed on Amazon for [the past several years](https://www.amazon.com/How-Price-Effectively-Managers-Entrepreneurs-ebook/dp/B073PK2SC5/)). At Amazon, prices with long lifespans and many, many prices with short lives live harmoniously under one roof.

Furthermore, this variability in the duration of prices can be found across companies and often within the same industry. For instance, Netflix raised the price of its standard US plan from \$15.49 to \$17.99 in January 2025 and to \$19.99 [in March 2026](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/netflix-price-increase-2026-subscription-fees/), after only 14 months. Spotify kept its US premium price at \$9.99 for 12 years from 2011 until 2023, then [raised it thrice](https://www.musicbusinessworldwide.com/spotify-hikes-prices-for-premium-subscribers-in-the-us-again12345/) in 3 years. Planet Fitness [maintained its basic membership](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/planet-fitness-raises-membership-fee-for-first-time-since-1998/) fee at \$10 per month from 1998 to 2024, before raising it to \$15. Trader Joe’s [sold bananas](https://www.cnbc.com/2024/03/25/trader-joes-bananas-get-first-price-increase-in-more-than-20-years.html) for 19 cents for 23 years, from 2001 to 2024, and ChatGPT [hasn’t changed](https://a16z.com/llmflation-llm-inference-cost/) its \$20 per month Plus subscription price since its launch, even though its [cost per million tokens fell](https://thegtmnewsletter.substack.com/p/ai-token-price-collapse-costs-rising) from \$60 in 2021 to 6 cents by late 2024. At the other extreme, Uber’s [surge multiplier varies continuously](https://www.nber.org/papers/w22627) by time and location, so the same ride can have a different price from one minute to the next.

Why do some prices live on for decades, turning into wizened old numbers that everyone knows and uses and fights to keep alive, while others die almost as soon as they are born? What determines the life of a price? While acknowledging there’s enough research and discussion on this topic to fill a good-sized library, in this post I want to explore these questions in the space of a blog post, with one particular perspective: that of the customer. The main insight from this exploration is that it’s not technology, or even menu costs, that drive the frequency of price changes, but customer psychology and customer response (in a psychological, not just economic sense). In the rest of this piece, I will build out this idea.

#### **Understanding some nuances behind the price of a life**

A lot is known about how long prices last. Economists define the period during which a specific price remains unchanged as a “[price spell](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/abs/10.1086/422559?casa_token=BpYutqwBERkAAAAA:pJNQfkzfiU-vokItf3rggjnFI4omhaARsieJUFrEesaqs1LHf4FgxyETM6fRA1vFZ5aota1kwVNv4w).” Thus, if a coffee shop sets the price of a cortado on its menu at \$3.75, keeps it for 13 months before raising it to \$4, the \$3.75 price spell is 13 months. However, underlying this simple definition are some nuances that need to be factored in.

First, in considering the life of a price, we need clarity about which price we are talking about. For instance, consider the Noosa Blueberry Yogurt Cup shown in the image below at my local Kroger. Its regular price is \$2.49, but at the time of this writing, it is on sale for \$2.00 for the weekend. This sale is temporary, and the price will go back up to \$2.49, where it remains most of the time. Consistent with industry pricing terminology, Kroger calls the \$2.49 price for the Yogurt Cup its “Everyday Low Price.” Thus, the sale price may vary depending on whether deep or shallow promotions are offered by the store, with \$2.49 serving as the reference price. For our purposes, the life we are interested in is the \$2.49 price.

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/35469603-5d84-4843-bdff-3390049d9499_1802x1125.png)

Noosa yogurt sale at Kroger, with sale price and the Everyday Low Price.

In one study on what the authors called “[nominal rigidity](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.101.1.234),” prices at a large retailer changed approximately every two weeks, but the most common price within a quarter, referred to as the “reference price” in the study, had “an average duration of roughly a year.” Here, nominal rigidity refers to the fact that prices are stickier than they should be, i.e., they do not change quickly in response to changes in the factors that influence them. Thus, frequent price changes and a durable regular price can coexist for the same yogurt cup and for any other product or service.

Second, the logic behind the need to change the price is important in understanding the life of a price. For instance, a hotel’s unsold room for tonight is inventory that is about to perish forever. A rideshare company has to navigate the rapidly shifting balance between available drivers and customers seeking rides. A cruise ship operator has a clock ticking over its head, counting down to the moment the ship leaves the port. These situations impel frequent price changes and favor prices with short lives that must adjust to dynamic and interrelated occupancy, revenue, and profit goals. Inflation is another motivator that has been particularly salient in recent years. In [one study by the Federal Reserve](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/notes/feds-notes/price-setting-during-the-covid-era-20230829.html), as shown in the figure below, the median frequency of regular price changes across the product categories studied rose to about 20% in early 2022, almost double the rate before the Covid pandemic, before declining as inflation eased.

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/03280f93-bdc0-4c33-8e97-499c99dd0133_1200x979.png)

Source: Price setting during the Covid era, FEDS notes, August 29, 2023

The interesting question of what prevents a seller from responding to relevant changes in pricing input factors by changing the price in tandem has been asked over and over again, and the answers have varied with the context. For instance, back in the days when prices were physically changed by a retailer by manually affixing price labels to each item in the store, the costs of changing prices were formidable and naturally suppressed the urge to change prices willy-nilly. Innovating business processes, such as tagging shelves rather than individual items, reduced these menu costs, but they remained substantial. Now, with electronic shelf labels and numerous other technologies, menu costs have declined to the point where it makes economic and operational sense for retailers, B2B distributors, restaurants, and many other industries to follow the lead of hotels, cruise ships, and rideshare companies. Yet, we find that in many industries, prices still have remarkably long lives, as the Prime and Planet Fitness examples illustrated.

In an [in-depth qualitative study conducted at an industrial manufacturer](https://assets.csom.umn.edu/assets/71545.pdf), researchers found that the managerial costs of adjusting prices (defined as costs associated with information gathering, decision making, and communication) were 6X menu costs (the costs of changing prices), and the customer costs (the costs of communicating the new prices to customers and negotiating with them) were 20X menu costs. Note that the customer costs considered in this study were the ones incurred by the seller in dealing with customers, not by the customers in dealing with the price change.

The key insight from this research generalizes to virtually every setting, even if the proportions may be different in any given situation. When changing a price (and shortening its life), the seller faces many more costs than just the cost of changing the price, and these costs are often complex, occur over a long, indefinite period, and are very difficult, if not impossible, to measure. And then of course, there are the costs which this study did not consider, the costs that shorter-lived prices impose on customers.

#### **Customer relationships and the life of a price**

Long-lived prices strengthen relationships with customers through various mechanisms, and conversely, short-lived prices hinder them. Consider a loyal Trader Joe’s customer who shops there regularly. Over the years, he has learned that the price of a banana at Trader Joe’s is 19 cents, and it has become a strong reference price in his mind. This price has become part of his grocery-shopping budget, and, even more significantly, it shapes his expectations for other prices at Trader Joe’s by [influencing the retailer’s price image](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/pricing-concept-the-salve-framework-51a). Trader Joe’s may not have promised to keep the price unchanged, but with the signage saying, “17 years and running” (in the image below), it feels like it will remain unchanged for the foreseeable future. The price’s long life bodes well for the customer’s relationship with the brand. But when the banana’s price changes to 23¢, as it did in 2024, it feels like a huge betrayal. Far more than the impact on how many fewer bananas customers will purchase because of the 4¢ price increase, the bigger issue is how this change of an iconic price will affect the loyal customer’s relationship with Trader Joe’s. And the answer is, not in a good way.

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/38908232-24c7-4e77-81f3-34c41998d094_1672x941.png)

Trader Joe’s in-store banana display with price shown prominently and its iconic status acknowledged.

Now imagine the same situation, but for much higher prices. Say, in a B2B context, the price of a particular service has remained unchanged for the past four years. Now, regardless of the reason, as soon as the customer learns that the price is increasing, they will begin to deliberate. Is this service worth keeping? Are there other options available that could serve us just as well for a lower price? Do we really use this service to make it worth this cost? In many such decision processes, affordability or the customer’s willingness to pay is less of an issue, and questions of value, incremental cost, and potential alternatives come into focus where they didn’t before, becoming more important.

The reason for the price change also affects how customers respond to the change. I’ve written before about the [classic price fairness perceptions study](https://web.mit.edu/curhan/www/docs/Articles/15341_Readings/Justice/Kahneman.pdf) where respondents were asked to what extent they believed price increases were fair when the reason was that the price increase was a consequence of higher cost, versus a price increase that sought to capitalize on stronger demand. To no one’s surprise, demand-based price increases were deemed to be unfair, even when the explanation was transparently articulated, while cost-driven increases were seen as less unfair. The main lesson here is not about which justifications should be used when changing price, but rather that every price change levies psychological costs on customers because they not only have to understand the seller’s logic behind the price change, but they have to consider how the price change will impact them and their relationship with the seller going forward.

Making this even more complicated, prices don’t just have to go up to alienate customers. Sometimes they can even go down and make customers unhappy. Take the case of a retailer where the customer buys the product at one price, and then next week, notices that the price has gone down, and others who are buying this product are paying a substantially lower price. Such customers [actually disengage](https://academic.oup.com/qje/article-abstract/125/2/729/1882204) from the retailer afterward, making fewer purchases. A solution would be to [offer a price match](https://customerservice.costco.com/app/answers/answer_view/a_id/628/~/price-match---costco.com-orders), as large retailers like Costco do, but most businesses can’t afford to do this. This implies that *any* price change requires caution, even a discount won’t make everyone happy, and may actually fray some customer relationships.

The main insight from this discussion is that prices live not just in the retailer’s databases and signs, but also in the customer’s memory and in their spreadsheets and shopping lists. Just because a retailer kills a price by changing it doesn’t mean that it stops living in the customer’s mind and records. The old price becomes the customer’s basis for judging the new one, and often, for judging the seller and assessing the state of one’s relationship with the seller. Deciding what the life of a price should be thus requires the seller to look back as much as it requires them to look forward.

#### **Just because a price can be changed doesn’t mean it should be changed**

Over the past twenty years or so, wave after wave of new technology has made it very easy and very cheap to change prices. One of the latest iterations is the adoption of electronic shelf labels, also known as ESLs, by retailers such as supermarkets and mass merchandisers, which, in theory, allow retailers to change prices multiple times a day in their physical stores as Amazon does on its website. However, all the evidence thus far suggests this is not the case.

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/458862b1-ffc8-47df-8eb5-0d593473762e_1151x1367.png)

Electronic Shelf Label in a Retail Store, Source: Wikimedia Commons.

One [study of ESL adoption from back in 2015](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3007095) found that it led to an approximately 21% increase in the frequency of price changes. Although this sounds like a lot, the baseline was low. Specifically, retailers in the study changed prices on 3.3% to 4.6% of their products in a median week before ESL adoption. After adoption, they changed prices for approximately 4.0-5.6% of their products each week. Even more interesting, most of these price changes were price reductions not increases. Post-adoption, the stores made approximately 31% more price cuts, while price increases did not increase significantly. Most of these price changes were small and targeted, leading to increased sales volume and gross margin increases of about 9%. The gains came mostly from things like perishable categories such as fresh produce, consistent with store managers marking down these products in a timely fashion, given the flexibility afforded by ESLs. Thus, instead of going unsold, some of these items were purchased by value-seeking shoppers at lower prices.

A [more recent and much larger study](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5271491) examined a US grocery chain with about \$3 billion in annual revenue that installed ESLs in all of its more than 100 stores between September 2022 and October 2024. In this case, ESL adoption had virtually no impact. Prices changed on approximately 4.31% of the products before ESL adoption, and this number remained virtually unchanged after adoption. There was no “surge pricing” defined by the authors as a price increase lasting a week or less before being reversed before or after adoption for a period of nearly three years. Unlike the older study, the number of price cuts, markdowns, clearance sales, and short-term discounts all remained unchanged. The authors suggested that, given the dependence of supermarkets on regular grocery shopping behavior by loyal customers, varying prices too frequently, even if allowed by technology, didn’t make sense to store managers. Similarly, on the supplier side, factors such as supplier agreements, promotional schedules, and operational limitations temper the frequency of price increases. What works for hotels, cruise ships, and rideshare companies doesn’t work for supermarkets and mass merchandisers, even when technology affords quick and cheap price changes.

One important point should be noted here. In the retail industry, there are plenty of short-lived prices. For example, many supermarkets have weekend sales, such as the Kroger 3-day sale shown below, “manager’s special” markdowns at the end of the day for a quick sale, and so on. These promotions are traditional and have been used for decades, well before dynamic pricing and surge pricing were known to managers or customers. The key difference here is that the short lives of these promotional prices have a clear, coherent narrative behind them. In the case of weekend sales, customers fully understand they are a ruse by the grocery store to get them to visit on the weekend, so it makes sense that they are limited to the weekend. Similarly, manager’s specials are understood as a way to get rid of produce nearing its expiration date, so they are available only when a short expiration period remains. The problem arises when the life of a price doesn’t make intuitive sense to the customer.

#### **The shaky, enshittified quality beneath a stable, long-lived price**

When dealing with prices, especially long-lived ones, customers have to consider and deal with the rampant enshittification of the underlying product. By enshittification, I mean the significant and clearly visible degradation of a product’s quality (another term used for this is [shitflation](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/shitflation), but in my opinion, enshittification is a better fit). A price may have a long life, but the underlying offering it represents is so degraded that it is unrecognizable compared to its original. We can see this in Amazon’s Prime membership itself. I observed earlier that its price hasn’t changed in four years, but a key benefit, its streaming service, has been thoroughly enshittified. Specifically, in January 2023, Amazon injected advertisements into Prime content, while simultaneously offering an ad-free tier for an additional \$2.99 per month (or \$36 per year). Thus, the \$139 annual price still lived, but the product that it stood for now had a new, higher price of \$175 per year. Amazon has continued to increase the ad-free tier price, so that as of this writing, it is \$4.99 per month (or \$60 per year). Thus, the original Prime now technically costs \$199, while the enshittified Prime still remains available at its iconic price of \$139. \$139 is dead, long live \$139. Such vigorous enshittification is evident in numerous consumer packaged goods, such as Q-tips (see below), [Hungry-man frozen dinners](https://www.4029tv.com/article/rossen-reports-skimpflation-consumer/41411336), [Smart Balance butter spread](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/skimpflation-inflation-reducing-food-service-quality/), and the list goes on.

Of course, in some cases, the change can also work in the customer’s favor, such as when a software subscription adds new features while keeping the rate constant. One obvious example is AI products where we’ve seen tremendous improvements and additional features (e.g., image generation, video generation) for the same price. This discussion means that it rarely makes sense to look at how long-lived a price is without considering whether and how the underlying product or service has changed over this life. A long-lived price may look like it’s alive when the quality underneath has deteriorated so much that only a zombie price remains, devoid of meaning or value.

#### **The life of a price should match up with customer perceptions and the price’s practical utility**

The core insight I’ve tried to develop throughout this piece is that the decision about the life of a price is, and if not, should be, impelled by the customer, not by the affordances of available technologies, and not by some obstinate, old-school executive or business owner who wants to keep the price unchanged, no matter the repercussions. Some prices are remembered by customers and even embraced, and their stickiness is a boon to maintaining customer relationships. All the examples I started out with, such as the Prime’s \$139 price, Planet Fitness’s \$10 monthly membership fee, and the 19-cent Trader Joe’s bananas, are or were iconic prices in the sense that they became part of customers’ understanding of these brands and their relationship with them. These prices earned their long lives even when they stopped making economic sense, and the brands held out as long as they could, and resorted to sneaky tactics to keep these prices alive (in the case of Amazon).

Most other prices, especially those found in the yield management world, are not so iconic. Customers don’t have a strong reference price for what a Marriott hotel room or a Carnival cruise should cost. Still other prices are encountered when needed (for instance, buying a replacement phone charger or a pepper shaker on Amazon) and then forgotten just as quickly, leaving no impression at all in the customer’s mind. These prices don’t need to have long lives, because they have no anchor in the customer’s memory or in their understanding of their relationship with the seller.

This insight provides us with a satisfying resolution to the Amazon conundrum about why prices with long lifespans and many, many prices with short lives live harmoniously under one roof at Amazon. The prices that Amazon changes many times a day are like ripples on a lake for Amazon’s customers; they come and go mostly unnoticed, making little impact or leaving a lasting impression. They are discovered by customers when needed, used for evaluation, comparison and decision making during a particular shopping journey, and then quickly forgotten. The Prime subscription price, in contrast, is completely different in every sense. It is iconic, it stands for Amazon’s brand image, as a hybrid retailer that offers excellent value and that encourages customer relationships. Customers notice this price, remember it, and react if and when it changes, disproportionately to its actual impact on their budget or on the extent to which it becomes less affordable. This price matters, and that’s why it needs to live as long as possible.

A second related insight is that as a price lives longer, it becomes more valuable to both the brand and the customer, which in turn extends its life. The 19-cent price of a banana was more valuable to the Trader Joe’s brand in its 23^(rd) year than it was in its 3^(rd) year, and the brand had to abandon this price with reluctance when it simply became untenable. In fact, it felt compelled to [explain the need for the 4-cent change](https://apnews.com/article/trader-joes-banana-prices-inflation-72b6d2313cf26206c25db390542a9930) to mainstream media outlets through a spokesperson:

“ We recently changed the price of a single banana to 23¢, which still represents a tremendous everyday value for bananas…We only change our prices when our costs change, and after holding our price for bananas at 19¢ each for more than two decades, we’ve now reached a point where this change is necessary.”

I could not find any information in the public domain about the retailer’s internal decision regarding whether it considered the trade-offs between raising the price to 23 cents and keeping it at 19 cents as a loss-leader for longer, and, if it did, what criteria it used to compare these options. Regardless, the fact remains that there is a clear and compelling trade-off to consider whenever an iconic price is involved. Planet Fitness faced the very same decision of raising its iconic \$10 per month membership and made a different decision. It [cushioned the psychological blow](https://www.scrippsnews.com/business/company-news/planet-fitness-raises-membership-price-for-first-time-in-more-than-25-years) by applying the higher \$15 price only to new members, while allowing existing members to keep paying the \$10 price as long as they kept their membership. The calculus of grandfathering existing customers indefinitely suggests that they placed a high value on the iconic price and were willing to trade off (highly profitable) incremental revenue in order to still keep the \$10 price alive. Here again, we see the same dynamic at play: the iconic price delivers far more benefits to the brand than simply the revenue from its realization. And to the extent it does, it keeps living.
