###### *Summary.* In a [September 2023 post titled “The Dark Side of Costco’s Pricing Strategy](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/the-dark-side-of-costcos-pricing), I argued that Costco’s two-part pricing structure, an annual membership fee combined with a tightly limited markup policy, results in an unavoidable degradation of the customers’ experience, especially at latter stages in their shopping journeys. Because the membership fee generates so much of Costco’s profit, the retailer must police who is permitted to shop in its stores, with frontline employees tasked with the unpleasant job of enforcement. Three years on, Costco has moved membership verification to much earlier in the customer’s shopping journey, from the checkout line to the warehouse entrance. In this piece, I argue that this change has made membership verification more visible and universal, making the potential downsides for customers less onerous. This logic confirms my original assertion that every business must scrutinize the components of its pricing model that are critical revenue and profit generators and pay careful attention to managing them and minimizing their aversive effects on customers, employees, and others. Costco provides an excellent case study of intentionally reducing the friction created by the core profit generator to its customers, while making it an even more effective and potent source of generating revenue and profit.

### **Introduction**

One time several years ago, I was shopping at Costco (as I often do). I had a full cart of various items, and when I got to the front of the checkout line, I realized I had forgotten my membership card. Unlike at another grocery store like Kroger, where the cashier nonchalantly uses their own loyalty card or asks someone else in line to use theirs so the customer can avail of promotions, the Costco cashier was very firm. I had to leave my cart, go to the membership desk, have my status verified, get a temporary card, then come back and join the line to complete the purchase. It took an extra hour for this entire process, just because I forgot my card and didn’t want to abandon my cart due to the sunk costs of my time and effort.

I described this experience to articulate what I called “[The Dark Side of Costco’s pricing strategy](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/the-dark-side-of-costcos-pricing).” My main point in that post was that with a two-part pricing approach (or what I called a “[membership-markup pricing model](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/the-dark-side-of-costcos-pricing?utm_source=publication-search)”), Costco self-imposes a [markup limit](https://www.foodandwine.com/how-costco-keeps-prices-low-11970443), allowing shoppers to buy items at very low prices (in many cases, roughly half of grocery store prices) and earn a large share of its operating profit through its membership fees. Since it makes most of its money from memberships, Costco must do everything it can to enforce the principle that “you can only shop here if you have a membership.” This includes limiting each membership to a total of two people, preventing shoppers from sharing their membership with extended family, encouraging renewals, and persuading regular members (who pay \$65 annually) to upgrade to an executive membership (\$130). Employees are tasked with enforcing these policies, no matter how much they negatively affect the legitimate member experience, as my own story showed. In my [2023 piece, I argued](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/the-dark-side-of-costcos-pricing?):

“Because Costco depends so heavily on membership revenue for profit, its price realization has a lot to do with managing membership sales, ensuring that it keeps signing on more paying members anyway it can.** *****It must build a wall to keep non-members out while concurrently offering them a gate to let them in and become active dues-paying members.*** Instead of unit sales or sales revenue, Costco’s primary profitability metrics are the size of its membership base and its churn rate. Costco is more similar in its price execution objectives to Netflix than it is to Walmart.

This is where it gets interesting, and we get to the main point behind the dark side of Costco’s pricing strategy: For Costco, managing memberships to realize price necessarily requires making some hard and unpleasant customer experience decisions. It means policing, denying, requiring hoop-jumping, being rude, and cracking down ruthlessly and repeatedly, all to keep non-members out, encourage non-members to sign on, and get current members to renew. None of these goals is possible without injecting a significant degree of unpleasantness into the shopping experience of members and the working experience of frontline employees.”

When I wrote the piece in 2023 and described my negative customer experience, a Costco cashier checked my membership card when I was checking out. Technically, [anyone could walk into a Costco and shop](https://www.reddit.com/r/Costco/comments/1b32itp/someone_asked_to_use_my_membership_card_at_check/), and then hope another shopper’s goodwill (by letting them use their card) during checkout would let them buy the groceries. But since then, Costco has installed digital card scanners at the entrances of its stores, which are staffed by employees. This process moves membership verification from the end of the shopping trip to the beginning and ensures that non-members can’t even enter the store easily.

This development provides an opportunity to explore and refine my original thesis about the inherent tension between the revenue- and profit-generating components of any price structure and the customer’s natural inclination to circumvent, bypass, or somehow break the constraints they impose. My refined argument, which I want to develop in this piece, is this: the zero-sum game between the seller and the customer I described in 2023 is still part and parcel of every pricing model, but the size of the loss imposed on customers can be managed. Members enjoy substantial savings (indeed, that is why Costco is a beloved retailer), suppliers can gain access to a large base of enthusiastic shoppers, and employees are among the most highly compensated in retail. The conflict arises when the company must police the boundary around its value-delivery mechanism to ensure that its profit-generating mechanism is not damaged. Costco does this by ensuring that only those who have paid the first part of the price (i.e., the membership fee) are allowed to enter the store and avail of its high-value product and service assortment. Introducing card scanners at the entrance helps make this boundary concrete and visible, reduces the likelihood of futile shopping journeys by its genuine members, and eliminates an adversarial, high-stress encounter that used to occur much later in the journey.

### **Costco’s tightly integrated price structure**

Costco’s membership-markup pricing model is a prototypical two-part price structure, where the first part is an annual membership fee for access to Costco, and the second part is the price of purchases made through its stores and digital channels. The membership-markup link follows from the fact that markup size is determined squarely by the membership fee, and the company’s profitability depends on both pricing model components. In addition to these two basic pieces, several other elements of Costco’s pricing model together form a tightly integrated and self-reinforcing system, as illustrated in the figure below.

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/f0ab3fc1-f4c1-4012-b740-233709cba176_1672x941.png)

Costco’s pricing model is a tightly integrated and self-reinforcing system

As of this writing, the annual membership fee is \$65 for a Gold Star (i.e., regular) or Business membership and \$130 for an Executive membership. The higher fee for Executive members comes with a 2% reward on purchases, which is calculated annually and sent to customers as a voucher. When it comes to product pricing strategy, Costco’s gross margin is significantly lower than that of its competitors. Its 14% ceiling on branded-item markups and 15% ceiling on Kirkland Signature markups are perhaps the most publicly known and recognized markup factors in all of retailing. Notably, to my knowledge, while these values do not appear as formal commitments or promises in Costco’s SEC filings, the principle behind them does, as Costco expressly articulates its [pricing strategy as follows](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/909832/000090983225000157/costannualreport2025.pdf):

“We operate membership warehouses and e-commerce websites based on the concept that offering low prices on a limited selection of nationally-branded and private-label products in a wide range of categories will produce high sales volumes and rapid inventory turnover. When combined with the operating efficiencies achieved by volume purchasing, efficient distribution and reduced handling of merchandise in no-frills, self-service warehouse facilities, these volumes and turnover enable us to operate profitably at significantly lower gross margins (net sales less merchandise costs) than most other retailers. We often sell inventory before we are required to pay for it, even while taking advantage of early payment discounts.”

As shown in the figure above, the power of Costco’s price structure stems from the reinforcing loop that connects its two parts. After they’ve paid the membership fee, customers have an incentive to shop at Costco to receive greater value from the membership. Their greater shopping frequency and high spend per visit generate high sales volume, which, combined with limited product selection, rapid inventory turnover, purchasing scale, and austere operating model, lowers Costco’s purchasing and operating costs. Lower costs and restrained markups generate attractive prices that are significantly lower than alternatives, thereby strengthening the value of being a Costco member and encouraging customers to renew, which in turn sustains this virtuous cycle.

The individual pricing practices shown in the figure support this so-called price realization system. Costco passes on the fruits of its cost reductions to customers, uses distinctive price endings to communicate markdowns and inventory status (which in turn becomes a valued price vocabulary of customers), offers highly visible loss-leaders (rotisserie chicken, hot dog-and-soda combo, pizzas), and maintains significantly lower prices on products that shoppers can compare with other retailers. Together, these practices reinforce Costco members’ beliefs that the company will protect their interests even when they are not paying attention to specific prices. Thus, the different elements of the price structure work together to produce Costco’s broadly appealing value proposition.

### **The significance of membership fees in the two-part pricing model**

Costco’s recent results suggest a need to examine how the two parts of the price structure interact in a more nuanced way. In my original piece, I argued that the membership fees are the primary driver of the retailer’s profitability. However, that is no longer the case; while membership fees remain indispensable, Costco is becoming less dependent on them to drive overall profitability. But first, let’s take a look at Costco’s situation compared with those of Walmart and Target, and see whether things are different now than in 2023. The top panel of the next table is from the 2023 piece, while the bottom panel shows the same values from the most recent quarterly earnings reports.

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/f879223a-bc4c-479e-99c1-cf6ee33b47eb_1043x880.png)

Reliance on membership revenue for Costco, Walmart, and Target, 2023 and 2026. Source: Costco, Walmart, & Target quarterly earnings announcements.

In 2023, I concluded that “Costco is able to sustain a significantly low gross margin because it supplements revenue from memberships. The table also confirms that Walmart and Target practice a traditional (to retail) form of markup pricing with substantially higher markups. Costco practices a different membership-markup pricing strategy that relies heavily on memberships.” As the table shows and as I discuss below, the reliance on membership revenue has decreased quite a bit for Costco (from 62.2% in 2023 to 48.8% in 2026), but the use of fundamentally different pricing models—membership-markup for Costco vs. traditional retail markup for Walmart and Target still applies.

In the most recent quarter, Costco had 82.9 million members, and the renewal rate was 92.2% (for context, 80% is considered an excellent retention rate in a retail bank, and 70% is over-achieving for a streaming service). From 2023 through 2025, considering full-year data, Costco’s revenue from membership fees increased by approximately 16%, from \$4.58 billion to \$5.32 billion. In contrast, operating income increased by approximately 28%, from \$8.11 billion to \$10.38 billion. Seen another way, membership fees were equivalent to 56.4% of operating income in fiscal 2023, but 51.3% in fiscal 2025 (and 48.8% in the most recent quarter as shown in the table).

Note that not all membership fees directly contribute to Costco’s operating income; the costs of acquiring and serving members (which Costco does not disclose) need to be subtracted. Nevertheless, it is clear that Costco increased its profitability from selling goods and services rather than from memberships during this period. This is also corroborated by the fact that Costco’s gross margin increased from 10.57% in 2023 to 11.12% in 2025. 55 basis points may not sound like a lot, but on \$270 billion of sales, it translates to nearly \$1.5 billion. Thus, since 2023, Costco has strengthened both parts of its two-part pricing model. It added members, collecting higher-margin membership revenue, and also earned more from selling goods and services.

### **The Costco member shopping journey now begins with membership verification**

As mentioned earlier, the big change from 2023 to 2026 is the shift in membership verification to much earlier in the customer journey. Previously, a mandatory part of the Costco member’s shopping journey was to present proof of membership at checkout. Membership verification occurred after much of the shopping journey was over, when the consumer had walked through the huge store and gone through the effort of searching, evaluating, succumbing to buying impulses, etc., to fill their shopping cart. The member showed a card (physical or digital) to the cashier or queue handler, and it was checked visually.

After testing in a handful of stores in early 2024, Costco rolled out digital scanners at store entrances throughout its chain later that year. Members now scan a barcode or QR code from their membership card. The attendant monitoring the scanner confirms the member’s identity with a visual check of the photograph and flags inactive or expired memberships, directing these shoppers to the membership desk before their shopping journey begins. Those who use someone else’s card are also flagged in the same way, mitigating (but not eliminating, see below) the unpleasantness from shaming those who share memberships. Now they are simply not allowed to enter the store, also reducing their likelihood of trying this scheme in the future.

It is worth noting that Costco’s executives justified the introduction of digital scanners on the grounds of throughput and labor management rather than customer experience. For instance, Richard Galanti, then Costco’s CFO, said in January 2024 that the scanner “helps speed up the process. When you go through checkout, particularly self-checkout, there’s not someone there having to review the card again.” CEO Ron Vachris told analysts in a September 2024 earnings call that the devices had “delivered on the goal of speeding up the checkout process,” and that they gave store managers “real-time traffic counts throughout the day, so we’re able to adjust front-end lines that we need to open and close.” On the revenue side, Costco’s claim has been modest. Vachris reported “a slight lift in sign-ups and renewals.” But analysts have been more bullish. In October 2024, Morgan Stanley’s Simeon Gutman reported that warehouses with scanners showed low-double-digit percentage increases in membership counts and estimated that the most likely scenario was approximately 4 million additional members in North America converting from non-paying shoppers. Whether Costco set out to improve price execution or arrived there in the pursuit of optimizing throughput, its current membership verification approach is better matched to its price structure than the previous verification at checkout.

The most important consequence of moving verification from the checkout to the entrance lies in the opportunities it offers to realize price by managing customer relationships before they turn sour. Someone with an expired membership can be directed to the membership desk to renew it, then proceed to shop. Someone trying to use another person’s membership can be denied entry and also directed to sign up for a membership without going through the shopping process and filling the cart, which a Costco employee would then have to empty. The entrance verification is more effective because it can distinguish between honest customer issues and deliberate attempts to exploit the retailer, and identify effective fixes for both types of customers. The next figure shows a commonly asked question on the r/Costco subreddit and an employee’s response explaining why the company checks membership at the entrance.

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/7f5ba8a6-d51a-48e8-a1ca-f525ab290251_1008x720.png)

Thus, this change offers an interesting lesson about price structures. The feature of Costco’s pricing model that generates significant revenue and profit for it need not be maximally inconvenient to customers. The company can enforce its restriction in many ways, some of which may be less painful and inconvenient (e.g., entrance verification) than others (e.g., checkout verification). Thus, understanding the customer’s journey and its interactions with the company’s touchpoints is useful for adjusting the enforcement of price features in the price structure that generate revenue and profit.

### **Digital verification at the store entrance pinpoints the location of potential conflict**

The digital scanners at the entrances to Costco stores have become the focal point of potential conflict between consumers trying to enter the store and frontline employees. Routine verification is now largely automated and very quick, taking a few seconds per member. The member scans their card, the system beeps and confirms their membership, and the employee simply stands and views the screen. When things do go wrong, such as when the person’s membership has expired, the person scanning the card looks nothing like the photograph on the card, or when a member forgets to bring the card, they can quickly be denied entry and sent to the membership desk, minimizing the number of uncomfortable interactions.

During the pilot in early 2024, a few members complained about having to wait in queues outside the warehouse to get in, usually when the store opened. Some members also didn’t like having to show their card again at checkout. Beyond such minor issues however, when conflict escalates, such as when a denied individual refuses to leave, or engages in abusive behavior, the entrance is where the conflict is likely to happen with one frontline employee monitoring the entrance (who may receive additional training to deal with such incidents), rather than one of a bank of cashiers, each having to deal with denials much later in the journey with a shopper who has invested far more time and effort into their store visit. Thus, the main point I made in the [2023 piece still applies](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/the-dark-side-of-costcos-pricing?):

“Costco *only* wins (in the sense of earning a profit or preventing a profit leak) when the customer loses (by paying for the membership and using it in a restrictive way). Conversely, the customer only wins (for example, by buying products without a membership) when Costco loses (by profit leakage). This is the main lesson that we can learn from this case study on the dark side of Costco’s pricing strategy. In the end, every pricing strategy, no matter how thoughtfully, ethically, and cleverly constructed, must culminate in a zero-sum game being played out between the company’s agents and its constituents, in this case, its customers, employees, and suppliers. The truth is that in pricing, zero-sum games abound, and they are essential for the sustainability of pricing models.”

However, what the scanner innovation teaches us is that the zero-sum game need not involve large losses; losses can be tempered by even greater creativity and cleverness in configuring the customer’s experience, while maintaining a large win. While Costco will always have to verify the membership of anyone who enters its stores, disallow membership sharing, and refuse non-members, it can make the verification process as painless, efficient, and customer-journey-friendly as possible.

### **The broader lesson for price execution**

Pricing strategy discussions often revolve around how much to charge and how to charge the customer. But implicit in every pricing model is also the issue of boundaries, such as who qualifies, what the customer receives for the payment, what is excluded, and what happens when the customer doesn’t follow the rules. These issues are often considered questions of operations or customer experience design. But as this case study illustrated, they are central to the success of the company’s pricing model and should be treated as a pricing strategy issue as well.

Costco’s use of digital scanners to verify membership at the entrance suggests a general principle that applies beyond retailing, to every business. When a customer restriction or qualification has economic significance, the customer should be able to verify that they have satisfied it before exerting too much effort, and the business should transparently report that such verification has been conducted and provide clear-cut remedies to the extent available. Waiting until the customer has expended considerable time, effort, and money before signaling disqualification, or providing ambiguous signals, is detrimental not only to the customer but also to the company’s reputation. This principle applies beyond warehouse clubs to such things as subscription sharing, eligibility for discounts or rewards, usage limits, privileges to return purchased goods and receive refunds, and other cases where customer behavior and outcomes are linked to a condition set by a pricing model.

Had the digital scanner been present at the entrance of the Costco store when I forgot my membership card, I would have discovered my oversight right away. I would still have had to go to the membership desk to shop that day, and that would have been inconvenient. But I would have felt more empowered with more choices, such as coming back another day or going to another store instead. And I certainly wouldn’t have had to wait in the long checkout line twice during the same visit. Costco would still have enforced its membership verification rule, but it would have inflicted far less pain and suffering on me.

My main point from the 2023 post, that there is an unavoidable tension between realizing price and inflicting pain on customers, still holds. But the amount of pain inflicted is not fixed. An empathetic understanding of the customer and a willingness to improve the customer journey can substantially reduce pain while maintaining the price structure’s profit-generating power. The dark side of membership pricing (or any other pricing model, for that matter) can become considerably brighter.
