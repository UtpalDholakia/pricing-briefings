##### ***Summary.*** Strikethrough price labels are among retailing’s most visible promotional and price-communication devices, and they have generated substantial class action litigation over the past decade. In this post, I explore why labels such as “Originally,” “Regularly,” “Compare at,” and “MSRP” on strikethrough price labels carry distinct legal meanings under FTC and state laws, even though they often lead shoppers to make similar inferences, that this product usually costs more, and I am getting a deal. I also examine how to credibly demonstrate what consumers actually take away from these retailer claims.

A shopper is browsing in the outlet store of the fashion brand Autumn Harvest when she sees a maroon sweater with two prices displayed on the price label[^1]. The \$120 price has been struck through (see below). The sweater’s current price is \$60, also known as its “[active price](https://www.marwickmarketing.co.uk/blog/google-product-price-markup-updates-for-retailers/)“ in Google’s terminology for online retailers. She experiences a sharp thrill that shoppers often feel when they’ve found a good deal and decides then and there to purchase the sweater. If we asked this shopper later what these two prices on the price label meant, she would probably tell us that the sweater used to cost \$120, or that it normally costs \$120 somewhere, and that she is paying half of that, or \$60 today.

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/cee6885d-dc0e-4bf4-88c5-a65b97b08c0a_1566x1004.png)

\$60 sweater with a \$120 strikethrough price label at the Autumn Harvest outlet store

The retailer’s employees, on the other hand, would have a very different answer to the same question. They would describe the \$120 struck-through price, also known as a former-price presentation, as a “compare at” reference price, that is, a benchmark price based on what similar products sell for in posh department stores. If probed further, they will carefully point out that the \$120 value is not a representation of what this particular sweater ever sold for, anywhere, at any time, but an internal benchmark used for accounting purposes.

This inconsistency in interpretation on the two sides is the crux of the tension that has led to numerous lawsuits over the past decade or so. Specifically, strikethrough pricing, also known as a former-price presentation or former-price comparison in legal discussions, is among the most visible promotional devices in retail, but it has also been one of the most litigated retail pricing issues[^2] in the form of class action lawsuits and enforcement actions by state attorney general offices involving [fictitious reference pricing](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/three-theories-about-target-kohls).

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/28cf2a6a-1b04-4536-8b9d-1ad5239870be_1122x1402.png)

Example of a strikethrough price label

Many retail store managers and pricing decision makers in the retail space view the strikethrough price label as a normal part of the brand’s price vocabulary[^3], alongside things like [charm prices](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/why-are-prices-ending-in-the-number?), sale signs, [specific price endings](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/why-are-prices-ending-in-the-number?), “limited time” color tags, and so on. In this sense, the [price vocabulary](https://www.leadershipreview.net/why-you-need-a-price-vocabulary/) has marketing, accounting, and operations purposes. Many consumers, on the other hand, read the price label strictly as a claim made by the retailer about the product’s price and value.

In this post, I want to consider why the strikethrough price label in its various forms generates more-or-less homogeneous consumer inferences, why the specific claims retailers attach to the struck-through price often fail to differentiate those inferences in shoppers’ heads (although from the retailer’s point of view as well as the legal perspective, they are or should be seen as distinct), and what kinds of evidence can credibly establish what shoppers actually take away from their exposure to a strikethrough price label.

#### Why a strikethrough price label functions as a retailer claim

Consider the price label in the figure above. On this price label, the line through the \$120 price serves as the basis for comparison in the shopper’s mind. It tells the shopper that two prices for the product exist: \$120 and \$60, with the current \$60 price (obviously) significantly lower. This dual price presentation also implies a relationship between the two numbers, and it is the consumer’s job to figure out that relationship to make sense of the current price. Having been exposed to such price tags over decades of shopping experience (at least in the US), people have become proficient at making quick judgments when they encounter them.

In consumer psychology terminology, the strikethrough price label generates a readily available external reference price for the shopper, which has been supplied by the retailer. Furthermore, we can distinguish between *internal reference prices*, which are shoppers’ expectations of the price for a particular product based on their prior knowledge and purchasing experiences, and *external reference prices*, which are provided by sellers[^4]. There is a large body of research over the last 50+ years on these two types of reference prices. Broadly speaking, it shows that when the retailer supplies a reference price, it materially influences the consumer’s assessment of the product in many ways, including their perceptions of its value, and affects the individual’s willingness to pay and their purchase intentions[^5].

Regarding external reference prices specifically, the research suggests that adding an external reference price to a price label or a verbal offer (during price negotiations) reliably increases consumers’ perceptions of savings and their estimates of the product’s perceived value, even when the reference price is implausibly high. Research from the comparative price advertising literature also reaches the same broad conclusion. Interestingly, even exaggerated external reference prices (strikethrough price labels are often examples of this), which consumers might intuitively distrust, still raise consumers’ estimates of the product’s actual value[^6].

To illustrate this, consider a shopper who comes across a coffee maker tagged “Regularly \$899, Now \$129” at Target. She may well roll her eyes at the \$899 reference price and even suspect it’s a suspicious price no one has ever paid for this machine. Even with this conscious skepticism, the research suggests that when asked later on what the coffee maker is actually worth, her estimate will be meaningfully higher than that of a comparable shopper who saw the same machine at a flat \$129 price without the \$899 strikethrough price. Thus, the strikethrough reference price will still apply to the shopper, even if she has consciously devalued it or even rejected it entirely[^7].

Returning to our sweater, given the sheer cognitive effort and time required for shopping activities, it is unlikely that most shoppers will verify whether the \$120 price is plausible while browsing in the store. Especially when things like brand cachet (the product brand and the store brand), perceived quality of materials (e.g., this looks like cashmere), etc., are factored in, the bias will favor the strikethrough price. Furthermore, and quite likely, the strikethrough price is processed quickly, the inference is made, and the shopper moves on (with or without making the purchase). Skepticism does not entirely protect the shopper.

The shopper’s inferences are also likely to extend beyond potential savings to the product’s market value. When she sees a \$120 strikethrough price label on the sweater, for example, the shopper will update her estimate of the sweater’s market value and of how much she is saving by buying at the active price[^8]. She may now believe, incorrectly, that this is a \$120 sweater, not a \$60 sweater, and that updated belief about the product’s value will persist past the moment in the store to the extent that the \$120 strikethrough price has made an impression. This price has become the benchmark against which all active prices are judged.

#### The subtle but significant differences in strikethrough label retailer claims

Given the recent history of litigation in this area, many retail pricing decision makers (who may often be store managers or corporate employees) are well aware that the price label they attach to their merchandise matters legally. The [FTC’s Guides Against Deceptive Pricing](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-233) have been in force since 1964 and still apply today[^9]. Furthermore, [California’s False Advertising Law](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?sectionNum=17500&lawCode=BPC), its [Consumer Legal Remedies Act](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displayText.xhtml?lawCode=CIV&division=3.&title=1.5.&part=4.&chapter=3.&article=), and other similar state statutes follow the same logic, with some variations, such as stricter timing requirements in some jurisdictions for what counts as a permissible “former price[^10].”

For strikethrough price labels, the nuances are important but are often overlooked. These laws treat different words and phrases on strikethrough price labels differently, assigning them different meanings. For instance, an “Originally” or “Was” price claim refers to the retailer’s own prior price, a “Suggested Retail Price” refers to the manufacturer’s suggestion of what the price should be, a “Compare at” refers to a price available somewhere else, and so on.

In the FTC’s Guides Against Deceptive Pricing, for example, a former-price claim is permitted only when the prior price was a genuine, bona fide price at which the article was openly and actively offered to the public on a regular basis for a reasonably substantial period in the recent, regular course of business, a manufacturer’s suggested retail price is permitted only when the product is regularly sold at that price in the relevant trade area, and a comparison to another retailer’s price requires that the comparison price be one at which the product is actually being offered within a reasonable proximity[^11]. Furthermore, if the price label or surrounding context implies that the former price was a price at which substantial sales actually occurred, the retailer should be prepared to substantiate that stronger consumer inference.

The legal taxonomy is precise because each label points to a different factual basis: the retailer’s own former price, another retailer’s price, a comparable-value benchmark, or a manufacturer’s suggested price. But whether shoppers actually perceive these distinctions similarly is an empirical question, and the extant consumer psychology evidence suggests that many do not. Let’s consider this point in greater depth by looking at five variations of the strikethrough price label, separately assessing the retailer’s and the shopper’s sides for each label. The table below summarizes this discussion.

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/a0e538ea-2b82-4c2b-b684-e7d9deb5b5ec_1122x1402.png)

An “Originally” tagged strikethrough price label

***1) “Originally” or “Was.”*** This label suggests that the earlier offer price (now struck through) was the retailer’s original price of \$120, and it has now been marked down. Under the FTC’s Guides Against Deceptive Pricing, a “Was” or “Originally” claim before the strikethrough price is treated as a former-price representation, and the seller must substantiate that the product was openly and actively offered at the higher price for a reasonably substantial period in the recent past. Some states impose more specific timing rules, including California’s three-month look-back period[^12]. The consumer inference, however, is often broader than the legal definition. Shoppers tend to infer not only that the product was offered at \$120 but that it was actually sold to other shoppers at that price, an inference the label itself does not strictly support[^13]. They may also infer that the sale is temporary and that the price will return to a higher level in the future, which can generate a sense of urgency to buy the product immediately[^14].

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/7ff27582-0d27-4415-a0e3-09f8d7ff6a84_1121x1403.png)

A “Regularly” tagged strikethrough price label

***2) “Regular” price or “Regularly”.*** This label is meant to anchor the shopper’s price evaluation to the retailer’s normal, non-promotional price by downplaying the temporal aspect. Under the FTC’s Guides and most state false advertising statutes, a “Regular” price claim is treated as a form of former-price representation, requiring that the advertised regular price be a price at which the retailer has actually offered the product on a regular basis for a reasonably substantial period. The same California three-month look-back rule that governs the “Originally” claim applies here as well[^15]. The difficulty is that for many retailers, particularly in the off-price and outlet channels, the “regular” or “regularly” claim lacks a referent that a typical shopper would recognize or even know, because the product almost never sells at the regular struck-through price for any meaningful stretch of time[^16]. From the shopper’s point of view, the inference is essentially the same as for the “originally” claim: the struck-through price is what the product normally costs, and I am getting a deal on it[^17]. Here too, whether the price marked as the regular price is the actual market price for this specific product or an internal designation made by the retailer is a distinction the consumer has no easy way to make, let alone the practical ability to verify it.

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/ce2e765e-4909-4951-b118-569d6be9fbbc_849x1222.png)

A “Compare to” tagged strikethrough price label

***3) “Compare at” or “Compare to.”*** This is the vaguest of the five types of strikethrough price labels considered here, except perhaps the unlabeled version. What is the struck-through price being compared to? Who is or was selling this comparable product? Where? When? A “Compare at” or “Compare to” claim can work differently depending on context. It may suggest that the same item is being offered by other retailers at the stated price, or that comparable merchandise of the same grade and quality is offered at that price. Either way, the retailer needs evidence for the comparison it has invited the consumer to make. Under the FTC’s Guides Against Deceptive Pricing, the “compare at” label is treated as a comparable-value reference, that is, a price at which goods of “like grade and quality” are being offered by other retailers in the trade area, not the retailer’s own former price[^18]. From a consumer perception perspective, however, this distinction is largely irrelevant. Research on semantic price cues has found that shoppers tend to interpret the higher number as a baseline market value, often assuming it represents the product’s typical price at some other retailer or, alternatively, the same retailer’s prior price. Put differently, the careful legal distinction made between “what this seller charged” and “what some other seller might charge” is missing from the shopper’s reasoning.

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/9f866774-bdd0-41b0-9f95-b02942d5609b_1122x1402.png)

A “Suggested Retail Price” tagged strikethrough price label

***4) “MSRP” or “Suggested Retail Price.”*** There is authority embedded in this variation of the strikethrough price label because it appears to be based on a price supplied by the manufacturer (presumably based on pricing inputs such as costs). The FTC’s Guides Against Deceptive Pricing recognize that many consumers believe a manufacturer’s suggested retail price is the price at which an article is generally sold[^19]. In categories where the product rarely, if ever, sells at the MSRP, including such categories as consumer electronics, mattresses, and apparel, the MSRP is closer to marketer-generated fiction than to a real selling price. As discussed earlier, research has found that suggested prices function as anchors regardless of whether the suggestion is informative about the product’s actual market value[^20]. In cases where few or no sales occur at the MSRP, the label can mislead consumers who infer that the MSRP reflects a real prevailing marketplace price. The persuasive force of the claim comes precisely from the appearance that the reference price originated outside the retailer.

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/13df8569-5ce4-4640-a410-200ffecb5dd4_1122x1402.png)

An unlabeled strikethrough price label

***5) Unlabeled strikethrough price.*** This is the most interesting category of strikethrough price labels, because no claim is made, and the shopper must use their judgment to make sense of the struck-through price. A common assessment attribution is “what this used to cost” or “what this normally costs[^21].” The consumer does not pause to consider whether the struck-through price on the label represents a manufacturer’s suggested price, a competitor’s price, the retailer’s previous price, or some aspirational benchmark. In a vacuum, the shopper will supply the explanation that seems most plausible, and which also typically happens to be the one most favorable to the retailer’s promotional message. The law also takes the view that an unlabeled or generically referenced strikethrough price label is treated as making essentially the same claim as a labeled former-price comparison, and that the same evidentiary requirements apply[^22].

The pattern across these five variants of strikethrough price labeling is consistent. The FTC Guides and parallel state statutes treat each price label as a distinct claim with its own evidentiary requirement, though there is some overlap. In contrast, the typical shopper treats them as broadly interchangeable signals from the retailer that the product is usually more expensive and is currently available at a significantly lower price. It is therefore a deal. In a nutshell, the specificity of these labels matters a lot to lawyers and regulators (and, by implication, to thoughtful retailers) but little to shoppers. Most fictitious-pricing disputes arise because of this mismatch.

#### The “internal benchmark” retailer defense

Retailer pricing decision makers often defend a strikethrough price by characterizing it as something used for internal benchmarking or communication purposes rather than as an external claim about what the product is worth. These purposes include serving as an internal margin benchmark or aspirational target (for revenue or margin), a record of the manufacturer’s suggested price displayed without expectation that it will be followed, or a placeholder used for internal merchandising calculations. The argument is that the strikethrough price was for internal reference and for the retailer’s employees; consumers should not have inferred anything from the price label.

In some cases, this internally focused account may be sincere. Retailers may genuinely use the struck-through price as a merchandising benchmark, planning input, system field, or margin reference. However, sincerity about internal meaning does not control the consumer-facing claim created when the number appears on a price label. This is a real way in which many retailers think about pricing. A furniture retailer, for instance, may set a “regular” price and put it on the price label because it reflects the price the company believes the product would command if it were to be sold through a department store rather than its own discount channel. The retailer never expects to sell the product at that price themselves. Internally, the number could also function as a margin reference, a buyer’s planning benchmark, or simply a number the merchandising system requires in a “regular price” field. From the retailer’s perspective, the price is real in an internal accounting sense, even if it has no marketplace existence.

However, once the consumer’s interpretation of these price labels is taken into account, three problems arise for retailers with this defense. The first, perhaps most significant issue is that consumer protection statutes are generally calibrated to consumers’ perceptions rather than to retailers’ intent[^23]. Even a sincerely held internal meaning does not take away the fact that many consumers were deceived by the information on the strikethrough price label. What’s more, consumers don’t have any insight into what the company is thinking.

The second challenge is that the disclaimer explaining the retailer’s intent in placing the information on the price label is never printed on the price label. Specifically, an authentic, complete version of the strikethrough price label would have to say something like, “This is the price we hoped to charge but never actually did.” Instead, a struck-through price is presented, followed by the active price, along with some qualifier like “Regularly” or “Compare to,” further muddying the waters. Third, once the inquiry shifts to the shopper’s perceptions, the defense is no longer relevant. It becomes an empirical question about what shoppers understood the reference price to mean. This question can be answered through a consumer survey[^24].

The Spann v. JCPenney litigation in the Central District of California provides an excellent illustration of how the internal-benchmark defense plays out when the discovery record is opened. The plaintiff’s complaint cited internal pricing documents indicating that only 0.2 percent of products were ever sold at the regular price during the relevant period, and that 98.7 percent of the company’s revenues came from products offered at a discount of 30 percent or more. JCPenney’s then CEO had been recorded at an investor event stating that “nothing was bought at regular price[^25].” The court certified the class, and the matter eventually settled for \$50 million. The internal-benchmark framing, in other words, might be a sincere description of how the retailer arrived at the number and why it chose to put it on the price label. However, it virtually never correlates with what the shopper takes away from the price label.

#### What did the shoppers really understand?

The discussion thus far raises an important practical question: If the shopper’s inference from exposure to a price label is legally relevant, how can we credibly establish what shoppers actually understood from a strikethrough price label? There is a well-developed body of practice for answering this question, much of it reflected in the Federal Judicial Center’s Reference Manual on Scientific Evidence, particularly its discussion of survey research[^26]. Five especially relevant principles from this guidance are briefly discussed here.

##### 1) Define the right population for the survey

The survey should begin with selecting the appropriate population. In a strikethrough pricing case, the relevant population might be actual purchasers of the product, recent shoppers at the store, likely purchasers, outlet-store shoppers, online visitors, or some other group, depending on the claim(s) being tested. This choice is important because the survey’s purpose is to measure what *relevant shoppers* were likely to infer from the specific price label in a specific shopping context.

##### 2) Use realistic stimuli of strikethrough price labels

The survey should provide respondents with the actual price label used by the retailer and an image of the product page from the website or app for an online retailer, in as much detail as possible. An abstract verbal description usually does not cut it. This is because the shopper’s inference from a strikethrough price label is heavily context-dependent and sensitive to factors such as the font used, the position of the words and numbers, the surrounding promotional claim (if relevant), and the product category. To the extent that the shopping context is stripped away in the survey, the results become harder to extrapolate to actual shoppers.

##### 3) Ask about the shopper’s inferences and evaluations of the stimulus, not a legal assessment.

The survey should focus on asking respondents what they perceived, understood, or inferred from the strikethrough price label, without biasing or forcing them. As an example, a question like “what did this tag tell you about the price of the sweater?” or “if you had bought this sweater at \$60, what would you have understood about its normal price?” is far more effective and valid than a question like “do you think this price tag was deceptive?” The first asks the shopper what they perceived, whereas the second asks them to render a legal verdict, which is beyond the purview of a survey respondent[^27].

##### 4) Use a control, where feasible

Questions about what shoppers infer from a strikethrough price label are often best addressed through an experimental design. A well-designed survey randomly assigns respondents to a treatment condition, in which the strikethrough price label is shown, and a control condition, in which the strikethrough label is absent or replaced with a neutral price presentation, while holding other stimulus and shopping context features constant. The difference in responses between the two conditions can then be attributed to the strikethrough price label. The choice of control is also a significant survey design decision. A no-strikethrough control tests the effect of the strikethrough price label, whereas a differently worded reference-price control can test the specific inferences made from specific labels such as “Originally,” “Regularly,” “Compare at,” or “MSRP.”

##### 5) Augment survey data with marketplace evidence

A survey can show what shoppers took away from the label, but marketplace evidence helps establish whether that inference had a factual basis. For example, if the strikethrough price is “Regularly \$120” on a price label, relevant questions about (1) the fraction of the SKU that sold at the \$120 price, (2) the distribution of realized prices in each channel, and (3) how long the SKU was offered at that price are worth answering. Finding this information can provide evidence of whether the strikethrough price had any factual basis in the marketplace. If the answer is close to “never,” the strikethrough price was a hypothetical number on a tag, not a price that applied to shoppers’ actual purchases. Marketplace evidence and shopper survey evidence reinforce each other and together form the strongest argument against retailer claims.

#### Implications for retail pricing decision makers and their advisors

The most direct implication for retail marketers and pricing decision makers is that when a particular form of strikethrough price label is used, whether explicitly or implicitly, a claim is being made about the product’s normal selling price, its market value, or both. As we’ve seen, the specific label (and therefore claim) attached to the strikethrough price (Originally, Regularly, Compare to, MSRP, or none at all) is generally indistinguishable to the consumer, but not to the laws that govern its use. Therefore, a pricing communication program built on assumptions like “Compare to” carries a meaningfully different meaning for shoppers than “Originally” does is shaky.

Promotional pricing design needs discipline. Before choosing and printing a strikethrough price on labels, retailers must answer an important question: at what price has this product actually sold, in which channels, and over what period? If the chosen strikethrough price has actually been, or is a significant sales price, the strikethrough price label is a powerful, legitimate way to communicate price and should be cheerfully employed. However, if the answers are negative, the retailer is making claims that shoppers are likely to read as factual statements, and strikethrough price labels will be hard to defend in any litigation that ensues.

For lawyers and expert witnesses working in this space, the core implication is methodological. The intuitions of witnesses from the retailer’s side about what shoppers “understood” a strikethrough price to mean are not meaningful. They are statements of the retailer’s intent, an entirely different issue. Persuasive evidence is likely to come from properly designed consumer surveys, often paired with marketplace data on realized prices for the products at issue, and informed by the substantial body of consumer psychology research accumulated over the past 50+ years on how reference prices anchor judgments of value.

Finally, for shoppers, the implication is somewhat pessimistic. The strikethrough price label is one of the most common and most effective promotional price communication devices in retailing. It works precisely because it capitalizes on commonly used cognitive heuristics that shoppers cannot easily turn off. The most reliable way to defend against undue influence from reference price cues is to ignore the strikethrough price entirely and ask whether the current or active price seems reasonable on its own (i.e., Is the sweater worth \$60?).

A strikethrough line through a price is easy to print, but it has a disproportionate influence on consumer decision making and buying behavior. For retailers, if not used carefully, this seemingly inexpensive price label will expose them to significant financial liability. The strikethrough price label needs to be treated with great caution by both retailers and shoppers.
