At every stage in the [customer’s decision journey](https://hbr.org/visual-library/2010/12/the-customer-decision-journey), price plays unique, specific, and predictable roles. Customers use available price information, price knowledge, and their perceptions and biases for specific and unique purposes to advance in their journeys. The upshot is that the effectiveness of pricing actions is stage-dependent. An effective pricing action for someone in the ‘Consider’ stage will fizzle or even backfire in the ‘Buy’ or the ‘Bond’ stages.

The good news for managers is that once they understand the distinct roles of pricing actions in their customers’ decision journeys, they can use it to enhance customer experience and influence and manage their behaviors throughout the journey. In this post, I want to explore the role of price and pricing actions at each stage in the journey. 

### **A (Very) Brief History of the Customer Decision Journey**

The Customer Decision Journey is a conceptual framework developed by McKinsey consultants in the early 2000s[^1]. It describes the stylized path taken by customers to learn about, purchase, and consume products and services. The path is cyclical, progressing from recognizing an unfulfilled need that motivates search and consideration of available alternatives. This leads to the purchase, followed by consumption (the ‘Enjoy’ stage). The customer also communicates their purchase experience and product evaluation with others through reviews and conversations and bonds with the company’s brands and products by becoming a loyal buyer[^2].

![](https://bucketeer-e05bbc84-baa3-437e-9518-adb32be77984.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/eda19348-6d14-4016-94ef-905e03fe2bd0_1892x1897.jpeg)

The Customer Decision Journey has its roots in the [Awareness-Interest-Desire-Action hierarchy of effects models](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/002224299906300103?casa_token=PdU3-1z3opIAAAAA:TRqcZEtttsRLreZlcUeREYdIWgzJVJkr9EJClytzGk6XmNfck6JXD9Qt5aih9j1zuf5TfWEXOKD-E3I) used by advertisers during the heyday of advertising in the 1950s and 1960s. It captures the evolution of the customer’s relationship with the brand, its products and services, and its employees and channel partners. The Customer Decision Journey identifies the specific points of contact between the company and the customer, known as “touch-points,” and the pathways of customers in navigating them. Touch-points are usually controlled by the company and are used to manage the customer’s experience in the journey.

### **The Behavioral Affordances of Price in the Customer Decision Journey**

Price supports distinct customer activities at each stage of the Customer Decision Journey. This is because customers have specific motivations and need to accomplish certain tasks at each stage before moving on to the next one.

The table below lists and defines each stage of the customer’s journey, describes how price is used by the customer, and lists relevant pricing actions taken by the company. (Note that in the table, ‘price’ is used to denote the actual price itself and the pricing structure within which the brand’s prices are located.)

### **Consider**

In the *Consider* stage, the customer assembles a small set of reasonable alternatives to investigate further. Customers often have budget constraints and use price to determine an alternative’s suitability for further consideration. An attractive and attractively presented price can also motivate the customer’s need for purchase and vice versa. Customers also use price as knowledge in this stage, either from prior experience or acquired just-in-time as they research and shortlist available choices.

To support the customer’s ‘Consider’ stage, the first order of business is to provide prices clearly. Where possible, the logic behind the price should be explained to give the customer reasons to [keep the brand in their consideration set](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0148296314000903?casa_token=Cxwvfudhef8AAAAA:tr44rUasOA07u_7Cww5RS-T4pEId7kv0hTYFNdRCUnWguMplM4aOpKo_qzbqmOVPGbCQakj-Ln0). Price cues such as [specific price-endings](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article-abstract/36/2/328/1942926), [signpost pricing](https://tlbnvault.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/01/Mind-your-pricing-cues.pdf), sale signs, and so on are also effective as the customer gathers information. And finally, novel pricing methods will generate excitement and pull the customer.

### Evaluate

In the *Evaluate* stage, the customer assesses a smaller number of alternatives in depth. Typically, no more than five or six, and as few as two choices are evaluated. The customer’s [task is to either pick](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article-abstract/43/4/614/2630530) the best option or weed out all inferior options until one remains. Either way, price helps screen out relatively inferior options ( e.g., “the price is too high”) or to home in on the best-value option. The customer also uses [price as a trade-off](https://www.emerald.com/insight/content/doi/10.1108/S1069-0964%282012%290000019012/full/html) against other product attributes or to compare different alternatives directly to one another.

Evaluation is influenced by setting the product’s price at a [popular price point](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/the-science-behind-behavior/201901/how-price-points-can-trick-us-making-purchase) within the product category that consumers consider to be the normal or accepted price for the product. For example, in the outerwear category, [popular price points of jackets](https://www.amazon.co.uk/Priced-Influence-Sell-Satisfy-Behavioral-ebook/dp/B07TCSWB8Z) are \$99, \$149, and \$199. When the brand has favorable prices, another method focuses on price [by facilitating direct comparisons](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/002224299806200204) with competitors’ prices. On the flip side, the manager may want to make it harder for the customer to process prices if they feel that competitors have lower prices or offer better value.

### Decide

Although the *Decide* stage is not formally part of McKinsey’s famous, or should I say infamous, model, I have considered it separately here because of its significance for pricing decisions. The *Decide* stage reflects the culmination of the customer’s evaluation process. The customer makes their choice and [shifts attention to when and where](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/bdm.408?casa_token=82XkQWhplKEAAAAA:Rv5nfmUABd9JI1IdaECwSEAng5DxPP5xmh5mxbmOpocnkfA6wdE8DUORsqoIEGlV53gzlOBxGHx243H06g) to buy the chosen product. At the *Decide* stage, the customer often uses price to justify their choice and reduce risk if possible, propelling the customer towards Buy.

The manager has ample opportunities to influence the customer here via pricing. They can use cues to display and communicate price to reinforce the customer’s choice or highlight a specific aspect such as the brand’s exclusivity, value for money, or low price. The customer’s buying risk can be reduced by offering low-price guarantees, price-matching guarantees, risk-free returns, and so on to provide reassurance. Price promotions can be used to keep the customer intent on following through with the purchase.

### Buy

The *Buy* stage is the focal point of the customer’s decision journey, the behavior that every marketer cares about the most. Imagine someone walking up to a door, knocking on it, and then entering or turning around and walking away. The stranger who knocked and then walked away is the marketer’s nightmare.

Pricing has a critical role in influencing the customer to walk through the door, i.e., complete the purchase and pay the price. Carefully crafted, situation-specific, just-in-time pricing actions are the ticket, which is why point-of-purchases promotions, limited-time offers, free trials, etc., are all so popular and attention-worthy.

The secondary task in the *Buy* stage is to reduce customers’ pain of spending money. Financing plays a key role here, as does using a pricing model that reduces payment friction and automates the buying process. It’s interesting that designing the financing structure is treated as an afterthought or not at all by pricing decision-makers. Most brands outsource it completely to Visa, Mastercard, or a bank.

### Enjoy

The *Enjoy* state is the most under-recognized and underserved state of the Customer Decision Journey. It includes the period from when the customer has completed the purchase through the product’s use and consumption. In many customer journeys, this state spans months or years. Customers have significant needs in this stage, such as justifying their purchase, validating and reinforcing their choice, and evaluating its value.

Yet, as I’ve pointed out before, this is also the state in which many companies fall silent with respect to pricing, resulting in a [Pricing Communication Vacuum](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/pricing-strategy-communicating-savings). More can be done here, with pricing actions and communications to reinforce the customer’s relationship with the brand. (Specific ideas on how to do this will be the topic of a future post).

### Advocate

In the Customer Decision Journey framework, the customer’s role in actively generating word-of-mouth, assuming that they like their purchase, is a big deal. The *Advocate* stage offers the brand measurable ways to monetize the customer’s relationship and reduce costs by bypassing the more traditional (and inefficient) methods of generating consideration and evaluation.

In the *Advocate* stage, the price allows the brand to give preferential treatment to customers who are active advocates in any number of ways from gifts, recognition, or more directly, [payments for performing monetizable actions](https://hbr.org/2021/06/what-happens-when-companies-pay-customers-to-write-reviews) like writing reviews or referring others. Many pricing managers don’t think of referral rewards, payment (or gifts) for writing reviews, social media posts, and other incentives as a part of pricing strategy. But these incentives work in the same way that a discount coupon or a free trial does. Rewards-based advocacy reduces the actual price paid by the advocate, just like a digital coupon reduces the actual price paid by the coupon user. Clipping a coupon and writing a review are both ways of purchasing the product for less for the customer and implementing price differentiation for the brand.

### Bond

The last distinct stage in the Decision Journey is *Bond*, which reflects the psychological ideas of loyalty and repurchase that are holy to marketers. There’s a decades-long tradition of glorifying loyalty-based repurchase behavior of customers, and the arguments in its favor are well-known.

From a pricing perspective, the fundamental question again is one of differentiation: Should loyal customers be treated like cash cows and left alone to buy regularly and generate a greater profit margin for the company as long as they will? Or should the relationship be periodically nourished (and hopefully extended) by treating these customers as special and giving them preferential prices?

Another more practical issue is that for bonded, loyal customers, the price can become a potential stumbling block, a source of friction in the sense that every repurchase transaction is fraught with the pain of payment. On the other hand, differentiated pricing that rewards loyalty makes the loyal customer feel special, publicly or privately, depending on how the pricing action is administered.

A solution to the problem of friction is to design and offer subscriptions targeted to loyal customers that also encourage repeat purchase behaviors. (There are a lot of nuances to unpack here in this one sentence, which we will do at another time). A solution to the challenge of making loyal customers feel special is to offer them a well-designed rewards program. Much of the discussion about incentivizing customer advocacy also applies to incentivizing customer loyalty.

### The Main Takeaway

In analyzing customer journeys, managers have considered touch-points to be stage-dependent for more than a decade. We can think of pricing actions in the same way. By treating prices and pricing actions as unique, stage-dependent activities in the Customer Decision Journey, the manager now has a whole new set of marketing programs at their disposal. They now have to figure out how to design and insert the most effective pricing actions at the correct points in their customers’ decision journeys.

In this post, I focused exclusively on McKinsey’s framework, which describes a rather extensive and thoughtfully conducted customer decision making process. In reality, there are [many other types of customer journeys](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/abs/10.1086/698414) that are quite different such as those dictated by habits, those that are low-stakes, and those conducted jointly with others as in a family or a B2B buying group. The stages in these journeys are entirely different. While the same logic of stage dependence applies to these other customer journeys, prices and price actions will work differently in these cases.

For managers, the main takeaway is that they should be paying attention to how their customers are undertaking their journeys and how they think of price and use price at each stage. Understanding this and then using pricing actions to support customers’ decision journeys will enhance customer experience and make the brand’s pricing strategy more effective.
