> *“Some things are in our control and others not. Things in our control are our own actions. Things not in our control are not our own actions” - Epictetus, The Enchiridion, 1.*

One thing that makes pricing decisions powerful yet frustrating is that the outcomes for evaluating them, whether the change in units sold, the change in revenue, or something else, can be measured reasonably well. Unit sales, revenue, etc., are already being measured as part of the organization’s normal reporting process, so little additional effort is required to calculate the lift and track the price action’s success. This ease of measuring outcomes for pricing is in stark contrast to the difficulty of measuring results of other marketing activities, particularly [brand advertising](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11747-022-00866-7).

#### The cause-effect gap in pricing performance

However, the cause-effect outcome measurements in pricing are typically relatively short in duration (e.g., the sales lift during a three-week-long price promotion), which is why other contextual factors can drive customers’ buying behavior in that window. Thus, the context in which the price action occurs often has an outsize independent impact on the results.

Just imagine running a half-off deal for patio furniture in the Houston market during the week that Hurricane Harvey hit us here back in August 2017. No matter how perfectly that promotion had been planned and executed, it would have flopped. On the other hand, imagine you owned a hotel in Austin at that time without a clue about pricing strategy. You would still have been [completely full with many more customers clamoring](https://www.washingtonpost.com/politics/full-extent-of-harveys-aftermath-starts-to-come-into-chilling-focus/2017/08/27/1b2b184a-8b56-11e7-8df5-c2e5cf46c1e2_story.html) for a room than you could have handled and made a killing. This is an extreme example. The truth is that moderate contextual factors, even a hotter-than-normal outside temperature, say, [can affect buying behavior](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S105774081300096X?casa_token=8U6uF63WiyUAAAAA:37TgdqN47mpXpy555kffpbAQAnXyF19gRmivtu6YLuAF5L6WJGNe2NNknRCPZce4JYJElfNNfeE0) and impact pricing performance.

This is the frustrating thing about pricing decisions. A price action that would have been highly successful at one time fails miserably at another time just because of external conditions beyond anyone’s control (which I call the “pricing context” here).

Now, when it comes to explaining a “good pricing decision,” the pricing context’s weight in affecting pricing outcomes throws up a formidable challenge. If our results are driven mostly by factors that have nothing to do with pricing, how can we celebrate good pricing decisions or fix bad ones?

In this post, I want to tackle this question. Specifically, I want to unpack the concept of a “good pricing decision” by using some academic ideas from research on judgment and decision making. JDM researchers have been studying the concept of a good decision for decades. We can use some of these insights to pinpoint a good pricing decision in a practically useful way.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

#### What is a good pricing decision? **

On the surface, the answer to the question, “What is a good pricing decision?” may seem obvious to many managers and entrepreneurs. It is dictated by the performance benchmarks their organization values for business outcomes. A company that values margin or aggregate profit may answer, “A pricing decision that makes us the most money is a good decision.” A company that is intent on competitive dynamics and managing market share may say, “A pricing decision that steals market share from our key competitor and weakens them is good.” A company that is customer-focused and emphasizes measures like customer equity and customer lifetime value will say, “A pricing decision that increases our core customers’ lifetime value the most is a good decision.”

Such responses are consistent with how psychologists have traditionally thought about good decisions. As E. Tory Higgins puts it[^1]:

> “The classic answer to what makes a decision good concerns outcomes. A good decision has high outcome benefits (it is worthwhile) and low outcome costs (it is worth it).”

#### What pricing decision makers say: Responses from practice

I’ve asked the question “What is a good pricing decision?” to many pricing decision makers over the years. Quite often, their reflexive responses tend to be that good pricing decisions are ones that “maximize the company’s sales” or those that “maximize the company’s profit.” Less often, they will answer that a good pricing decision is one that delivers superior customer value or one that increases or maintains pricing power or helps to build and strengthen relationships with customers.

When the pricing environment is challenging, they may say that a good pricing decision is one that allows the company to survive the severe downdraft and live to fight another day. In high inflation environments such as what we have experienced recently, a commonly perceived hallmark of a good pricing decision tends to be the “ability to raise prices without alienating our customers.”

Each of these answers appears reasonable because it aligns the pricing decision to a business outcome that is significant to the organization. Different organizations have different goals and choose relevant outcomes to measure them, so they should use different criteria to define good pricing decisions. Some of them even try to consider the context when choosing the appropriate outcome to measure.

#### The process vs. the outcomes of pricing actions

> “We should work on the process, not the outcome of our processes.” - W. Edwards Deming

However, these answers confuse the process of making pricing decisions with the outcomes to a lesser or greater degree. The responses make the mistake of focusing on the results while ignoring the significance of the decision making process.

In reality, it is deceptively difficult to pin down the characteristics of a good pricing decision and even more challenging to execute them[^2]. When making a pricing decision (or other consequential business decisions, for that matter, as mentioned earlier), the manager decides at time A, and the results of the decision unfold afterward at time B, which may be weeks, months, or even years later. In fact, for most pricing decisions, the outcomes occur over an indeterminate length of time, even though we apply some arbitrary, seemingly reasonable cut-off to measure results.

Consider, for instance, the boost in sales that occurs when a popular grocery store runs a weekend promotion (from Friday to Sunday) on some of its products. One portion of the sales increase comes from customers who buy and use more of the promoted products, and another portion comes from customers who switch their purchases from a competitor store. Both these sources of sales gains are beneficial for the grocer. However, many sales during the promotion are made to shoppers who are only stocking up because the items they regularly purchase from the store are on sale[^3]. For these customers, the 72-hour price promotion is simply “stealing” from the grocery store’s full-priced future sales.

Even with the most sophisticated tracking systems and assistance of AI-based predictive models, it’s difficult to pinpoint exactly how much of the grocery store’s sales increase is real and worth celebrating and how much is taken from future sales. (This is because even many customers buying the promotion may not know their own motivation). Furthermore, consumer research suggests that more grocery shoppers are behaving strategically and lying in wait for especially deep discounts, negating the sale’s very purpose[^4]. My main point is that the price promotion’s success, or lack thereof, cannot be accurately ascertained by measuring the promoted items’ immediate sales or even calculating the change in sales.

Pricing decisions never occur in a vacuum. After a decision is made and before its outcomes can be measured, factors outside the manager’s or the brand’s control often exert a strong influence. They might not be as severe as Hurricane Harvey mentioned earlier, but nevertheless, they produce significant effects on consumer behavior. Furthermore, these impacts can be beneficial or harmful and have nothing to do with the pricing decision.

To understand and explore the ramifications of this point, let’s briefly consider three cases from different industries. The first one describes the recent experience of Target when it inadvertently waded into the American culture wars, the second one describes the traumatic experience of the beloved Texas-based ice cream brand, Blue Bell, and the final one is about the good fortune of Spin Master, the company that made the Hatchimals, a wildly popular line of toys that was the rage a few years ago.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

#### Case 1. Pricing Target’s clothing line to celebrate Pride month

Imagine you have helped to launch US retailer Target’s Pride-themed line of clothing, taking considerable care to price each item moderately, consistent with the prices of other apparel that you sell. Your pricing challenge was manageable because you’ve been able to use years of historical pricing and sales data on this popular seasonal sale. It’s a pricing activity that you perform annually because Target has supported the LGBTQ+ Pride awareness month each June for the last decade by selling a range of apparel and other consumer products with dedicated in-store displays, signs, and supporting media and influencer advertising. This year is no different, and you are certain your pricing work will pay off.

But it doesn’t turn out that way. Controversy erupts almost as soon as the Pride displays go up. In stores throughout the country, many customers express outrage at the centrally displayed Pride merchandise, particularly targeting (no pun intended) the baby- and toddler apparel, the children’s books, and an adult one-piece, tuck-friendly swimsuit. TikTok videos calling for a Target boycott start getting millions of views.

[Embedded content: www.youtube-nocookie.com](https://www.youtube-nocookie.com/embed/EWBF1XCsCZw?rel=0&autoplay=0&showinfo=0&enablejsapi=0)

The boycott spreads through other social media, even leading to physical protests and confrontational behavior by some enraged shoppers, ultimately resulting in a noticeable impact on store sales. Unknowingly, your brand has waded into the middle of today’s fiercely raging culture wars.

Citing safety concerns, senior executives “adjust” the Pride marketing program within days, removing many items on sale from the line, relegating the Pride display to less-trafficked store areas, and publicizing these retractions. The net result is the clothing line that you priced so painstakingly is a complete bust,[^5] so much so that its effects carry over to store traffic and overall sales; the entire company’s revenue is impacted adversely because of the products you priced.

#### Case 2. Pricing Blue Bell ice cream for convenience store sales

Imagine you are the pricing manager at Blue Bell Creameries, a beloved Southern US brand[^6]. You have considerable pricing power and have made the decision to raise your ice cream prices by about 10% for the convenience store channel through the summer season. You want to increase the profit margin from this channel because convenience store shoppers buy ice cream on impulse and are less price sensitive, and your brand has strong recognition with them.

A similar seasonal price increase was very successful last year. You have every expectation that sales will be stable and profits will rise during the hot and humid summer months. You feel confident with your decision. You have made it thoughtfully, using a consistent process, after considering all the relevant factors such as your costs, competitors’ prices, how convenience store customers buy ice cream, how much they love your brand, and your company’s value proposition.

Two months later, however, calamity has struck. Compared to last year, your company’s ice cream sales are down by more than 70%. The drop in profits is even greater. The reason has nothing to do with prices. Your company, Blue Bell, has had to recall all ice cream products because of a listeria outbreak in the manufacturing facilities. You are devastated; by any measure, your pricing performance is abysmal.

***Case 3. Pricing the Hatchimals, the hottest toys of the year***

Imagine you are the pricing manager at Spin Master, a Canadian toy company. Utilizing the *value pricing framework*, you have just gone through a structured process to price your new toy called the Hatchimals[^7]. Hatchimals, which are interactive fuzzy toy creatures that hatch out of an egg after lots of coaxing, will be launched globally in early October, just in time for the holiday shopping season.

[Embedded content: www.youtube-nocookie.com](https://www.youtube-nocookie.com/embed/wFh9cKRW6Es?rel=0&autoplay=0&showinfo=0&enablejsapi=0)

Based on a careful analysis of numerous factors, including costs, customers’ reference prices, the previous prices of similar toys, parents’ propensity to spend for Christmas gifts, and your firm’s distribution and branding strategies, you have settled on a suggested retail price of \$60 for each Hatchimal.

Five months pass, and now it is New Year’s Day. Thanks to the constant media coverage and social media buzz (see below), the Hatchimals line has been a smashing financial success. In fact, they were the “hot toy” of the holiday season around the world. American shoppers were so desperate to get their hands on one that they lined up outside Toys “R” Us, Walmart, and Target stores for hours. As soon as a new shipment of Hatchimals arrived, it sold out within minutes.

Opportunistic consumers resold these toys on eBay, where the average price for a Hatchimal rose to \$143. As the figures below show, sales exploded, and virtually every Hatchimal sold well above \$60 in the secondary market.

#### Distinguishing between the pricing decision process and pricing outcomes

These cases illustrate the bounded scope of pricing decisions. Each cautions against emphasizing outcomes when evaluating pricing decisions. The answer to whether the pricing decision was good or bad for each company was overshadowed by the unforeseen external circumstances that intervened between the pricing decisions and the performance.

The disappointing sales and the partial recall of Target’s Pride apparel had little to do with its prices or the value delivered by the product line and everything to do with the charged political atmosphere. Blue Bell Ice Cream’s recall negated any potential benefits of increasing prices on the bottom line. The unexpected media-driven success of Hatchimals led to massive product demand and scarcity throughout the holiday shopping season, drove up prices in the secondary market, and left the company feeling it had underpriced its offerings.

In each case, there was a mismatch between the thoughtful, structured process used to make the pricing decision and the unsatisfactory outcomes that ensued. Still, despite the second-guessing, we can confidently say that the managers of these companies made good pricing decisions because they carefully followed a structured process suited to their organization.

#### An informed decision is a good decision

Whether it is the military, medicine, or industry, decision making experts have warned against relying on outcomes when evaluating a decision’s quality and judging whether the decision was a good one. In medical decision making, physicians are taught the concept of an *informed decision* when helping patients choose between different treatment options.

An informed medical decision is one where all the available information about the various options is gathered and weighted, and the final choice involves consideration of these factors and the patient’s and the physician’s values[^8]. In other words, a good medical decision has two properties: (1) the patient has considered all relevant factors and gathered and utilized appropriate information about each course of action with input and guidance from the physician (team), and (2) the choice reflects the decision maker’s core values.

Notice that in this definition of an *informed decision*, nothing is said about the patient’s health outcomes. The omission explicitly acknowledges that numerous factors are outside the patient’s and the physician’s control (including simple ignorance) and have nothing to do with the decision process but can still affect the patient’s health outcomes. Under these circumstances, the best that the patient and physician can do is put their heads together and make an informed decision after careful, dispassionate thinking, and then act. The outcomes will take care of themselves.

This is a crucial point that also applies to pricing decisions. Informed decision making is a core aspect of what we should consider to be a good pricing decision. In the *[value pricing framework](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/pricing-tool-the-value-pricing-framework)*:

> A good pricing decision is one that is made deliberately and thoughtfully, considers all relevant factors—costs, customer value, reference prices, and the value proposition, is based on the company’s business philosophy and values, and is explained clearly to customers and to employees involved in implementing it.

A good pricing decision uses a structured, information-rich process consistently. While we can identify the main inputs and decision architecture of such a process generically, every company will have a particular set of issues that are relevant to them and need to be considered closely. Thus, the specific process used by each company will be customized to its resources, available data, managerial mindset, and corporate culture.

Distinct from the process of setting and changing prices, a key aspect of a good pricing decision is the comprehensive and unambiguous communication of prices (and the logic behind them where applicable) to customers and the frontline employees responsible for implementing it. Far too many companies spend all their effort on the back end in working out the prices and little or no effort in communicating these prices, even internally within their organization.

#### Conclusion

Most pricing decisions take place within an uncertain, volatile, and intensely competitive business environment, and using a thoughtfully designed, comprehensive process that is consistently applied to establish the price and communicate it to employees and customers provides a layer of order and stability. Applying a consistent process is the way to make good pricing decisions.

So, are you making good pricing decisions? Yes, if you are making them in an informed way. No, if you are making them in an uninformed way. Either way, the results don’t matter and are irrelevant to whether your pricing decisions are good ones.
