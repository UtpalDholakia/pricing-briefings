##### ***Summary: ***In the [kick-off post of this series](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/from-diocletians-edict-to-netflixs), I proposed a working hypothesis that the history of price-change communication can be traced through five accumulating vocabularies of price legitimacy: authority, virtue, necessity, mechanism, and future value. The second post in this series examined antiquity and the medieval world, where authority, virtue, and mechanism were used to legitimize prices globally. This post covers the early modern period from 1500 to 1800, when private commerce took over the task of setting and justifying prices from public authorities. I consider case studies from three continents, including the price currents of Antwerp and Amsterdam, the correspondence of Medici and Sephardic merchants, the auctions and administered prices of the English and Dutch East India companies, the Ottoman narh registers, the Dōjima rice exchange in Tokugawa Osaka, and the Dutch tulip pamphlets of 1637. This broad investigation finds that the *necessity vocabulary*, the workhorse of modern “rising input costs” communications, was invented in the early modern merchant’s letter and was verified by its pairing with the price current. It also finds that when sellers’ price narratives failed in this era, audiences answered with remarkably disciplined counter-communications of their own, from satirical pamphlets to peaceful “riots.”

#### **An envelope sent by an Amsterdam merchant, 1686**

Imagine you are a merchant with offices on the Warmoesstraat in Amsterdam in 1686, a few minutes’ walk from the Exchange. It is Tuesday, and the printed *prijscourant* has arrived. It is a price sheet, listing current prices in Amsterdam for hundreds of commodities, from Polish rye and Setúbal salt to pepper, indigo, whale oil, and Virginia leaf tobacco. The sheet only provides the prices, no context for them[^1]. When sending the latest price information to one of your customers in Danzig, you enclose a prijscourant along with a letter that provides context. It explains why the price of pepper has risen this month (the return fleet still hasn’t returned at the Cape), why insurance is more expensive (French privateers are to blame), and ends with an exhortation that you are trying your very best on his account.

The 1686 merchant’s envelope is remarkably modern in its construction. It contains a context-free price list and a letter explaining what the prices mean and why they’ve changed relative to last week’s list. Both forms of price communication were developed in the early modern period and were designed for use in tandem. A good deal of what has gone wrong with price communication in the three centuries since comes down to our retaining an emphasis on the pricing context without including a price list (or its equivalent) each time. Today’s price communications routinely provide detailed (even if banal) explanations, yet they leave out evidence the recipient can use to verify the seller’s claims.

#### **The birth of private price communication**

In the previous post, we saw that price communication in antiquity and the medieval world was institutional and public, conveyed through means such as the edict carved in stone, the assize enforced at the pillory, and the inspector walking the stalls. The dynamic was similar in all these instances, with the price communicated by an authority and accepted by the populace, mostly because there was no choice. During the early modern period, covering approximately 1500 to 1800, new technologies and infrastructure significantly changed both sides of this dynamic. The printing press allowed market information to be reproduced and disseminated in large quantities at much lower cost, while the establishment of regular postal routes enabled merchants and others to send price information consistently to their correspondents for business purposes. Permanent exchanges were formed, providing central points for price making. Antwerp’s New Bourse opened in 1531, and Amsterdam’s Exchange in 1611. The chartered joint-stock companies, such as the English East India Company (founded in 1600) and the Dutch Verenigde Oostindische Compagnie, or VOC (founded in 1602), created vast private organizations that had the pricing power even greater than that of today’s FAANG companies.

The entity communicating information about prices was no longer necessarily someone in power, such as an emperor, a guild, or a bishop. Price information was now being shared by merchants writing to customers, brokers putting together a price sheet, a company announcing a sale, or an exchange broadcasting a closing price. The audience also changed accordingly, from vassals and subjects to correspondents, buyers, subscribers, and investors. These entities had far more power in the relationship. The shift in the balance of power forced sellers to work harder to explain their offerings and their prices and to use different strategies. Accordingly, this period produced three new forms of price communication, that I want to focus on in this post in turn: (1) the price current, in which the market is the creator; (2) the merchant’s letter, in which the merchant is the author; and (3) the auction and the exchange, in which a mechanism is the creator, live and in public.

#### **The legitimization of prices in the price current**

Historians studying commercial journalism have traced the earliest surviving price currents to Antwerp in the 1540s. By 1600, versions of price currents were regularly published and used in all the major European centers of commerce; the Amsterdam current is the best known, appearing continuously for more than two centuries. The core conventions of the price current were established early on and didn’t change much over time, listing the commodity's name, the units in which it was sold, and the price or price range[^2]. By 1540, merchants in Antwerp were routinely sending price currents to their correspondents in their commercial letters[^3].

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/0fb12c25-983c-4897-9c9a-c7b56bcddbe8_943x2751.jpeg)

Antwerp General Commodity Price Current, 1797. Published by Mollet & Co. Source: International Institute of Social History. This is a later descendant of the price-current tradition that began in sixteenth-century European commercial centers.

Three features of the price current particularly merit a pricing practitioner’s attention. First, the price current only listed prices and price changes, without any providing any additional context or information. The reader had to compare the previous current to the present one and figure out which prices and changd and why. It was entirely factual and descriptive, with no identifiable author. Considering it through the lens of vocabularies of price legitimacy, this corresponds to the mechanism vocabulary from [our framework](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/from-diocletians-edict-to-netflixs), where the mechanism (in this case, the market) is completely hidden. Even the Assize of Bread spelled out the formula and identified the officials responsible for administering it.

Second, and somewhat less intuitively, the price current’s legitimacy was derived from the process by which prices were certified for inclusion in it. For instance, the prices in the Amsterdam current were compiled by the city’s sworn brokers (licensed intermediaries who had taken an oath before the magistrates and could lose their office if found dishonest) and published under official sanction, meaning that mistakes could be punished by officials. The price current was trusted by the mercantile community because of the institutional apparatus that compiled and certified it[^4].

Also recall the pattern from the [medieval period covered in the previous post](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/authority-legitimacy-and-13th-century) in this series, where the religious vocabulary of *hisba* was grafted onto the pre-existing machinery of the market inspector. In the present case, the ordering of vocabularies runs in the other direction: a faint hint of the authority vocabulary, through the certification process, vouches for the completely opaque mechanism by which prices are generated and included in the price current.

Third, the price current greatly increased the visibility of prices, making them commodities in and of themselves, and enabling long-distance commerce between people who rarely, if ever, met in person. Merchants mailed price currents to those they traded with over vast distances; traders in mainland Europe, North Africa, and England now had the same price information at roughly the same time, reducing price dispersion. The price current effectively served as a verification device for every merchant’s price claims and increased the importance of the accompanying explanatory letter to contextualize the price information.

#### **Using the “necessity” vocabulary to justify prices**

The merchant’s letter, containing the price current and an accompanying explanation, is among the earliest instances of peer-to-peer price communication: a seller contextualizing the price for a buyer as part of an ongoing relational exchange. Before that, as we’ve seen in earlier posts, price communication had a distinctly hierarchical aura.

One vivid example of this comes from the surviving correspondence of Hildebrand Veckinchusen, a Hansa merchant trading between Bruges, Lübeck, and the Baltic. His letters are full of news about prices, complaints about overstocked markets, and explanations of why he couldn’t find goods at the prices his customers expected[^5]. By the 16th and 17th centuries, the volume of such correspondence had exploded in volume. For instance, the Castilian merchant-banker Simon Ruiz of Medina del Campo received over 50,000 letters between 1558 and 1598, and the 18th-century Roux firm in Marseille left behind an archive of over 78,000 letters[^6]. The price-contextualizing letter that accompanied the price current was essential in enabling long-distance trade over extended periods.

In justifying the price, merchants used what our framework calls the “necessity” vocabulary. To explain higher-than-expected prices, they would say things like the convoy has been delayed, the harvest has failed in X or Y, a granary didn’t store the grain properly, and it spoiled, leading to a shortage, or the port dues have been increased. The list of excuses goes on and on. The tone and structure with which these justifications were framed at the time will seem familiar to anyone who has read an email announcing a price increase from a vendor. In effect, it is saying, “The price increase was not by choice; the circumstances are to blame.”

There are two key differences between the price justifications of the early modern era and those of today. The first is the price current’s consistent presence. Any claims made by the merchant in the letter could be readily verified with one glance at the price current. As Francesca Trivellato has shown in her study of the far-flung network of Sephardic merchants of Livorno, stretching from Lisbon to Goa, individuals’ reputations were adjusted quickly and ruthlessly through letters circulating within it. Anyone caught giving false explanations risked being frozen out of future business opportunities[^7]. For the early modern merchant, accurately justifying a price change without embellishment meant surviving to trade another day.

Second, sellers engineered their price structures so as to be able to invoke the virtue vocabulary in their support. The most sophisticated practitioners of this were the Florentine bankers. The Church’s usury doctrine had made it sinful to charge interest openly on loans, so the Medici bank of Florence lent money through bills of exchange, hiding its price in the spread between the exchange rates at issue and at maturity. Economic historian Raymond de Roover’s classic study describes how the price of credit was communicated as the price of currency[^8]. In the context of our price legitimacy framework, this is an instance of a mechanism vocabulary (the exchange rate) used to satisfy a virtue constraint (the usury doctrine). It is also an exemplar of a common modern price communication practice: relabeling an aversive aspect of price as something more palatable, such as calling a mandatory, pointless add-on a “resort fee.”

#### **Examining the East India Companies’ surprisingly modern pricing tactics**

The chartered trading companies of the early modern era faced the challenge of setting prices across entire product categories when the only technology available was pencil and paper. The English East India Company solved this problem using a creative, theatrical approach. It sold its imports by holding great sales periodically in London. These sales were announced in advance, with printed catalogs circulated to traders and merchants, and the goods were made available for inspection in the Company’s warehouses[^9]. The bidding was conducted “by the candle.” A short candle was lit as bidding began, and whoever had the highest bid when the flame died won the auction.

This method was in practice in England by 1641. Its creativity lay in making the auction end completely random so that bidders couldn’t behave strategically as they do in eBay and other online auctions. If they wanted something, they had to bid with alacrity, in case the flame died out. In his capacity as a servant of the Council of State in 1652, John Milon recommended that “the way of selling by inch of candle, as being the most probable means to procure the true value of the goods.” Samuel Pepys, who watched the Admiralty sell surplus ships this way in the 1660s, observed that a bidder smartly watched for the final flare of candle wick and then shouted the last bid[^10]. Here, the vocabulary of mechanism has been opened up to scrutiny by buyers and embellished with a theatrical flair. The candle auction is a 17th-century attack on collusion and last-second sniping and a push for price transparency.

Unlike candle auctions, real-time auctions today are far more opaque. They may occur entirely online, often with bidders dispersed all over the world. Regardless of duration, there’s no way to know whether the bidder on the other side of a bid is a human or a bot, or even some entity colluding with the seller or auctioneer to manipulate prices. From a fairness perspective, candle auctions appeared (at least) far fairer than the average modern online auction.

Faced with the same pricing challenge as the English East India Company, the Dutch Verenigde Oostindische Compagnie (the VOC) chose a different solution. It administered prices by decree, no matter what that entailed. As a concrete example, the VOC had a monopoly on Moluccan spices and kept prices of items like nutmeg, cloves, and mace unchanged for decades in its European markets[^11]. It restricted cultivation to islands it controlled, destroyed clove and nutmeg trees, paid producers artificially low fixed prices, destroyed surplus stocks, and almost annihilated the population of the Banda Islands with heinous brutality, all in the name of maintaining prices[^12]. And we think [Burberry](https://www.bbc.com/news/business-44885983), [Nike](https://www.greenqueen.com.hk/nike-destroying-new-returned-sneakers/), and [Lacoste](https://www.yahoo.com/lifestyle/articles/fashion-brand-faces-backlash-shopper-214500975.html) are evil for destroying a few thousand pieces of shoes or jackets by cutting them up! Obviously, there are gradations of evil.

The VOC’s approach towards buyers was to maintain radio silence. They gave no reason why prices didn’t vary and didn’t believe they owed anyone an explanation, showing how the authority form of price legitimacy is so easy to embrace when the seller has power. It could afford to do so because, if a 17th-century European consumer wanted cloves or nutmeg, the VOC's offerings were the only choice. The company became the villain of 17th- and 18th-century consumers and activists. As we will see throughout this series, a monopolist's preferred approach to price communication is silence. They don’t explain because they don’t have to, no matter the ultimate repercussions.

#### **Price communication outside Europe: The cases of the Ottoman narh and Osaka’s Dōjima exchange**

Interesting cases of price communication extended far beyond private European entities in the early modern era. We will consider two cases from outside Europe in this section. In the Ottoman empire, the older price legitimizing vocabularies of authority and virtue continued to operate alongside the mechanism vocabulary, on a scale and sophistication that dwarfed anything in medieval Europe. The *narh* system set official prices for goods and services across the empire’s cities. Prices were set by the kadi in consultation with guild representatives and experts, recorded in registers (*narh defterleri*) maintained by the courts, publicly announced, communicated to the guilds, and enforced in the markets by the muhtasib (the same office as in Mamluk Egypt [during the medieval period](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/authority-legitimacy-and-13th-century)).

The surviving registers, including the great register of 1640 studied by Mübahat Kütükoğlu, list the official state-sanctioned prices for hundreds of goods, from bread and mutton to shoes and spices. The main pricing method used then, as now, was cost-plus, in which costs were documented and then modestly marked up. This should remind the reader of Kautilya’s approach in the medieval period, in which a combination of authority (the kadi and the sultan himself), virtue (the religious obligation to do the right thing), and mechanism (cost-plus methodology) is integrated into a comprehensive pricing and price communication apparatus.

One Ottoman practice deserves special attention from modern pricing practitioners. New *narh* registers (i.e., updated price lists) were issued after currency debasements and recoinages, such as the coinage correction of 1624, as well as at seasonally significant periods such as the approach of Ramadan[^13]. At sensitive times, the authorities took over the task of establishing and communicating prices, thus shifting the locus of responsibility (and blame) from individual merchants to the state. In contrast, as we have seen over the past few years, modern sellers facing inflation, supply dislocations, and currency devaluations are left to fend for themselves, and even attacked by the authorities for self-serving purposes. The Ottomans intuitively understood that changing prices is more legitimate when the task is performed infrequently, publicly, and by a disinterested authority. This system faded only in the mid-nineteenth century, as free-market policy propagated: fixed prices no longer applied to meat and most foodstuffs in Istanbul in 1856, and after the 1870s, only bread remained under narh[^14].

Half a world away, Tokugawa Japan built the era’s most advanced mechanism-based price communication system, and then spent the next 90 years in ambivalent angst about what they had created. Established in 1697 and formally authorized by the shogunate in 1739, the rice market at Dōjima in Osaka was the world’s first organized futures exchange, complete with membership rules, clearinghouses, margin requirements, and daily settlement prices[^15]. The price established at Dōjima was the reference price for rice, which is to say, for the fiscal backbone of the entire samurai economy covering much of Japan. It was broadcast throughout the country using medieval technology. Relay couriers (*kome bikyaku*) carried it to Kyoto, Nara, and Edo, while enterprising brokers relayed the information over long distances, [waving flags from hilltops](https://www.nippon.com/en/japan-topics/g02365/) to their network, who spotted them by peering through telescopes. Thus, the price in Osaka was communicated across hundreds of kilometers in a fraction of the time it would take couriers to transmit it[^16].

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/6bf77630-466d-457f-ba45-45e2872c4d07_933x1280.jpeg)

A signaler waves a flag from a platform atop the Dōjima Rice Exchange in a print from the series *Naniwa fūzoku hyakuzu zukai*. Source: National Diet Library

In 1775, the shogunate banned signaling market prices with flags and repeatedly extended the ban, but only partially succeeded against determined information smugglers until 1865[^17]. It is noteworthy that, rather than trading, speculation, or price enforcement, the *communication* of prices was targeted by the authorities. The shogunate had also intervened directly in setting prices, imposing a floor on rice prices in 1735 after riots against speculators in 1733, but the long war on flag signaling suggests that when an authority could no longer dictate the price, it instead tried to control its distribution. Modern descendants of this instinct are not hard to find, from contractual gag clauses that restrict merchants from disclosing payment surcharges to platform rules that forbid sellers from advertising lower prices elsewhere. When the price itself cannot be controlled, its knowledge becomes the target of control.

#### **The tulip pamphlets of 1637: when the audience takes over the pricing narrative**

No episode from the early modern period illustrates the important role played by the receiver or the audience in the price communication process better than the Dutch tulip mania. During the winter of 1636–37, the prices of tulip bulb contracts, which were traded mainly in tavern “colleges” by prosperous merchants and master craftsmen, skyrocketed. A single pound of the common Switser variety of tulips went from 125 guilders at the end of December in 1636 to 1,500 guilders by the beginning of February 1637. Then in early February, the market simply stopped functioning, and prices collapsed[^18]. On 24 February 1637, a consortium of florists put their heads together and proposed a mechanism to unwind this crash. They suggested that buyers could cancel their forward contracts by paying the seller roughly ten percent of the agreed price, the so-called *rouwkoop*, or grieving money[^19].

Between February and May 1637, Dutch presses churned out pamphlets and satirical songs about the crash by the boatload. The most famous of these was the three dialogues between two characters whose names translate as True-Mouth (Waermondt) and Greedy-Goods (Gaergoedt), printed by Adriaen Roman of Haarlem[^20]. The dialogues are satire and propaganda, through and through. In them, Gaergoedt tempts his friend with the buy high, sell higher mantra, suggesting that in other businesses he will barely earn 10 percent, “but with Flora it is cent for cent” (100%). Waermondt ultimately lays out the moral verdict for this fiasco as “a brainless commerce, against all good business ways.” These pamphlets and other media output during this period used a wide-ranging virtue vocabulary, calling the prices illegitimate and fueled by avarice, folly, and ultimately subject to divine judgment. This is [Diocletian’s preamble](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/from-diocletians-edict-to-netflixs), but now the roles have been reversed. The audience is the authority, (rightly?) wielding moral indignation against the irrational prices set by the marketplace.

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/2bdfccfb-fcba-4756-8e6b-584f5cc7a9e0_1200x767.jpeg)

Allegory on Tulip Mania by Jan Brueghel the Younger (c. 1640), Frans Hals Museum, Source: Wikimedia Commons.

The irony here is worth appreciating. Most of the data we have on the tulip mania that historians and economists have worked so assiduously on over the centuries is sourced from amateur pamphlets, rather than the merchants and florists who partook of the orchids. As an example, Peter Garber’s famous dataset of tulip prices draws dozens of its observations from the dialogues, and historian Anne Goldgar has shown that many of the most outrageous prices in the popular legend descend from the pamphlets via a chain of uncritical copying.[^21] The truth is that the actual prices of tulips during that period have been lost to history, so we will never know whether it was really a mania or just a short-lived bull market that became the target of activists and comedians.

#### **From just price to natural price**

Beneath all the cases we’ve covered thus far, the intellectual ground was shifting. Recall from the previous post that the doctrine that governed European price morality was the scholastic just price, developed by Aquinas and his successors, which treated every exchange as a matter of justice between the parties. The just price was close to the prevailing market price and had the heft of being moral and two-sided in the sense that the seller who charged more than the just price sinned, as did the buyer who exploited circumstances to pay less. A price was a moral act on both sides of the exchange. This doctrine remained the era’s official theory of exchange well into the 16th and 17th centuries. The theologians of the School of Salamanca pushed it further toward the market, situating the just price as something that was estimated naturally by the market[^22].

As time passed, the moral framing gradually receded from price communication, and in 1776, Adam Smith laid out an entirely new framework for the so-called natural price. Smith distinguished between *the market price*, determined by supply and demand, and the *natural price*, the sum total of the natural rates of wages, profit, and rent. He described the natural price as “the central price, to which the prices of all commodities are continually gravitating”[^23]. Thus, in this view, prices are natural phenomena, akin to the moon’s phases, tides, or hurricanes. The concept of “just” faded into the background of economic discourse, and with it the central concept of associating prices with morals. Virtue became less meaningful, and necessity and mechanism took over as the ways to justify prices and price changes.

The audience was not attuned to this shift and continued to consider moral aspects when reacting to prices. As an example, E. P. Thompson’s famous study of the English food riots revealed that the crowds who seized grain during years of famine or crop failure were remarkably disciplined. They took over the marketplace by force, then set what they considered to be reasonable prices, the so-called customary just prices, ensured that the available grain was sold at those prices, and gave the proceeds to the sellers. This was market intervention at its most genteel. Thompson described the food riots as “a last desperate effort by the people to reimpose the older moral economy” against the free-market economy[^24]. Through the lens of the current series on the history of price change communication, the food riots were an example of the audience taking over. The crowd decided what legitimate prices meant, and then enforced marketplace transactions to realize those prices. Here, the audience became the price-setter and the price-enforcer, albeit in a calm, non-violent way.

Thus, price communication in the early modern period expanded its scope from authorities, sellers, and merchants to involve the active participation of the citizens and consumers. The audiences became more than an audience, a key force for legitimizing prices and maintaining that legitimacy over lengthy periods. Thus, today’s customer who calmly accepts the airline’s explanation of “supply and demand” for why ticket prices have gone up, but then goes berserk when prices surge for an Uber after a storm, is acting very much like the crowd in the English food riots. They are displaying pretty much the same moral economy that Thompson wrote about 300 years ago.

#### **Lessons from early modern commerce for today’s pricing decision makers**

As with every era in this series, the case studies of price communication during 1500-1800 provide useful guidance for today’s pricing decision makers and communicators. Here, I will touch upon four lessons briefly. First, when the “necessity” vocabulary emerged in the merchant’s letter, the merchant’s potentially self-serving explanation could be verified by the prices listed in the accompanying current. Furthermore, given the relational nature of the merchant relationships and the difficulties involved with building new ones, lying or even exaggerating was costly. In contrast, today’s explanations of price increases are rarely verifiable. As customers, we are expected to trust in whatever explanation the seller provides, and it is taken for granted that no verification will be provided. There’s a real opportunity waiting for a business that is willing to take the trouble (and the risk) to provide verification by letting customers independently observe and confirm the claimed explanation for the price change.

Second, we saw that every successful application of the mechanism vocabulary during the early modern period came with visible certification, whether it was from the sworn brokers who stood behind the contents of Amsterdam’s price current, the publicly visible flickering flame of the candle auction, or the robust clearinghouses and settlement rules of Dōjima. More generally, the mechanism vocabulary works so well because it transfers the responsibility of communicating prices to a disinterested and powerful third party. But as should be obvious, trust is paramount. In contrast, today’s algorithmic pricing systems usually ask customers to accept whatever price is quoted to them without any discernible, trustworthy explanation. In other words, saying that “the algorithm sets the price and it is a black box” makes the mechanism far less effective in persuading customers or gaining their acceptance of the price. Automating pricing with AI tools is only exacerbating this problem.

Third, failing to provide explanations and justifications for a price change does not make the problem go away. The VOC failed to explain its pricing for a century and a half because it had the power to do so, but ultimately its grip and its image were both broken by pamphleteers, rivals, and reformers, who then got to define what the Company had been. Companies with strong brands and market positions face the same temptation today, and the same potential future reckoning looms for them.

As an example, in July 2011, Netflix [restructured the plans](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1065280/000119312511196126/dex991.htm) it offered its customers, raising the price of its combined DVD-and-streaming service by [approximately 60 percent](https://www.latimes.com/business/la-xpm-2011-jul-13-la-fi-ct-netflix-20110713-story.html). The company announced this significant price increase in a short blog post that provided no explanation. Perhaps not surprisingly, Netflix lost around 800,000 subscribers in the following quarter, and its stock fell by three-quarters from its summer peak. Netflix’s CEO e[ventually had to publicly apologize for having](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/netflix-ceo-i-messed-up/) “slid into arrogance based upon past success.” Since then, every time Netflix has raised prices, directly or indirectly, it has been careful to provide an explanation (even if many customers deem it unconvincing). In a nutshell, every time a brand raises prices without doing the groundwork to contextualize and justify the price, customers react negatively, hurting the brand and undermining its future credibility. Over time, these negative effects can compound, leading to measurable brand dilution.

Fourth, pricing communicators need to be mindful of the narrative behind their pricing strategy at all times. Even if they haven’t crafted one, their customers certainly have done so, and then the customers’ narrative becomes the well-accepted one. We saw this in the tulip trade’s case, where the price information that we know and use today is derived from satirical pieces rather than any unbiased, serious source. In the same way, the shrinkflation of our own decade is being documented by Redditors on r/shrinkflation and by TikTok influencers, with hardly any input from the CPG brands [practicing price-pack architecture](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/understanding-price-structure-updated).

What a difference it would make if the companies took the trouble to explain the logic (even if only cursorily) for why they are offering so many different SKUs and why their lines and prices are changing so often. Just like the early modern merchants who were kept honest by the commercial system in which they operated and the factual information contained in the price current, today’s managers need to keep themselves honest, knowing powerful customers are waiting to draw conclusions and protest when they see price changes that are not contextualized. The current default strategy is to apologize and backtrack, hoping there is no permanent damage to customer relationships. The cases from the early modern period teach us there may be a better way.
