##### ***Summary.*** Value-based pricing promises managers a disciplined way to translate customer value into price, yet many B2B firms fail to realize its full potential. In this post, I argue that the core challenge in effectively implementing value-based pricing lies in having the organizational capability required to realize the recommended value-based price and capture the identified economic value during pricing negotiations after a good value-based price has been calculated. This challenge applies to established B2B firms and is particularly relevant for startups, where initial founder-led success can give way to subsequent erosion in realized prices. The most revealing diagnostic is therefore the gap between the recommended value-based price and the price customers actually pay. Closing it requires building price execution capabilities. I discuss three possible avenues to improve pricing execution capability and make value-based pricing projects succeed.

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/f1c11a18-112b-4787-93e9-18376b3568ee_1448x883.png)

Consider the following composite not-quite-fictional scenario, based on several B2B pricing engagements I’ve been involved in or observed. An industrial SaaS firm (we’ll call it Helion Systems) had just completed an extensive (and costly) seven-month-long value-based pricing (VBP) project. During this high-profile multidisciplinary project, the Helion team had carefully [identified, quantified, and validated](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/pricing-concept-the-value-based-pricing) the key drivers of customer economic value[^1], which included reduced downtime, labor cost savings, and improved output yields compared to the next-best competitive option. After all the hard work was done, the annualized economic value of the Helion software offering to the customer was estimated to be nearly 70% higher than that of the competitor’s offering. There was ample room to raise prices and justify the increase. The team determined that the list price could be increased by about 18 percent relative to the competitive benchmark they had chosen and then maintained at that level of premium.

The application of the VBP method was rigorous, received buy-in from all major company groups through active involvement in the project, and demonstrated excellent face validity. The VBP project appeared to be on track for a solid positive outcome. The pricing strategy change was approved by the executive team, and the sales force was called in to corporate for a coordinated training session to implement the new pricing approach in the field.

A year went by, and then in a quarterly review meeting of the executive team, Helion’s CFO asked the Chief Revenue Officer, “What was the average realized price across the 43 deals we’ve closed since we changed to VBP?” After a lengthy, uncomfortable silence, the answer came, and it stunned everyone present into an even longer and more uncomfortable silence.

Instead of raising prices by 18% relative to the competition, as the VBP work had confidently recommended, Helion’s average deal realized price was 9% *below* the chosen competitive benchmark after the VBP project. In other words, Helion’s sales team was charging about 23% less than they should have. All the economic value of Helion’s superior software was being frittered away and then some. Not surprisingly, over the course of the year, each individual discount that led to this outcome had been argued for, internally justified, and approved, using typical deal desk jargon such as “response to competitive pressure,” “strategic-account considerations,” “keeping the valued client happy,” and many others. Value-based pricing had failed spectacularly at Helion.

In my experience, Helion’s entanglement with VBP is far from unusual. In fact, it’s more likely than not that after the initial surge in enthusiasm, and even after an excellent analytical engagement where VBP calculations are completed satisfactorily, a VBP project will eventually fail in a B2B firm. Even after two-plus decades of regular exposure to VPB and intermittent adoption, cost-based and competition-based pricing methods still dominate, while value-based pricing, even though customer-centric and associated with stronger performance in B2B settings, hasn’t taken hold. In this post, I want to explore one important reason for this puzzle[^2].

#### **A Brief Historical Perspective on Value-Based Pricing and Its Implementation**

We can trace the core idea behind value-based pricing to the Economic Value to the Customer (EVC) concept from the early 1980s, defined as:

##### “…the relative value a given product offers to a specific customer in a particular application-that is, the maximum amount a customer should be willing to pay, assuming that he is fully informed about the product and the offerings of competitors. This will normally correspond to the purchase price of the product he is currently using, plus (or minus) any value difference between this product and the product whose EVC is at issue[^3].”

***VBP goes mainstream.*** The EVC concept was expanded, refined, and packaged into a viable pricing method by academics, consultants, and corporate pricing managers in the 1980s and 1990s, resulting in a fully fleshed-out method primarily used in B2B settings, called [Value-Based Pricing](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/pricing-concept-the-value-based-pricing). Three lines of work during this period are particularly noteworthy. First, consultant Bradley Gale’s work extended EVC from a theoretical concept to a practical method for quantifying value and provided tools, such as customer value maps and market-perceived quality profiles, that VBP applications could use to translate differentiated benefits into customer perceived value and price-realization estimates[^4]. The second line of work was more academically oriented and built out methods for assessing, documenting, and communicating the economic value created for customers[^5]. Finally, several popular pricing textbooks, including those by Nagle, Dolan and Simon, and Monroe, exposed larger audiences to VBP, popularized its logic and methodology, and solidified shared vocabulary[^6]. By the early 2000s, value-based pricing was a core pricing method[^7], and it remains so to this day[^8].

***VPB and profitability.*** By then, enough industry experience had accumulated with the logic behind VBP, if not the specific method, that academics began to study its effectiveness. One early survey-based study of Belgian electronics and engineering companies found that VBP was positively associated with the successful launch of new products, while cost-based and competition-based methods were not[^9]. The authors of another survey-based study from 2013, using similar measures with a larger and more heterogeneous sample, reported their findings this way:

##### “We find that value-based pricing is positively linked to firm performance, and that competition-based pricing is negatively linked to firm performance. These results thus provide strong empirical support that value-based pricing is positively correlated with profitability, regardless of company size, industry or nationality. This finding is very significant: Small and medium-sized companies predominantly use competition-based pricing approaches... Companies operating in highly competitive industries typically use competition-based pricing approaches... companies in Asia use competition-based pricing approaches to a significantly larger degree... Our data suggest that all companies – i.e. also small and medium-sized companies, companies operating in commodity industries and companies in Asia – will significantly improve profitability by implementing value-based pricing[^10].”

***The puzzling low VBP adoption***. Interestingly, the academic evidence linking VBP to profitability didn’t translate into its widespread adoption by companies. A review of nearly two dozen survey studies conducted between 1983 and 2006 in the US, Europe, and Asia found an adoption rate of just 17% for VBP. In contrast, cost-based pricing adoption clocked in at 37%, and competition-based pricing was used by 44% of the respondents’ firms[^11]. Two decades of follow-up studies have found a similar pattern of results, suggesting that the vast majority of B2B companies continue to use costs or competitors’ prices as the key inputs into their pricing decisions. Even post-COVID, the picture hasn’t become much rosier (at least in Germany). VBP continues to face significant adoption challenges[^12].

***Why don’t B2B companies use VPB?*** This brings up an interesting paradox. Lots of research, not to mention conventional wisdom, suggests that VPB is associated with greater profitability, more so than other pricing methods. What’s more, it is the favorite method of pricing experts, academics, and consultants for B2B applications (including yours truly). Yet its actual use by B2B companies lags far behind cost-based and competition-based pricing, both of which are far more popular. Many managers I’ve interacted with over the years have had a common reaction to VBP: they love the idea, aspire to implement it, but are somewhat intimidated by it.

One useful explanation for this implementation gap comes from probing the underappreciated concept of *pricing capability*, defined by Dutta, Zbaracki, and Bergen (2003) as follows[^13]:

##### “…we argue that these processes for setting or changing prices are capabilities that a ﬁrm can use as a basis for competitive advantage. Given that a ﬁrm has created value, we argue that it is not a foregone conclusion that the ﬁrm will capture that added value by setting the right prices. Rather, ﬁrms must develop that ability in its pricing processes. Further, we argue that these processes are imperfectly imitable because of time decompression diseconomies[^14]. A ﬁrm cannot simply purchase the systems and skills required for pricing effectively. Instead, a ﬁrm must tailor and develop its pricing systems and processes to meet both its own and its customers’ requirements[^15] (p. 616).

Pricing capability deserves far more attention in pricing strategy discussions than it gets, and in many B2B firms, it is taken for granted when it shouldn’t be. As the authors describe it, pricing capability is an organization-specific characteristic, that includes competent coverage of all necessary pricing activities (such as identifying competitor prices, setting pricing strategy and translating it to prices, convincing customers about the price change logic, and negotiating prices with customers), plus the organization’s pricing know-how to gather and disseminate information, analysis, and decisions, the existence of routinized processes to simplify the complexity involved in applying a pricing method at scale (whether it is across a large assortment or across a large customer portfolio), the coordination mechanisms to ensure that everyone within the organization is on the same page, and the authority hierarchies to ensure that decisions about pricing, from price setting all the way through negotiation and quoting, are controlled in a reasonable way[^16].

The concept of pricing capability provides a whole new layer of insight into why there might be such large gaps between the potential of VBP, its adoption, and its successful implementation. Most B2B firms know how to calculate the VBP price (the six-step method is relatively straightforward, as is the logic of identifying sources of economic value), but they may not know how to make VBP work within their organizations because they lack the competence to apply it.

For example, a sales organization that lacks the routines, governance, discipline, or incentive structures to follow through on the value-based prices recommended by the pricing group will fail to realize the method’s potential. Prices recommended by VBP will remain aspirational, essentially theoretical artifacts meticulously created by the pricing group, while the sales organization continues to behave exactly as it did before and to realize sub-par prices.

The most direct empirical support for this pricing-capability-based explanation of the implementation gap comes from qualitative case research by Töytäri, Rajala, and Alejandro on the barriers to value-based selling in industrial B2B relationships[^17]. In their multi-firm qualitative investigation, the authors found that the obstacles to implementing VBP were cognitive and organizational rather than analytical. As expected, realizing the VBP was much harder than calculating value-based prices. The most significant barriers to VBP implementation in their study were the seller’s uncertainty about the magnitude and defensibility of the value claim, the customer’s skepticism about value estimates generated by suppliers, the lack of capability of the firms’ salesforce in articulating and quantifying value during negotiations, and an absence of structural support for having a good value conversation with customers, which included lacking things like incentive systems, training infrastructure, and governance routines.

#### **The VBP Implementation Gap in Startups**

VBP is also an appealing pricing method for many startup founders in the B2B space. It not only gives them a reasonable way to determine a price for their new offering in the early stages before it is ready for the market, but the underlying logic also seems effective in persuasive situations, such as investor pitches or venture capital competitions. However, when it comes time to quote prices to customers, VBP can quickly become a vulnerability for startups. This is because the organizational backbone needed to make VBP work effectively is even more fragile in these companies compared to larger, well-established firms. Let’s explore the issues involved in the application of VBP in startup settings in a little more detail.

***The founder is the pricing capability.*** During the early founder-led phase, VBP tends to work reasonably well, although the underlying reason has little to do with formal organizational capability. In this phase, the founder *is* (literally and figuratively) the pricing capability. Having internalized the customer’s pain points during the product build-out, the founder has a strong grasp of the product’s economic value to the customer. What’s more, the founder doesn’t need to deal with routines, coordination mechanisms, or authority hierarchies at this stage. Deep knowledge and conviction help. It becomes easier to defend the value-based price when the other side is trying to negotiate it down if you know exactly what the competitor’s offering costs the customer and how much better your offering is in alleviating customer pain.

***Deep knowledge & conviction don’t scale.*** Unfortunately, this founder-led approach to defending the VBP doesn’t scale well. It only works as long as the founder remains involved in every customer discussion and pricing negotiation. Once the first sales rep walks in the startup’s door, all bets are off. That’s because they only have secondhand familiarity with the underlying value mechanisms and relatively little skin in the game. Equally problematic, they are unlikely to have as much autonomy as the founder, for instance, to walk away from a deal to avoid compromising the price.

Faced with an experienced procurement team at a large enterprise customer pushing back on price, the new rep’s incentive structure, which may include such things as the need to hit quotas, hit revenue targets to get those RSUs, and other factors favoring urgency that are common in startups, all tilt the sales rep toward taking the easy way out and conceding on price. Practitioners who specialize in the transition from founder to professional sales force commonly warn that first sales hires rarely replicate the founder’s early sales performance[^18]. This gap is partly a function of skill and partly because the founder’s conviction in the VBP rarely carries over to sales reps.

***The startup sales rep can’t match the procurement team’s expertise***. Another issue that commonly exacerbates the entrepreneur’s challenge with VBP is the structural asymmetry in expertise between procurement employees at an enterprise customer and the typical B2B startup sales rep. Specifically, the procurement function in large industrial and enterprise buyers has become a skilled professional discipline[^19]. The people who work as buyers at places like Chevron, Boeing, Caterpillar, etc., are highly trained in negotiation and pricing techniques and enter negotiations with benchmarks, should-cost models, and concession targets that put pressure on the seller’s value claim. In comparison, the average startup sales rep is likely to be significantly underpowered, often with little specialized negotiation knowledge beyond an understanding of their offering’s features and benefits, no track record or pipeline, and no formal training. In this battle of two foes, the Goliath wins handily, and what started as a VBP implementation quest quickly turns into an all-out retreat to a far lower survival price.

#### **How to Reduce the VBP Implementation Gap**

As I’ve argued thus far, the puzzle of the VBP implementation gap can be explained as a problem of pricing capability in the Dutta-Zbaracki-Bergen sense. If this is the case, the next question is about what a typical B2B firm, whether a startup or an established firm, can do to build its pricing capability and adopt and implement VBP effectively.

Here, I want to suggest three mechanisms that could work in tandem to close the gap between the VBP-recommended prices and the realized prices. These mechanisms vary in scope, ambition, and applicability, and they address different points in the path from price-setting processes through to price negotiations and post-deal assessment. When applied in combination, they can form what is referred to as the firm’s *price execution capability*[^20], defined as the organizational infrastructure and processes that enable the VBP application to address the typical challenges encountered during price execution and come out on top.

***Using negotiation guardrails.*** The first mechanism concerns how the VBP is communicated to customers during the price negotiation. In many implementations of VBP, sales reps are given an ROI calculator, which is a spreadsheet or an app that the rep can fill out during customer conversations to quantify labor cost savings, revenue gains, reduced downtime, etc., for the customer[^21]. ROI calculators provide a tangible way to engage customers with a collaborative mindset, think through the offering’s value in a logical, step-by-step way, and gradually build customer buy-in. However, research shows that ROI calculators work effectively only under certain conditions, which are often lacking in the fundamentally adversarial conversations between sales reps and the procurement team during price negotiations[^22].

An alternative approach is to use a so-called *negotiation guardrail*, a structured response framework that maps every common procurement objection to a specific economic value driver that the sales rep can articulate without consulting a spreadsheet. AI tools can help draft first-pass guardrails, but the economic logic must be validated by pricing and sales leaders. For example, when the procurement person claims a 20% price advantage from a competing offering, a well-designed guardrail returns the conversation to the underlying mechanism by which value is generated, often through a concrete discussion of what the competitor’s offering omits and what that omission is worth to the customer over the (potentially lengthy) term of the contract. The point is to bring back the discussion to a more nuanced and accurate consideration of economic value to the customer, drawing on the seller’s deep knowledge of the offering’s value drivers, which is generally not available to the customer’s procurement team[^23].

***Implementing price authority levels***. The second mechanism concerns setting the approval parameters for giving discounts in the firm’s sales department. One framework that works well in B2B settings is an explicit definition of so-called price authority levels[^24], in which sales reps are authorized to grant discounts within a narrow band (for instance, 0 to 5 percent) at their discretion. Concessions beyond this band require progressively senior approval, with the greatest concessions (in percentage terms or dollar values) reserved for a member of the executive team, such as the CFO or CEO. Establishing these escalating constraints has two effects. Internally, the escalation requirement signals to sales reps that discounts are scarce and senior management is paying attention, which, over time, recalibrates the sales force’s expectations about what counts as a reasonable discount. Externally, when a customer’s procurement team learns that a deep discount requires a CFO or CEO sign-off, the request becomes more costly (and consequential) to make, and the implicit floor on negotiated outcomes rises. Price authority levels can also generate useful diagnostic feedback. If a substantial proportion of price negotiations with customers require escalation across multiple levels, it suggests that the value-based price has become misaligned with customer expectations or that contextual conditions have changed or been misread, both of which may indicate that the VBP model needs revision[^25].

***Conducting a win/loss audit.*** The third mechanism for improving pricing capability and closing the VBP implementation gap involves conducting a win/loss audit, defined as a structured retrospective inquiry into recently completed sales engagements, in which the firm systematically interviews buyers (and, in many cases, the sales reps who handled the engagement) to understand the factors that drove the deal outcome[^26]. Over the past two decades, this practice has been adopted by many B2B firms; the most comprehensive applications combine interviews conducted by third-party

consultants with internal conversations with sales reps to triangulate the drivers of wins and losses[^27]. Pricing practitioners have claimed that firms conducting win/loss audits experience meaningful improvements in win rates and increased revenue, although some caution is warranted, given that most of the data on this comes from consultants who have an economic incentive to talk up these programs[^28].

One issue is that win/loss audits often treat price as a monolithic explanation for outcomes, which limits their ability to identify weaknesses in the firm’s pricing capability. Further insight is needed by probing the specific role price played in the outcome. For instance, there is a difference between the situation in which the VBP price is simply too high relative to the customer’s perceived value in a given pricing context and when the price is consistent with value but the sales reps fail to articulate its basis or otherwise come up short in pricing negotiations. While the first is a failure in applying the pricing method, the second is an execution failure, and each one points to different remedies.

Another limitation is how much weight to give sales reps’ explanations in win/loss audits. Research on sales-failure attribution suggests that sales reps reporting on lost deals tend to exhibit a systematic bias toward externalizing the cause of failure to factors outside their control, with price routinely given as the most prominent external attribution[^29]. Thus, a rep’s account of why a deal was lost on price should be treated as a hypothesis that requires further testing rather than a conclusion. A rigorous win/loss pricing audit should triangulate the sales rep’s account against the customer’s own account, gather enough evidence from multiple wins and losses to distinguish between patterns and idiosyncratic occurrences, resist the temptation to interpret persistent “price” attributions as a mandate to lower the value-based price, and be open to considering other types of price execution gaps.

#### **Implications for Pricing Decision Makers**

I want to leave you with two key insights from this in-depth consideration of pricing capability in VBP implementation. The first is that the most consequential investment a B2B firm can make in its pricing function is rarely in the method used to estimate value-based prices. The analytical foundations of VBP are well-established and accessible to any firm willing to apply them. The far more difficult challenge lies in the firm’s pricing capability. For VBP to be implemented successfully, the firm needs to have in place the organizational routines, decision making processes, and governance structures that allow the VBP recommendations to survive furious negotiations with seasoned procurement employees of an enterprise customer who have a dozen different ways to talk price down. For founder-led firms in particular, this transition typically occurs at the moment of the first sales hires, when the founder’s personal conviction in the offering’s value and deep knowledge about it must be encoded into systems that travel with the sales team rather than the founder. A firm that completes the analytical VBP exercise and stops there has done perhaps a third of the work; the remainder lies in putting in place the negotiation guardrails, the price authority levels, and the win/loss audits I have described.

The second insight is that the gap between the price recommended by a firm’s VBP model and the price its customers actually pay is the most informative diagnostic in the entire pricing function. This [price realization gap](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/the-value-pricing-framework-updated) is rarely measured systematically, and when it is, it is often dismissed as a transitional problem that can be solved with more sales training. Treated seriously, however, the gap reveals where the firm’s value claim is actually being defended, and where it is quietly being conceded. Closing that gap is what distinguishes a firm with a VBP method in its pricing toolkit from one with a successful VBP strategy.
