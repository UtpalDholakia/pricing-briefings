#### **A Classic Pricing Dilemma**[^1]

Although it was just another meeting on a random Tuesday afternoon, it could well have been a skirmish in a battle royale, marketing on one side and sales on the other. Apex Blend’s CMO, Sarah Sidwell, was meeting with her counterpart and VP of Sales, Mark Sanders, after months of conflict. It was the summer of 2024, and the two executives were set to make decisions about the company’s promotional strategy for their line of premium high-performance blenders. Both were seasoned executives with decades of experience in the consumer durables space. But their views on price promotions couldn’t have been more different. Sarah Sidwell kicked the discussion off:

> *“My instincts and experience are both telling me that offering a price promotion is totally unnecessary right now. All it will do is undermine our premium brand positioning. Mark, you know our product quality is unparalleled. Everyone regards us not just as having the most advanced blending and food processing technology, but also knows us for delivering an amazing customer experience. We’ve spent millions of dollars and worked hard for years to get here. I strongly feel that our premium pricing strategy with minimal consumer discounts is the driving force for our success. You only have to look at our 2024 performance to see I am right. We’re on track to grow our revenue by 18% this year. Our gross margins will be north of 45%, and after making some mistakes, we’ve limited discounting programs to our previous generation’s products. The consultant we hired conclusively found that the 2023 promotional campaign decimated our profitability. Not only did we lose money, but who knows how much our brand was damaged by that promotion? Now I know you believe promotions are essential, so help me understand your view on this.”*

Mark Sanders knew that Sidwell was committed above all else to maintaining Apex Blend’s position as a premium brand. She was constantly referring to Apex as “the Ferrari of blenders” to the point it got annoying. In every executive meeting, she had consistently articulated that all strategic decisions should flow from the goal of enhancing the Apex premium brand image. Nevertheless, he felt obligated to share the harsh realities that his sales team encountered on a daily basis. He responded:

> *“Sarah, I appreciate that you are willing to consider my viewpoint. I strongly believe we need to be much more aggressive with our promotional strategy, not less. The number one feedback I’m getting from our retail partners is their frustration with our lack of meaningful promotional events. A 25% promotional discount would make a huge difference. For one, it would increase our brand’s awareness in the marketplace, which, you know, is not that great. Second, it would also support my team in their interactions with retail accounts. And third, it would attract new consumers who are currently priced out of our brand and also encourage existing customers to upgrade or purchase additional Apex Blend products. And frankly, the 2023 promotional campaign’s impact was not as clear-cut as the consultant made it sound. I honestly wonder if he knew what he was doing. When I ran the numbers, I found the promotion was actually quite profitable. I am happy to share this analysis with you.”*

#### **Company Background**

***Market Overview***

The U.S. blender market experienced strong growth after the pandemic, reaching approximately \$3.85 billion in revenues in 2024. Its market size was forecast to grow to \$5.76 billion by 2034. The consumer portion of the blender market (excluding commercial buyers) was valued at \$2.16 billion in 2024 and expected to grow at a rate of approximately 12% annually through 2031.

Within the consumer market, blenders could be classified by performance, motor power, and target use (although these factors overlapped considerably). The market also included personal (or portable) blenders, countertop blenders, and commercial-grade units. Power categories ranged from “under 300 watts” to 1,500+ watts, with premium models typically featuring 1,000+ watt motors. Premium blender brands had to balance technology, blending performance, and aesthetic design elements while maintaining reasonable pricing.

Premium blenders routinely possessed preset programs, variable speed controls, and self-cleaning cycles. Higher-end models also came with sound enclosures, advanced blade designs, and containers made from BPA-free materials.

Blenders were sold individually or in starter packages with multiple container sizes. A typical premium starter package would include a high-performance base unit, large blending container, personal-size containers, and specialty accessories. Distribution channels included kitchen specialty retailers (like Williams Sonoma and Sur La Table), department stores (Macy’s, Nordstrom), big-box electronics stores (Best Buy, Home Depot), warehouse clubs (Costco and Sam’s Club), mass merchandisers (Target, Kohl’s, and Walmart), online retailers (Amazon), as wll as direct-to-consumer channels.

In addition to Apex Blend, the top brands in the blender market included Vitamix (the leading premium brand), Ninja (mid-tier to premium), Blendtec (premium), KitchenAid (mid-tier), Breville (premium), Hamilton Beach (entry to mid-tier), Cuisinart (mid-tier), NutriBullet (personal blenders), Oster (entry-level), Philips (global brand), and numerous smaller Chinese brands sold mainly through online retailers. Beyond Vitamix’s leadership, the blender market was rather fragmented.

***Apex Blend Company Profile***

With these market factors as the backdrop, Apex Blend designed, manufactured, and marketed premium high-performance blenders, specializing in professional-grade motors, advanced blade technology, and precision engineering. It targeted affluent and upper-middle-class home cooks and health enthusiasts who took these identities seriously. The company used a selective network of premium kitchen specialty retailers, high-end department stores, and its own direct-to-consumer channels.

Earlier in 2024, Apex Blend’s CEO, Josh Weinberg, had established four strategic priorities for the company for the next five years: (1) expand selective retail distribution, (2) increase market share in the premium blender segment to 12%, (3) preserve brand prestige and performance leadership, and (4) achieve revenue growth of at least 15% annually while keeping gross margins above 42%. Revenues in 2024 were projected to hit \$89 million with a gross profit of \$37.4 million. He had made it clear to Sidwell, Sanders, and other members of the executive team that their performance evaluation and future in the company would hinge on executing these priorities.

***Consumer Research Insights***

Over the past couple of years, Sarah Sidwell had commissioned several consumer research studies to better understand the blender market as a whole, and Apex Blend’s target customers more specifically. She deemed the following findings to be particularly noteworthy.

A 2022 study by *Kitchen Insights Research* surveyed 1,800 households with incomes over \$75,000 who had purchased blenders and juicers in the past year. The study’s key findings were as follows:

- 65% of respondents owned at least 2 blenders, whereas 32% owned 3 or more blenders (including personal-size units). Very few respondents in the study owned just one blender.

- 42% of respondents purchased their blenders from specialty kitchen retailers, 28% from online retailers, 15% from warehouse clubs, 10% from department stores, and 5% from other channels.

- 35% of respondents cited blending power and performance as the most important criterion in choosing a blender, 25% cited build quality and durability, 18% cited ease of cleaning, 12% cited price, and 10% cited the blender’s design/noise level.

- 48% of respondents used blenders primarily for making smoothies, protein shakes and other health beverages. Only 9% reported using their blender regularly for alcoholic beverages like margaritas, mojitos, and piña coladas.

- When choosing a blender, 38% were influenced by online reviews and 19% by social media demonstrations on platforms like TikTok and Instagram. 41% of respondents preferred to see a blender in person before making the purchase.

- 45% of respondents received or purchased blenders as gifts during holidays.

- 25% of respondents reported that they would be motivated to buy a new blender because of a price discount, whereas 38% would be motivated by free accessories, containers, or other incentive gifts.

Apex Blend’s own customer analysis using 2023 warranty registrations by 1,750 customers found that 75% of its customers were aged 25-55, 68% were female, 78% had household incomes over \$100,000, and 58% considered healthy eating and fitness to be their primary lifestyle focus. They rated blending power and reliability as the most important purchase factors. Surprisingly, price or discounting were mentioned as a purchase driver by relatively few of these customers.

Furthermore, a 2023 brand awareness market study conducted by a third-party found that unaided brand awareness for the Apex Blend brand name was only 8% among households with incomes under \$100,000 and 18% among those with incomes over \$100,000. In contrast, the leading brand Vitamix enjoyed awareness levels of 62% across income segments, while Ninja achieved an unaided awareness of 47% in these segments. When asked about recent advertising recall, fewer than 5% of study participants recalled seeing even one Apex Blend advertisement.

***Apex Blend’s Marketing Mix***

**The Product Line**

Apex Blend offered four lines of blenders, focused on advanced motor technology and precision engineering. The lines were priced using a standard good-better-best pricing approach, with the idea of earning higher margins from higher-priced items.

1\. **Essential Series (E-Series)** – These were Apex Blend’s entry-level premium blenders with an average retail price of \$189.

2\. **Professional Series (P-Series)** – In the second tier, with a significant price difference to the E-Series, these were mid-tier professional-grade blenders with an average retail price of \$329.

3\. **Master Series (M-Series)** – This was Apex Blend’s high-end performance line with an average retail price of \$429. M-Series blenders were sought out in commercial applications by those who owned juice and smoothie stores, coffee shops, etc.

4\. **Elite Series (Elite-Series)** – This was Apex Blend’s ultra-premium line with an average retail price of \$599. More than one celebrity chef and many mom and foodie influencers had posted themselves using this blender on social media.

The high-end Elite-Series featured proprietary turbine blade technology and sound enclosures, going beyond the comparable Vitamix offering. The M-Series blenders had a commercial-grade 1,500-watt motor and came with precision speed controls. The P-Series blenders had 1,200-watt motors and had recently been evaluated as the quietest blender in its quality bracket by Consumer Reports. Finally, the entry-level E-Series blender was positioned as the premium option for those wanting to explore the premium segment. It came with a 1,000-watt motor and essential blending features.

**Sales and Distribution**

The company maintained strong relationships with retailers, with most major retail accounts carrying all four blender lines. The primary competitive advantage for Apex Blend was that its retail accounts enjoyed higher margins when selling its products compared to the major competitors. Specifically, the gross margins for Apex Blend unit sales averaged 48% compared to 42% for Vitamix and other competitor premium brands.

Mark Sanders and his team had been selective in choosing retail partners. Apex Blend blenders were sold through three upscale kitchen specialty chains, which contributed 42% of its overall trade orders, premium department stores like Nordstrom and Bloomingdale’s (contributing 28% of total trade orders), 125 independent specialty stores across the country (22%), and direct-to-consumer channels, including the company website and catalog, which currently contributed only about 8% of total orders. Thus, Apex Blend’s marketing channel structure was more old-school and less reliant on online channels. Sidwell wondered whether this was a strength or a liability in the post-COVID consumer behavior environment.

Apex Blend’s sales force was another strength for the company. It consisted of six experienced account managers with an average tenure at the company of 8 years. Their compensation was structured as 65% base salary and 35% incentive-based bonuses. Each account manager visited the assigned retail accounts on a monthly basis to present new products, provide retail training, and offer merchandising support. In comparison, the sales staff of competitor blender brands only visited their accounts (which overlapped) three or four times a year.

**Advertising**

In 2024, Apex Blend allocated \$3.2 million (which amounted to approximately 3.6% of projected sales) to advertising. This could be compared to an estimated spending of 5-6% by leading competitors like Vitamix and Blendtec. Most of Apex Blend’s spending went toward national advertising in premium food and lifestyle magazines (Food & Wine, Bon Appétit, Martha Stewart Living, Women’s Health) and targeted digital campaigns focused on the fitness and wellness communities. Here, too, Apex Blend’s approach could be considered old school, firmly endorsed by Sarah Sidwell.

Interestingly, cooperative advertising with retailers through avenues like in-store signage or local market advertising was not a priority for Sidwell at all. She insisted on maintaining strict brand standards and having full control over every Apex Blend-related marketing communication and brand exposure. Consequently, her team would only support retailer advertising in premium magazines that aligned with its brand positioning. In contrast, other blender brands across the quality-image spectrum relied far more on advertising through retail partners and also on social media and influencer marketing campaigns.

**Pricing and Promotion**

Sarah Sidwell was a strong proponent of using pricing strategy to communicate and reinforce Apex Blend’s premium brand image. Accordingly, she believed that the pricing of the blender lines should deliberately reflect its brand premium, and had advocated for a premium pricing approach that gave more weight to customer value, located prices at higher price points relative to the corresponding competitor products, and avoided giving discounts or other incentives to customers. She wanted consumers to buy Apex Blend blenders for their own sake, not because they were on sale. After considerable discussion and input from Sanders and his sales team, she had settled on a good-better-best suggested retail price structure of \$199, \$329, \$429, and \$599 for the four lines. While these prices were higher than the corresponding Vitamix products, she still felt that the 9-endings were not ideal and smacked of low prestige. She would have preferred rounded or even prices but had lost the price-ending battle to Sanders. She had also pushed for pre-printing prices on the blender packaging to discourage retail partners from discounting indiscriminately. After several fierce debates with Sanders, she had prevailed on this issue.

In 2023, responding to pressure from retail partners and sustained insistence by Sanders, Apex Blend ran its first significant price promotion, a 25% consumer discount on all E-Series blenders offered from March 15 to May 15. The promotion was announced to retailers in January with special promotional pricing for products ordered during the promotional period. Sanders’ sales team asked the participating retailers to accept a 44% margin instead of their usual 48% to have some skin in the game, and virtually all accounts had complied. The same 25% off offer ran on Apex Blend’s DTC channels, with some announcements made beforehand through TikTok videos and a website banner. However, the brand did not have much of a following on TikTok, and there hadn’t been much engagement or impact on DTC sales.

Apex Blend sold a total of 41,200 E-Series blenders during the promotional period. Based on anecdotal reports shared by account managers, Sidwell estimated that about half of the retailers passed along the full 25% discount to their shoppers, while the remainder averaged a pass-through of 12%. Warranty registrations completed by customers during this period indicated that 78% of the purchases were from households that already owned at least one Apex Blend blender, 72% felt the discount was important in their purchase decision, and 15% indicated interest in upgrading to a higher-end series in the future.

A second promotion run in late 2023, over Sidwell’s protests and Sanders’ backing, offered brief one-week 25% discounts on specific models across all blender series. This offer created a surge of orders for the affected models that strained manufacturing capacity, with some back orders extending into early 2024. Sidwell also noted that post-promotion sales of these models dropped significantly below prior-year levels.

Earlier in 2024, over Sanders’ objections, Sidwell pushed for limiting the promotion to a modest 15% discount and restricted it to select previous-generation models with the main goal of flushing out inventory. Several retailers complained to Sanders’ team members that such a promotion offered little value to them. Trade orders showed minimal increases during the promotional period.

Finally, Apex Blend had also run “gift with purchase” promotions offering premium blender accessories or additional containers with qualifying purchases. Shoppers loved this promotion; sales surged 12% when this offer was active. However, retailers, by and large, despised these offers, claiming that they increased operational complexity, took up non-revenue-generating space on shelves and inventory, and wasted employee time. Their feedback was to stop running such promotions or fulfill the incentive through another channel, such as mailing the incentive to customers.

In comparison to Apex Blend, both Vitamix and Blendtec rarely ran price promotions. Instead, they focused on product innovation and certified refurbished blender programs, offered through owned DTC channels or very specific retailers. In contrast, mid-tier brands like Ninja offered regular seasonal price promotions, many of them 30-40% off, several times a year.

#### **Analyzing the 2023 E-Series Price Promotion**

Adding further nuance to the decision about Apex Blend’s promotional strategy going forward, Sidwell had engaged an external consultant, a pricing strategy expert, to evaluate the 2023 E-Series promotion and provide her with an estimate of how effective it had been. Using statistical modeling using time series data of sales from the previous seven years, the consultant projected that normal sales without the promotion would have been roughly 24,500 units over the March through May time period of the promotion. The consultant also made the following calculations:

***E-Series Promotion Financial Data (Per Unit)***

- Normal average retail price: \$189.00

- Promotional average retail price: \$141.75

- Normal manufacturer selling price: \$90.72

- Promotional manufacturer selling price: \$76.61

- Variable costs (including allocated overhead): \$58.20

To calculate the promotion’s profitability, he then used the formula:

{(actual units × actual contribution) - (forecast units × normal contribution)} = incremental contribution impact.

Additionally, the consultant also estimated the potential for cannibalization of the P-Series line that could be attributed to the E-Series promotion, projecting normal sales of 28,400 units during the promotion period (while actual sales were only 26,800 units). He calculated \$52,640 in lost contribution from this cannibalization.

On the positive side, he estimated that Apex Blend saved \$22,100 in inventory carrying costs because of reduced inventory levels on account of the promotion.

Mark Sanders objected strongly to the consultant’s analysis and even questioned his pricing expertise in internal executive memos and meetings when the promotion strategy was under discussion. He believed that the consultant’s analysis was flawed for three major reasons:

1.  **An overstatement of the baseline E-Series Sales forecast by the consultant**: Sanders reviewed E-Series sales data for the promotional period and the corresponding prior year period. He found that the March-May orders for the previous year, 2022, totaled only 16,750 units, and the sales for the two months before the promotion, January and February 2023, were 18% below the 2022 levels. Sanders therefore estimated that the 2023 normal sales should have been 13,735 units (or 18% below 2022 same-period levels), instead of the 24,500 units used by the consultants.

2.  **Inaccurate allocation of overhead costs**: Sanders argued that, using the logic of only considering costs that are incremental to the pricing decision, the only variable costs to be included for the profitability analysis were direct labor costs and materials costs (which totaled \$39.80 per unit). He believed that allocated overhead costs should be excluded.

3.  **Invalid adjustments (cannibalization and inventory savings) made by the consultant**: Although the inventory cost savings supported his position, Sanders still felt that both estimates of P-series line cannibalization and inventory cost savings were essentially guesswork and therefore should not be included in the analysis of the promotion’s profitability. He even believed that the entire thesis of cannibalization used by the consultant was flawed and lacking common sense.

After correcting the alleged flaws and redoing the analysis, Sanders arrived at a very different assessment of the promotion’s profit impact from the value reported by the consultant.

#### **Next Steps—Promotional Strategy for 2025**

After Sarah Sidwell and Mark Sanders had each presented their points of view and shared their respective analyses of the 2023 E-Series promotion, the tension in the room ratcheted up perceptibly. An impasse was building, but it had to be resolved by the time the meeting was done. Decisions about how Apex Blend should approach promotions in the coming months and years, or whether it should abandon them altogether, had to be tackled head-on.

Among the issues that needed to be resolved were: (1) Should Apex Blend run a promotion, and if so, what type of promotion or promotions should it run? (2) What goals should the brand set for its promotional campaigns? (3) If a promotion was on the table, how should it be designed? What factors should be considered?
