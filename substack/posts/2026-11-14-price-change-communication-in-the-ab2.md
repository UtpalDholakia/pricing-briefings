##### ***Summary. ***The persistent inflation during the 1970s helped establish many of the price communication tools and practices still widely used today, including advance notices of price increases, cost-based explanations, index- and formula-based pricing, surcharges, automatic adjustments, and shrinkflation. This post examines how government controls on wages and prices, and companies’ responses to them, shaped these practices and what they teach today’s pricing decision makers. The key lesson that emerges is that customers experience price changes cumulatively. Effective price change communication must account for this history, acknowledge the financial and psychological burdens to customers of deteriorating value, explain price decisions accurately, and make commitments they can keep.

#### **A Purchasing Manager’s Desk, March 1974**

Imagine you are the purchasing manager working at a mid-sized rubber goods manufacturer in Akron, Ohio, in the first week of March 1974. The wage and price controls that tamped down your suppliers’ prices for more than 2 years are being discontinued soon, and not surprisingly, every letter on your desk this morning is about prices. A resin supplier has written to you stating that, effective April 1, prices for all resin grades will rise by 8%, reflecting the ongoing increases in feedstocks and energy costs. Orders confirmed before then will ship at current prices. A trucking company has enclosed a copy of an Interstate Commerce Commission (ICC) order authorizing a 6% fuel surcharge on all freight charges, effective February 7. This month’s electricity bill has a line item labeled “fuel adjustment charge” that is much higher than last month’s, and there’s an enclosed insert explaining that this charge rises and falls automatically with the prices of coal and oil. Your own sales department has left a draft of a letter on your desk. It is an announcement to be sent to your company’s customers announcing an across-the-board price increase of 7% starting next month. You observe that it has been written using the advice given by L. E. Frailey.

#### **The persistent inflation of the 1970s**

The stagflation era of the 1970s is often associated primarily with respect to the magnitude and longevity of inflation. In the United States, for instance, consumer prices rose 3.3% in 1971, 8.7% in 1973, 12.3% in 1974, 13.3% in 1979, and 12.5% in 1980.[^1] Between January 1971 and December 1982, the consumer price index more than doubled. In dealing with high inflation for over a decade, sellers had to raise prices frequently by necessity, often several times a year. As a vivid example of this, consider that only approximately 12% of magazines changed their cover price in a given year in the 1950s. But in 1974, more than half of all magazines published in the United States raised their prices at least once.[^2] By the middle of the decade, the announcement of a price increase had become such a recurrent practice among American corporations that standard templates and procedures for raising prices had been established.

#### **President Nixon’s wage and price freeze of 1971**

Perhaps the single most significant announcement about prices during the 1970s was made by President Nixon on a Sunday evening in August 1971. He had spent the first two and a half years of his presidency pushing back on the idea of price controls. For example, in a televised address to the nation on June 17, 1970, [he proclaimed](https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/address-the-nation-economic-policy-and-productivity):[^3]

“I will not take this Nation down the road of wage and price controls, however politically expedient that may seem. Controls and rationing may seem like an easy way out, but they are really an easy way into more trouble--to the explosion that follows when you try to clamp a lid on a rising head of steam without turning down the fire under the pot. Wage and price controls only postpone a day of reckoning, and in so doing, they rob every American of a very important part of his freedom.”

But then he reversed course, with experts homing in on three things changing his mind. First, two months after the aforementioned speech, Congress passed the Economic Stabilization Act of 1970, giving the President the authority to freeze wages and prices (whether he wanted to do so or not). With each passing month, his inaction was perceived by many Americans as a sign of weakness, or worse, indifference.[^4] Second, by the summer of 1971, inflation continued to remain above 4%, putting pressure on the President. Compounding this challenge, various experts, including the Treasury Secretary of his own administration and the Federal Reserve Chairman, came out in favor of wage and price freezes.[^5] Third, there was a run on U.S. gold reserves in the second week of August, leading to a suspension of gold convertibility. Eventually, a freeze on wages and prices became unavoidable.[^6] The President announced his decision in a speech broadcast from the Oval Office on Sunday, August 15, 1971:[^7]

“The time has come for decisive action, action that will break the vicious circle of spiraling prices and costs. I am today ordering a freeze on all prices and wages throughout the United States for a period of 90 days. In addition, I call upon corporations to extend the wage-price freeze to all dividends. I have today appointed a Cost of Living Council within the Government. I have directed this Council to work with leaders of labor and business to set up the proper mechanism for achieving continued price and wage stability after the 90-day freeze is over. Let me emphasize two characteristics of this action: First, it is temporary. To put the strong, vigorous American economy into a permanent straitjacket would lock in unfairness; it would stifle the expansion of our free enterprise system. And second, while the wage-price freeze will be backed by Government sanctions, if necessary, it will not be accompanied by the establishment of a huge price control bureaucracy. I am relying on the voluntary cooperation of all Americans, each one of you: workers, employers, consumers, to make this freeze work. Working together, we will break the back of inflation, and we will do it without the mandatory wage and price controls that crush economic and personal freedom.”

It was a brief but comprehensive communication about prices that applied to every price and wage in the entire US economy.[^8] Notably, it did not assign blame to anyone for the inflation, instead positioning it as “one of the cruelest legacies of the artificial prosperity produced by war.” It insisted that the measure was temporary and not meant to create or support an ongoing bureaucratic infrastructure, something that turned out not to be true. And finally, like the communications from governmental authorities during the First and Second World Wars, it called upon every citizen to cooperate for the good of the country and their fellow Americans.

In the short term, President Nixon’s communication served its purpose. The Dow Jones Industrial Average rose 32.9 points the next day, the largest one-day gain in its history up to that point, on record volume.[^9] A poll by *Time* magazine the following week found that 73% of respondents thought the freeze was a good idea, while only 16% opposed it.[^10]

#### **The consequences of the wage and price freeze of 1971**

In the first phase, the freeze established a price ceiling, defined as the highest price “pertaining to a substantial volume of actual transactions” during the 30 days ending August 14, 1971, and a disclosure requirement that sellers provide a record of the highest prices or rents they had charged for public inspection.[^11] In the second phase, which began on November 14, 1971, every retailer had to prominently display its base prices for all food items and for “those 40 items in each department which have the highest sales volume, or those items which account for 50 percent of total sales in each department, whichever is less,” by January 1, 1972.[^12] Until the lists were posted, each area of the store had to have a sign at least 22 by 28 inches, announcing that base price information was available upon request and providing a supply of “Base Price Information Request Forms.” When someone filled out a form, the retailer had to reply in writing. Even the wording of the reply was standardized. It had to say, “In reply to your request, we are pleased to inform you that our base price for \[item\] is \[\_\_\_\].”[^13]

These new rules were enforced by the Internal Revenue Service, which had no prior experience enforcing government-regulated prices. Still, the agency hired 3,000 new agents, and during the first phase, the Service “responded to almost 8,000 media inquiries; participated in 68 news conferences, 614 radio and 330 television programs.” During the second phase, it “issued 1,367 news releases publicizing regulations,” answered 3.4 million public inquiries, contacted 27,000 retailers, wholesalers, and packers “to assure compliance with pricing and posting requirements,” and distributed over 18 million copies of stabilization publications.[^14] Eventually, this massive effort had to be supported by thousands of volunteer “price monitors” recruited by the AFL-CIO in early 1972 to check the posted base prices against shelf prices.

#### **The freeze-influenced price increase communication**

As part of the massive effort, two bodies were created to administer the regulations behind the wage and price freeze: a 15-member Pay Board with 5 representatives each from labor, business, and the public, and a 7-member Price Commission, led by C. Jackson Grayson, the dean of the business school at Southern Methodist University. The Price Commission had a staff of about 600 employees and operated from October 1971 to January 1973. It broadly divided US companies into three tiers and managed them differently. Companies with sales above \$100 million had to obtain the Commission’s approval before raising any price above its base price and had to wait up to 30 days for a decision. Firms with sales between \$50 million and \$100 million could raise prices, but had to report their price increases quarterly. Smaller firms were allowed to administer the rules themselves without oversight.[^15]

Approvals by the Price Commission were based on the impact of costs on the firm. A firm could raise a price to the extent that its “allowable costs” increased, and only if its profit margin, measured as a percentage of sales, did not exceed the average of its best two of the three fiscal years before the freeze. Essentially, the rule allowed a full pass-through of cost increases, subject to the profit ceiling. As of May 5, 1972, the Price Commission had approved 3,398 filings for price increases totaling an estimated \$8.8 billion, denied 494, and had 1,332 still awaiting a decision.[^16]

For the pricing department in large US companies, this meant that every price increase now had to be planned well in advance and written up as a proposal, with an explanation anchored entirely in rising input costs. Customer value, reference prices, and the value proposition, which are considered important inputs to pricing decisions today, were excluded entirely by law during this period. Once approved, the firm had to turn around and make the same announcement to its customers, explaining that it was raising prices *only* because its input costs had gone up.

The Price Commission approvals were themselves public communications of price changes. For instance, in December 1972, the Commission approved price increases for General Motors and Ford. General Motors was allowed to raise prices on its 1973 models by an average of \$54, and Ford by \$62.55 (although Ford had requested a price increase of \$91.53), based on its passing on cost increases to customers. The Commission also noted that no specific models from either company could be priced more than 6% above the August 1971 prices. This was price transparency on an entirely new level without any input from the company involved.[^17]

The wage and price controls did not last long. The third phase, beginning in January 1973, removed prenotification in most industries, and food prices rose at an annual rate of approximately 25% during the first half of the year.[^18] On June 13, 1973, President Nixon announced a second price freeze for up to 60 days, and blamed food inflation on “increased demand at home and abroad, by crop failures abroad, and… some of the worst weather for crops and livestock that we have ever experienced.”[^19] With grain prices rising and the prices of chickens frozen, poultry farmers were losing money at an increasing rate on every bird they sold to the market. In July, Time magazine reported that they were “systematically gassing, drowning and suffocating a million baby chicks.” According to one report, it cost the farmer about 49 cents per pound to get the chicken to market, and because of the frozen prices, they could only charge 40 cents per pound.[^20] The statutory authority for mandatory controls expired at midnight on April 30, 1974.[^21]

After the price controls ended, the rise in prices was, not surprisingly, fast and furious. The BLS attributed the acceleration of consumer prices in the second quarter of 1974 to “the termination of remaining controls on wages, prices, and profit margins on April 30.”[^22] The end of controls raised 1974 inflation by between 1.7 and 3.1 percentage points as “artificially depressed prices snapped back,” and between August and December 1974, paper napkin prices rose at a 43 percent annual rate and toilet tissue at 77 percent.[^23] The consumer price index rose 12.3 percent over 1974. We may surmise that the apparatus of price-change communication that had been put in place by large corporations to apply for price increases to the Pricing Commission was now being used to communicate price increases to customers and other constituents.

#### **The anatomy of the 1970s price increase letter**

The price increase letters of the 1970s had a standard format, and usually contained four pieces of information: the advance notice, the effective date and price protection, cost justification, and a tone which might (but not necessarily) include an expression of regret.

***Advance notice.*** An example of advance notice in price change communication can be found in the lead antiknock compound industry, which consisted of four major sellers: Du Pont, Ethyl, PPG, and Nalco, whose price-announcement practices between 1974 and 1979 were challenged by the Federal Trade Commission. The Second Circuit’s 1984 opinion described the practices as “the use by each company of contract clauses requiring at least 30 days advance notice to customers of changes in price” and “providing advance notice of price increases to the press.” The court found that advance notice “was initiated by Ethyl, well before the entry of Du Pont or the other two manufacturers into the market, as a means of aiding buyers in their financial and purchase planning,” and described it as “a common practice in the chemical industry.”[^24] The FTC argued that the communication went beyond customer service and was aimed at competitors.

Ethyl and Du Pont “not only gave the 30-day notice of price increases, which their contracts required, they gave several additional days notice so that competition could respond,” and the Commission quoted an internal company document that stated the logic plainly: “This timing gives 37 days notice and allows one week for competition to respond, including a weekend.”[^25] Over the 5-year period, there were 24 price increases, and in 20 of them, all four firms arrived at the identical list price on the same effective date.[^26] The Commission concluded that while the 30-day notice was lawful, providing the additional days was not. The court reversed this opinion, finding that the practices had “an independent legitimate reason,” and that in an industry of four sellers, “manufacturers quickly learn of competitors’ price changes, usually within hours, regardless of the advance public notice.”[^27]

The Ethyl case nicely brings out the multiple audiences and the non-overlapping objectives of price communications. While the advance notice was intended as a courtesy to existing customers, it was also meant to (indirectly?) inform competitors as well as manage relations with regulators.

***Effective date and price protection.*** The case of the gypsum wallboard industry is helpful in unpacking the role of the effective date in the price communications of this era. In 1971, a federal district court described the gypsum wallboard industry’s practice in terms that could equally apply to most industrial goods of the period. Price increases “were usually announced by the initiator of the price increase substantially in advance of the effective date,….” in order to afford an opportunity for customers to meet their existing resale commitments at current prices.” A customer was given the opportunity to stockpile the product at the old price, which it could then use to fulfill production commitments at its current price, a practice the court called “job price protection.” Competitors who imitated one seller’s price increase “usually adopted the same effective date,” because a firm that moved earlier “could make no sales.”[^28] Thus, establishing an effective date served as a commitment device for the seller and as a planning device for the buyer.

***Cost justification.*** The third piece of information in the price increase letter was the reason for the increase, usually higher input costs. During this era, considerations of customer value, reference prices, or, in fact, any other factor beyond costs, were largely absent from price communications. For instance, when Bethlehem Steel raised prices by 5% in August 1968, its statement said that “the price increases are expected to add only enough to revenues to offset cost increases just incurred or expected.”[^29] Likewise, when it announced a 5.5 percent increase in December 1977, it “cited sharply rising costs of raw materials and labor,” and the company “contacted the Council of Economic Advisers before making the announcement.”[^30] One key difference in these communications is the relative lack of transparency about the specificity of the cost increases. While telling customers that the price increases were due to rising costs, the communications rarely explained precisely which input costs were rising and by how much. Thus, the letters seemed transparent but hid the essence.

***The letter’s tone.*** The fourth element of the price increase letters was to frame the increase in a way that matched the customer. In L.E. Frailey’s [Handbook of Business Letters](https://archive.org/details/handbookofbusine0000lefr/page/790/mode/2up), the author provides useful guidance on what tone to use in communicating a price increase:

“Although it is not possible to set forth any “one right way” to announce higher prices, certain general principles seem to emerge from the comments already made.

1. Don’t apologize. Prices must go up and down with the business market. The seller is always entitled to a reasonable profit.

2. On the other hand, don’t be callous or indifferent if the announcement happens to be made when orders are easy to get. The customer is entitled to courtesy and consideration in good times as in bad times.

3. In a general rising market, it is usually unnecessary to justify the advance. Don’t weaken your position with explanations.

4. If, for some special reason, an explanation does seem desirable, be specific. Figures that prove increased cost of production are far more convincing than generalities.

5. When possible, accompany the announcement with some comment or fact that points to more favorable business conditions. Make the buyer realize that higher prices are only an indication of better times for *him*.”

#### **Using surcharges to deal with inflation**

The inflationary environment of the 1970s also led to the adoption of another pricing practice: surcharges. Then, as now, a surcharge was presented to the buyer as a separate line item, clearly attributed to an increase in a specific cost component, and it generated the implicit understanding that it would go away when that cost came down, thus potentially absolving the seller of responsibility for the price increase.

The oil embargo of October 1973 led to the widespread use of surcharges. When independent truckers shut down highways in December 1973 and February 1974 to protest the price of diesel, they were assuaged by a settlement that included an Interstate Commerce Commission (ICC) order that allowed truckers to add “a percentage surcharge not to exceed 6 percent” to freight charges, with the full amount passed through to the actual fuel purchaser.[^31] When the second oil shock hit in 1979, the ICC modified the unitary surcharge into a formula, publishing a new percentage on a weekly basis based on a diesel price index, setting its price on January 1, 1979, as the base.[^32]

The surcharge resolved the problematic and unpleasant communications associated with frequent price changes. Instead of offering detailed explanations for monthly price increases in letters, the seller could now simply say they had adopted an index-based pricing model, which would change as the index’s underlying components changed. In the language of our price-legitimacy vocabulary, in an inflationary environment, index-based pricing shifts the vocabulary of necessity (“We \*must\* increase prices because our costs have gone up”) to the vocabulary of mechanism (“We are raising prices because the index says we must”).

#### **Index- and formula-based pricing**

During the 1970s, a large proportion of prices in the United States began to be set using formulas and indexes rather than on an ad hoc basis, reflecting a reliance on cost as the primary input variable and the near-complete exclusion of customer value, reference prices, and the value proposition. This shift occurred gradually, escaping the notice of many affected buyers.

The application of cost-of-living escalator clauses was not new to the 1970s. Back in 1948, for example, General Motors and the United Auto Workers had agreed to use just such an escalator, tying quarterly wage adjustments to the consumer price index.[^33] But by 1966, only about 20% of workers were covered by major union agreements. This changed in the 1970s. The percentage of workers covered increased to 28% in 1971, 39% in 1973, and 60% in 1976, where it remained for the rest of the decade.[^34]

Perhaps not surprisingly, once inflation fell, employers renegotiated and removed cost-of-living escalators. Similarly, the federal government adopted automatic cost-of-living adjustments for social security payments[^35], and commercial contracts followed suit. The General Services Administration directed its regional offices to use escalation clauses in long-term leases in November 2023. While only about 9% of government leases had such clauses in 1978, by March 1982 the number had jumped to 69%.[^36] Electric utilities were also allowed to automatically pass on rising fuel costs to their customers without a rate hearing, resulting in high electricity bills and concerns about a lack of accountability.[^37]

#### **The communication of price increases by consumer brands**

Consumer brands faced a different challenge. While they didn’t have to satisfy regulators, they were still subject to consumer decision making and evaluation as prices increased inexorably. During the period from 1973 to 1981, inflation was top of mind for most Americans. For instance, in an October 1978 Gallup poll, 83% of Americans chose inflation or the cost of living as the most important problem facing the country.[^38] Different brands used different strategies to deal with inflation, all of which will seem familiar to today’s inflation-hardened readers.

***The Hershey chocolate bar. ***Hershey had maintained the price of its nickel bar since 1921 by using shrinkflation. The company’s policy was clearly expressed as follows: it decreased “bar weight when cocoa bean prices soared” and increased it “when bean prices declined.”[^39] But by the beginning of the 1970s, the nickel price point was no longer viable for a chocolate bar. It was discontinued on November 24, 1969, after a run of nearly half a century. What followed was a combination of shrinkflation and price increases throughout the seventies, as shown in the next figure, a familiar playbook for today’s CPG brand managers.[^40]

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/6d07c75d-ccb7-4012-9feb-2b03fe00ac1f_1672x941.png)

***Meat and coffee.*** Just like the price of eggs caused consternation among American consumers in 2023 and 202, the prices of beef and coffee caused a lot of angst among consumers in the 1970s. When beef prices skyrocketed in early 1973, President Nixon imposed a price ceiling on beef, pork, and lamb.[^41] Just like the price freezes, retailers were required to display a list of ceiling prices for all relevant products they sold, leading to a 20% to 30% drop in meat sales in some stores.[^42] Coffee prices rose because a frost in Brazil destroyed a crop. The wholesale price of coffee rose from \$1.21 a pound in June 1975 to well over \$4 by the spring of 1977. Major brands like Hill Brothers and General Foods adopted shrinkflation, reducing their 16-oz cans to 12 oz while holding prices constant.[^43]

***Supermarket prices.*** Universal Product Codes or UPCs were introduced in 1974.[^44] This was a significant technological leap, from having to mark every package individually with the price to maintaining price records in a database that could be revised conveniently, and using bar codes to scan packages and speed up the checkout process. In the inflationary environment, consumers pushed back, arguing that retailers could now raise prices more often because it was easier. Michigan enacted an item pricing law in 1976, requiring every package to have a price clearly marked on it rather than relying on shelf price tags or scanning the package at checkout. A handful of other states followed suit. These laws remained on the books for three decades; in Michigan, they were replaced by the Shopping Reform and Modernization Act, which allowed retailers to use shelf signs and in-store price scanners.[^45]

#### **Lessons from the 1970s for today’s price change communication**

The 1970s are a particularly useful period for today’s pricing practitioners to draw insights from, because inflation persisted throughout the decade, echoing the pattern we are in now with greater amplification. Consumers had to deal with prices that rose frequently for many of the products they purchased over a lengthy period. Shrinkflation worked up to a point in some categories, but as costs kept rising, as we saw in the Hershey case, it was no longer enough. The claim that prices had to be increased because costs were rising also became old news, and consumers grew tired of this explanation. This era offers useful insights into what to do (and what not to do) during an extended period of inflation, when the rate is generally trending higher, but there are periods of respite. I have framed these insights as actionable advice for pricing decision makers.

***Recognize and try to alleviate the consumer’s financial and psychological burdens***

Suppose a company has to raise its prices by 8% annually for two consecutive years to manage rising costs, but feels relieved that it is only raising its prices by 3% the third year because inflation has somewhat subsided. While this may seem like a positive development for the firm’s view, it is just less bad news, not good news for the customer, who is now paying 20% more than they were three years earlier (often without a commensurate increase in their income, and often with [enshittification of the product](https://pluralistic.net/2023/01/21/potemkin-ai/#hey-guys)). Announcing a lower price increase as a win (e.g., “Happy days, we are only adjusting prices 3% this year”) is not going to work.

Given repeated shrinkflation, which must necessarily be part of any consumer-facing pricing strategy during prolonged inflation (whether it is through a reduction in quantity, a degradation of quality or service, or both), the consumer also faces a psychological burden in addition to the obvious financial one. They now have to figure out exactly how much they are getting per dollar today versus three years ago, and whatever the number is has to be adjusted further to account for enshittification (e.g., my favorite salsa is not just 23.5% more expensive but also 17% less tasty).

Most communications about inflation-driven price increases today simply don’t consider this issue. They are too company-focused and pay little or no attention to the customer’s subjective experience of the price increase or the hardships (large or small) they face in their lives because of it. The big missing piece in this communication is empathy. Even if the company doesn’t have a good solution for the customer when it raises prices out of necessity, it is worth acknowledging the burdens consumers face in this economic environment, specifically beyond the price itself. Going a step further and offering real solutions, even if they involve a non-pricing answer, such as introducing a lower–price, lower–quality option, is likely to be welcomed by inflation-weary consumers.

***Leave some flexibility for maneuvering, given an uncertain future***

Although inflation generally trended higher throughout the 1970s, prices did not rise linearly (much like what we have been seeing since Covid). After blazing through the early 70s, inflation dropped substantially in 1975 and 1976 before picking back up.[^46] Businesses had to deal with this choppiness, so they could not afford to breathe easy and assume prices would come down in the future. They could not promise their customers that inflation would be temporary and that prices would return to the 1968 or 1970 levels again.

The same concern applies today. When communicating future prices, it is best to commit to decisions a company can control in an uncertain economic environment. Perhaps the easiest way to do this is to manage the duration for which prices remain effective. In industries where formula- or index-based pricing makes sense, the frequency of price changes will need to be accelerated. In settings where prices tend to have longer lifespans, those lifespans will shorten. Where escalation clauses can be written into contracts, they should be. When the seller has no choice but to raise prices, customers should be given advance notice, just like we saw in the purchase manager example earlier in this post. Warnings that higher prices need to be given early and often, in tune with the volatility of input price changes. When there’s an opportunity to lower prices slightly, it should be embraced and publicized. And finally, complex price structures should be embraced because they offer sellers more degrees of freedom to achieve fine-grained, targeted price changes.

***Provide a coherent and complete explanation***

One area where today’s businesses are similar to those of the 1970s is their incomplete or even omitted explanations for price changes. Obviously, there’s a whole spectrum of price changes in which explanation matters to varying degrees. In the 70s, temporary laws were enacted that froze prices and required businesses to post maximum prices using very specific formulas. After that, when the regulations were removed, letters communicating price increases to customers adopted the necessity vocabulary, focusing mainly on the general theme that prices were rising because input costs were rising.

Things are a bit more complicated today because most businesses have become more sophisticated in pricing and tend to use customer value and reference prices to set and adjust prices to a far greater extent than in the 70s. Correspondingly, costs are less of a contributor to prices for many companies or even entire industries. Thus, it is important to provide customers with a clearer and accurate understanding of the logic behind the price change, especially in an inflationary environment. For example, it would make sense for a discount brand to blame its rising prices on costs, but it would be ludicrous for a luxury brand to do so. See the [recent explanation from PepsiCo](https://www.pepsico.com/newsroom/press-releases/2026/pepsico-makes-iconic-snacks-more-affordable-ahead-of-super-bowl) below for a relatively concrete example, while noting that the decision here is to lower prices. It would be much harder to produce such an explanation to justify raising prices.

PepsiCo’s press release explaining its price reductions, February 2026.

The enduring lesson from the pricing experience of the 1970s is that every price communication between a seller and a buyer is not a standalone event; it is related to all prior communications and sensitive to the overall economic environment and the specific nature of the relationship between the two parties. When crafting a pricing communication, the seller needs to take this history and context into account, make feasible commitments, and provide customers with as much information as possible to reduce their financial and psychological burdens.
