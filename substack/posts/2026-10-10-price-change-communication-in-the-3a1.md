##### ***Summary:*** In the *[opening post of this series](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/from-diocletians-edict-to-netflixs)*, I proposed that the history of price change communication can be understood through five accumulating vocabularies of price legitimacy: authority, virtue, necessity, mechanism, and future value. The next three posts traced their development through antiquity and the medieval world, the early modern period, and the industrial age. This fifth post focuses on the first half of the 20th century, encompassing the two World Wars, when national governments became the dominant communicators of prices. It examines the U.S. Food Administration’s fair-price lists and window cards, the General Maximum Price Regulation of 1942, rationing by the Office of Price Administration (OPA), the Home Front Pledge, Britain’s Ministry of Food, and John Kenneth Galbraith’s account of wartime price controls. It also covers the interwar period, that included the Roaring Twenties and the Great Depression of the thirties.

##### ***Author’s note:*** I treat 1914–1946 as a monolithic period in this post, despite the long interval between the wars and the milestone events in between, such as the economic boom of the twenties followed by the 1929 Crash, and the Great Depression. It should be noted that the pricing and price communication infrastructure used in the Second World War was largely a response to the First World War’s failures. In 1941, American officials repeatedly invoked the experience of 1917, and many of the Office of Price Administration’s choices during that period are hard to make sense of without that context. During the period covered in this post, the state takes the seller's side, frankly asking the buyer to help enforce prices. Virtue reigns supreme, expressed as patriotism rather than justice.

### **A Grocery Store in Ohio, Spring 1944**

Imagine shopping for groceries in a mid-sized American city in the spring of 1944. In your pocket is the *[War Ration Book Number Four](https://eisenhowerfoundation.net/primary-source/war-ration-book-number-four)*. A rationing calendar cut out from today’s newspaper is folded in your coat pocket. An OPA poster (like the one below) hangs prominently in the store window. Inside the store, a card beneath the shelf of canned peas reads “Ceiling Price 14¢.” This tag is prescribed by federal regulation. Every cut of meat in the butcher’s case behind the counter is tagged with two prices, a monetary price in dollars and cents and a ration price in points. To purchase meat, you have to spend both currencies. The point values change at the start of every month, so the grocer has to relabel everything in his store.

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/2a483129-8afe-4675-b0ea-da6a257ab594_1731x909.png)

Keep the home front pledge. Poster created by the Office of Price Administration to promote compliance with food conservation and price control programs, 1944. Source: UNT Digital Library.

On some of your shopping visits, you may see a woman with a clipboard observing the displays with eagle-like eyes. She is a volunteer price-panel assistant with the local War Price and Rationing Board, checking 12 designated foods against their OPA ceilings. If she finds an overcharge, she will record it and return the next week to verify that the grocer has corrected it.

This procedure in the last year of the Second World War affected all parties involved, from the shopkeeper to the shopper to the volunteer citizens, and more than anyone, the federal government. As the *[previous post](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/price-change-communication-in-the-industrial-age)* in this series on price communication posited, the price tag from the industrial revolution era made the seller’s price publicly available information and even a form of public commitment. In this grocery store, circa 1944, however, the commitment was made on the seller’s behalf by a federal agency. It chose the wording entirely autonomously without any input from the seller; the customer’s role was relegated to supporting the war effort by adhering to the displayed prices (or failing to do so by disregarding the rules), refusing to overpay or overbuy (even if they could afford to do so), and ensuring the seller (e.g., grocers) followed the rules.

### **Price Communication During the First World War**

The First World War (1914–1918) brought on severe inflation to every economy involved on either side, including the United States. Between 1914 and 1920, American consumer prices doubled (for context, U.S. consumer prices rose by *[about 25 percent in the five years](https://www.bls.gov/cpi/)* after the Covid pandemic).[^1] This was the inflationary backdrop against which the federal government first attempted to communicate price information to American citizens on a national scale, but it did so tentatively and with little conviction.

Viewed alongside the elaborate price communication apparatus of the Second World War to follow, what is most striking about the First World War’s machinery is just how much it leaned on soft persuasive techniques and how little on compulsion. In August 1917, Congress passed the Food and Fuel Control Act, widely known as the Lever Act. The Act created two agencies that controlled a large proportion of retail prices between them: the U.S. Food Administration under Herbert Hoover and the Fuel Administration under Harry Garfield. Both agencies had significant statutory powers, including licensing, margin-setting, and, in Garfield’s case, the direct fixing of coal prices throughout the supply chain, all the way from the mine to the collier.[^2] However, the U.S. Food Administration, which could directly affect the day-to-day lives of American consumers, chose the persuasion route, using its coercive powers sparingly, if at all. It built its price-communication machinery around what the agency called the “weapon of publicity.”[^3]

The phrase describes a specific form of soft social pressure that we would call naming and shaming, or doxxing the wicked, today. It worked as follows. Local fair-price committees first established a public benchmark for everyday necessities such as sugar and flour. Newspapers then published these benchmarks so that buyers and sellers knew what the government considered fair. Volunteer retail price reporters visited stores regularly and identified merchants who charged higher prices. The agency published the scofflaws’ names and relied on public pressure for enforcement; ot didn’t take them to court or punish them. Thus, during the First World War, a merchant identified as a profiteer risked not only public criticism but also a loss of reputation that would be ruinous to them in the longer term. Local administrators described their role as holding “in check the forces of speculation and avariciousness” through exposure rather than by legal force.

When the Food Administration eventually resorted to more stringent enforcement measures, it acted through the offending merchant’s supply chain rather than through the criminal courts. Specifically, federally licensed wholesalers were prohibited from selling to merchants identified as offenders. This prohibition was also widely publicized locally, so that the community knew why a certain store was no longer stocked with groceries. Some grocers avoided harsher penalties by refunding excess profits or donating the money to the Red Cross. These settlements were also used to publicize both the violation and its consequences to the general public.[^4]

One reason for the reliance on publicity-based behavioral control rather than punitive strategies was Herbert Hoover’s preference for voluntary cooperation. Another, more pragmatic reason was that Congress had prohibited compulsory rationing, and consequently, the Food Administration had limited direct enforcement authority. Publicity was likely the most potent tool it had available. As we will see later in this post, during the Second World War, federal price administrators would conclude that publicity and voluntary cooperation were insufficient and that more force was needed to maintain prices.

The First World War’s price communication machinery had two instruments worth considering in greater detail. The first was the *fair-price list*. Local “fair price” committees, meeting in county seats across the country, determined what retail prices were reasonable for consumer staples given prevailing wholesale costs, and published these values in local newspapers. Retail price reporters, who were volunteers, then walked through the stores and reported merchants charging more than the published prices. In both the process and the information conveyed, the fair-price list was analogous to the [price current of the early modern period](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/price-change-communication-in-early). But where the Amsterdam *prijscourant* communicated market prices, the fair-price list communicated what the prices “ought” to be, with the “ought” clearly and confidently stipulated by the government. Thus, the prijscourant’s mechanism was now infused with the price legitimacy vocabulary of virtue in the fair-price list.[^5]

The second instrument of price communication was the *window card*. Participating retailers displayed a placard stating that the store sold its goods at fair prices. The card’s main role was to convey compliance with the prevailing pricing policies and, by doing so, to make the retailer’s participation in the program a badge of honor. We can find variations of this communication approach all around us today, from the “we match our competitors’ prices” sign and the fair-trade certification mark to the “radical transparency” tagline and the little padlock and trust seal on the online checkout payment screen. These signs all cue trustworthiness, with the added message that the trust is derived from an external authority’s abilities.

On the consumers’ side, the Food Administration ran a vast household campaign, enrolling millions of homes whose members signed pledges committing them to conserving food and displayed a “Member of the United States Food Administration” card in their front windows.[^6] The pledge signaled the consumer’s commitment as an active and willing participant in the government’s social enforcement of prices.

This method of publicizing prices and enforcing them had one structural weakness. Actual prices repeatedly exceeded those published in the fair price lists during 1917 and 1918. When this happened, everyone could see the violations during their shopping trips, exposing the government’s impotence in controlling prices and showing that (at least some) merchants could ignore the rules with impunity. During this period, the lack of punitive consequences doubtlessly reduced the credibility of mandated prices. Note that this was the very same “narrative mismatch” problem discussed in the *[second post of this series](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/authority-legitimacy-and-thirteenth-century-shrinkflation)* on Diocletian’s Edict, where the gap between decree and visible practice depleted the authority’s authority.

When the First World War ended, the machinery that sought to control prices was quickly dismantled despite efforts by some officials to retain it. Prices continued to rise throughout 1919 and 1920, and public support for wartime controls weakened significantly after the war ended. This pattern would reappear in a more amplified form in 1946. The main lesson here seems quite obvious but sometimes forgotten, that price legitimacy based on an emergency is difficult to retain once the emergency is over.

### **Price Communication During the Roaring Twenties and the Great Depression**

In the 23 years between the Armistice in November 1918 and Pearl Harbor in December 1941, the primary driver of price communication changed twice. During the 1920s, with the shuttering of the wartime agencies, the initiative in communicating prices shifted decisively back to commercial entities, and specifically to three institutions that had been growing in strength since the industrial age covered in the *[previous post](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/price-change-communication-in-the-industrial-age)*: the national brand, the chain store, and the price ticker. While the first two institutions communicated prices to consumers on a large scale, the third communicated prices to traders, investors, and speculators far and wide, supporting the wild stock market gyrations of the late 1920s and ultimately the 1929 stock market crash that led to the Great Depression.

***1) One-price advertising by national brands.*** The national brand extended the 19^(th) century’s single-price logic across the country during the twenties. Mass magazines and, later, commercial radio allowed manufacturers to advertise branded products at specific prices to a national audience. For instance, a price printed in the *Ladies’ Home Journal* ad expanded the scope of the brand’s public commitment from a narrow, in-person promise to one made simultaneously to readers nationwide. Advertising spending doubled during the decade, and identity-based and price-based ads became common. For the first time, consumers gained price knowledge incidentally while consuming mass media for entertainment and information.[^7]

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/99777a11-e21c-42e2-815c-430b0e4a31a8_1190x1615.png)

Coca-Cola ad Ladies Home Journal December 1921, Source: Wikimedia Commons

***2) Promotional pricing by chain stores.*** Like national brands, chain stores were customer-focused, but they created a different pricing system. Companies such as the Great Atlantic & Pacific Tea Company in groceries and Woolworth’s in general merchandise expanded rapidly by securing large discounts from suppliers and leveraging their relatively advantageous cost structures to charge significantly lower retail prices. They popularized a slew of promotional pricing communications, including the loss leader, the weekly special, and the volume discount. An emphasis on promotions also brought the issue of price transparency to the forefront. While consumers and competitors could see the low advertised prices, the advantageous wholesale price and the machinations behind it were completely opaque. The logic of why the prices were so low and what that meant for the product’s quality and the seller’s business model began to become meaningful to various recipients of the price communication.[^8]

The political response to the retail hype was intense, focusing on both how prices were set and how they were communicated. Anti-chain-store campaigns in more than 40 states sought to impose special taxes on large chains. At the federal level, this movement produced the Robinson-Patman Act of 1936, often called the “anti-chain-store act.” The law prohibited suppliers from giving large buyers discriminatory discounts unless the differences could be justified by lower costs. It also restricted advertising allowances, brokerage payments, and other forms of compensation that could function as hidden discounts. In the context of this series, Robinson-Patman followed the same logic as the Interstate Commerce Act’s ban on railroad rebates that we covered in the [industrial age post](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/price-change-communication-in-the). Both laws sought to reduce the gap between the publicly visible price and the hidden realized price.[^9]

A second, distinct movement around fair trade laws sought to prevent retailers from charging prices that were too low. They were typically state-level statutes allowing manufacturers to establish minimum resale prices for branded goods. Retailers could not discount below the stipulated prices, regardless of their size or purchasing power. These laws initially faced federal antitrust challenges, but the [Miller-Tydings Act of 1937](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/law/miller-tydings-act-legalizes-retail-price-maintenance/) exempted qualifying agreements from the Sherman Act. Fair trade laws argued that resale price maintenance was a matter of fairness. Their very name implied that the manufacturer’s fixed price protected legitimate competition, while the discounter’s lower price leaned on illegitimate forms of competition by threatening independent merchants. Here, then, is an interesting and unusual situation where a fixed price was defended primarily as protection for small retailers rather than as a more efficient way to conduct business and benefit end consumers.

***3) The stock ticker.*** A third form of price communication, involving stock prices rather than prices of products and services, grew rapidly during the 1920s. The stock ticker was a telegraphic machine that printed a running stream of stock symbols and prices onto a narrow paper tape. It had originated much earlier, back in the 1860s, and was in regular use in brokerage houses by the 1910s.[^10] In the 1920s, however, it spread far beyond brokerage houses and significantly influenced the stock-buying behavior of the general public.

As stock ownership spread beyond the major financial centers during the Roaring Twenties, brokerage firms opened branch offices across the country. Many included a customer room with chairs, a large quotation board, and a ticker displaying current prices. Members of the public gathered there during trading hours to watch prices change, many with little connection to finance. They followed individual stocks through the day much as they might follow a sporting event. As Dana Alexander Moyes vividly described it[^11]:

“Brokers’ offices were crowded from 10 A.M. to 3 P.M. with seated or standing customers who, instead of attending to their own business, were watching the blackboard. In some ‘customers’ rooms’ it was difficult to get access to a spot from which the posted quotations could be seen; no one could get a chance to inspect the tape.”

Stock prices were reported and re-reported through various channels. Tickers transmitted them continuously to brokerage offices. Newspapers published the previous day’s closing prices every morning in dense tables showing each stock’s high and low. In this era, the Dow Jones Industrial Average encapsulated the market’s condition by popularizing a single number that anyone could track. The Dow rose from about 63 in 1921 to 381 in September 1929, with each new high being dissected by market experts, investing titans, and everyone else.[^12] The general public was completely plugged into the market, so much so that, as John Kenneth Galbraith described it in his book on the 1929 stock market crash, this way:

“It follows that to be out of touch with the market, ever so briefly, was a nerve-wracking experience. Happily, this was not often necessary. Ticker service was now nationwide; a local telephone call would get the latest quotations from almost anywhere.”

Furthermore, two specific aspects of this era made stock price movements a matter of widespread public concern. The first was the growing practice of buying stocks on margin. This involved paying a fraction of the stock’s price, often around 10 percent, and borrowing the rest from brokers, who in turn borrowed from the banks. The stocks served as collateral for the loan, and margin buying enabled consumers to use leverage, encouraging speculation.[^13] By 1929, brokers’ loans to margin customers exceeded \$8 billion (approximately \$150 billion in today’s dollars), and margin credit accounted for approximately a fifth of the market value of the New York Stock Exchange. Because of leverage, even a moderate price drop could wipe out the speculator’s stake (much as it does for many of [today’s crypto traders](https://www.cnbc.com/2025/10/22/the-biggest-crypto-wipeout-was-led-not-by-bitcoin-but-much-smaller-tokens-heres-what-happened.html)), prompting the broker to send urgent telegrams demanding additional funds and threatening liquidation.[^14]

The second aspect of that era was the concurrent broad adoption of installment credit for consumer goods. During the 1920s, expensive durable goods such as automobiles, refrigerators, radios, and washing machines *[were sold on installment plans](https://americanradioworks.publicradio.org/features/americandream/b1.html)* by the millions, under which the buyer made a down payment and then a series of weekly or monthly payments until the price was paid off. Each of the major automobile manufacturers established its own finance subsidiary to provide credit for its vehicles. In advertising for such goods, the dollar amount given prominence was frequently not the full price but the periodic payment amount, a practice that continues to this day.[^15] In both cases, the price presented to the buyer was expressed in a form that emphasized what the buyer paid at present rather than the full sum owed.

This culture of borrowing to buy stocks and consumer goods magnified the collapse when it came. Share prices fell by more than a third in the closing months of 1929 and continued their downward spiral for nearly three years. Margin loans were called in and could not be repaid, installment purchases went unpaid, and consumer prices fell by approximately 25% between 1929 and 1933, so the central pricing issue of the early thirties became deflation.

***The thirties and the Blue Eagle.*** After the untold suffering of the early thirties, the National Industrial Recovery Act of 1933 created the National Recovery Administration (NRA), whose central instrument was the “code of fair competition.” Industry by industry, codes established minimum wages, maximum permitted hours of work, and price floors in an attempt to halt the price-cutting that policymakers associated with the deflation of the early 1930s. Firms that pledged to abide by these codes were entitled to display the NRA’s emblem, a stylized blue eagle, over the motto *We Do Our Part*, in their windows and on their packages. General Hugh Johnson, who ran the NRA and had served on the War Industries Board in the First World War, promoted the emblem through public campaigns modeled on First World War methods, which included parades, motorcades, radio addresses, and a large procession in New York City. Consumers were urged to patronize only retailers and grocers displaying the emblem; businesses that did not were sometimes boycotted.

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/321e747c-d4da-4acd-b68b-b9642fe7923a_1183x1536.jpeg)

NRA_member, We do our part poster, circa 1933, Wikimedia Foundation

The Blue Eagle worked through the same mechanism as the fair-price window card from the First World War. Without providing detailed price lists or even any specific pricing information, it showed that the retailer had agreed to abide by the code. By displaying the emblem, the retailer essentially aligned its pricing strategy behind the federal government’s authority, making its compliance visible to customers. The emblem’s absence also made the retailer’s nonparticipation visible. The key distinction here was that the problem being solved was deflation rather than inflation. After the Supreme Court struck down the NRA in 1935, the emblem disappeared from common use[^16], but the idea behind it and the fair-price window cars survive in the “certified,” “compliant,” and “member” badges that sellers display even today to indicate that an outside authority has reviewed or approved their conduct with respect to price or other business practices.

The interwar period illustrates two features of price communication that are worth noting. First, when the federal government withdrew from direct price management after the First World War, the gap between the price advertised by the seller and the price actually paid by the buyer once again became politically important. The Robinson-Patman Act, state fair-trade laws, and the NRA codes each attempted to narrow or control that gap using different logic. Second, the window card and the Blue Eagle emblem confirmed the effectiveness of certification by outside authorities in both wartime and peacetime settings. When the United States returned to comprehensive price controls in 1942, it retained this public display approach, adding specific price information for consumers to compare with merchants’ prices.

### **Price Communication During the Second World War**

Compared to the First World War, the Second World War involved larger fiscal deficits and a more complete mobilization of Americans. Yet it produced considerably less inflation. Economists continue to debate the relative importance of taxation, forced saving, war bonds, and price control on prices in the two wars. However, one point that is generally agreed upon is that pricing policy during the Second World War relied on lessons learned from what had worked, and more importantly, what had not worked during the First World War. By 1942, the American federal government had concluded that First World War-style persuasion without enforcement was not enough. The Emergency Price Control Act of January 1942 gave the OPA broad legal powers.[^17] On April 28, 1942, Price Administrator Leon Henderson issued the General Maximum Price Regulation, referred to as “General Max.” It took effect for manufacturers and wholesalers on May 11 and for retailers on May 18.[^18]

The fundamental rule was straightforward. A seller’s maximum price for a commodity or service was set to be the highest price that the seller had charged during March 1942. Instead of calculating new prices or applying general cost formulas, the agency simply froze the seller’s own recent price. If a seller had not charged a price for a given item in March (e.g., they didn’t sell it that month), it had to use the March price of its closest competitor for that item. New commodities were priced using a markup ratio relative to a comparable item.[^19]

For a pricing practitioner, the paperwork required by General Max provides interesting insights. Every seller subject to regulation had to preserve its base-period records and, by July 1, 1942, prepare a statement showing the highest prices charged during March, together with the customary allowances, discounts, and other differentials that affected the amount actually paid. The statement had to remain available for inspection by any person during ordinary business hours. Retailers no longer had the right to maintain the confidentiality of their prior prices or price structures; the only way other sellers could maintain confidentiality was to show that disclosing prices would cause substantial injury.[^20]

Section 1499.13 went further, prescribing the wording of a retail price communication in detail. For the products classified as necessities (referred to as “cost-of-living commodities” in the regulation), such as aspirin, cigarettes, rib roast, bed sheets, and bicycle tires, every retailer had to display the maximum price “in a manner plainly visible to, and understandable by, the purchasing public.” The maximum price had to appear on the item itself or on the shelf, bin, or rack where it was sold, and it could not be obscured by another posted price, a price book, or overlapping price lists. The wording adjacent to the price was also fixed, with retailers required to use either “Ceiling Price \$” or “Our Ceiling \$.” Mail-order sellers were subject to a similar rule. They could print the ceiling price next to each item in the catalog, or place a signed notice on the front cover stating that no listed price exceeded the applicable ceiling and promising to provide the maximum price for any item upon request.[^21] By prescribing the details of price presentation, such as which words to use, where to place those words, the necessary clarity, and the treatment of competing prices, Section 1499.13 laid the foundation for future consumer protection law as it pertains to presenting price information. Today, as with many other things, the devil is in the details when it comes to price disclosure, and many of the rules governing it were established during the Second World War.

The phrase “Ceiling Price” also changed the perceived source of the price by presenting it not as something the merchant could control, but as something imposed by an outside authority. At every point of sale, the grocer had to reiterate this using the government-provided standardized explanation of the pricing mechanism. “Our Ceiling” allowed slightly more of the seller’s own voice into the message, but the word “ceiling” still emphasized that the government was in charge.

***Price controls and rationing.***** **As has been *[discussed by many experts](https://www.jec.senate.gov/public/_cache/files/7171cb80-ef0d-4058-b3e6-f20fe608745f/the-economics-of-price-controls-final-092122.pdf)* in *[recent years](https://www.hoover.org/research/price-controls-still-bad-idea)*, price controls can quickly become problematic when demand at the controlled price substantially exceeds supply. The OPA tried to circumvent this problem by pairing price ceilings for products with rationing them. This has been described as a two-price system, in that legally purchasing these goods required the buyer to use two currencies: money and a specified number of allocated points. Its usefulness is explained by John Kenneth Galbraith this way[^22]:

“There has never been any doubt, in theory, of the ability of a price control authority to maintain a fixed price in a particular market if the price-fixing is supplemented by rationing. Rationing, if properly administered, has the well-understood effect of limiting demand to what is available at the fixed price and thus establishing a special market equilibrium that is wholly stable. During World War II in the United States a considerable number of consumer’s goods – mainly foods – were rationed, and a large number of producer’s goods and materials were subject, through the allocation of the entire supply to specified uses, to an equivalent control.”

Beginning in May 1942, sugar was rationed in the United States, followed by coffee in November. Broader food rationing was implemented on March 1, 1943, with the introduction of the *[War Ration Book Two](https://www.sechicagohistory.org/archive/browse/war-ration-book-two/)*, which worked on a point system. The book provided sheets of stamps to consumers in red and blue colors. Blue stamps were to be used to purchase approximately 300 processed foods, including canned and bottled goods, as well as dried and frozen items. Red stamps were to be used for meat, butter, cheese, and cooking oils. The stamps came in different denominations, and every American consumer received a monthly allowance of points. In the store, every rationed product displayed two prices, one in money and the other in points. The money price was dictated by price-ceiling regulations, while the point price was set independently by rationing authorities, who adjusted it periodically in response to supply. This complex two-price system impacted virtually every American consumer because, as John Kenneth Galbraith explained[^23]:

“What had previously been an expedient designed to meet a particular situation, and one that more often than not was confined in its application to a particular commodity or service, became in the Second World War a comprehensive policy. Controls over prices and wages were the rule; freedom from such regulation was the exception.”

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/bd33409a-9f00-4e69-8a43-12ec1370094b_1442x1025.jpeg)

Advertisement Explaining How to Shop With Ration Book Two, Source: Wikimedia Commons

This system imposed a substantial communication burden on both grocers and shoppers. Grocers had to label every rationed item with its money price and point price, then relabel everything in their store each time the OPA changed the number of points required in response to changing supply. In February 1944, the agency simplified the stamp currency by making each stamp worth 10 points and introducing 1-point tokens that could be returned as change. This revision solved a significant practical problem in which shoppers had received no change before when the stamps they used for payment exceeded the purchase’s point value.

Throughout the period when rationing was in effect (about three years for most goods, five-plus years for sugar), shoppers had to manage a second currency whose value changed periodically. As historian *[Richard Lingeman put it](https://www.amazon.com/Dont-Know-Theres-Nation-Books/dp/1560254653/)*:

“For the housewife, the rationing system meant the mastery of a constantly changing system of point values in the papers; while shopping, she kept one eye peeled on the monetary price and the other on the little red numerals posted on the shelf below products indicating their point price. She practiced double budgeting: money and points. She had to keep track of which stamps were valid during a certain time period, which were outdated, and what they might buy.”

To disentangle this complexity, newspapers printed rationing calendars showing which stamps were valid at any given time, and OPA point charts were reprinted and posted in stores.

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/df812457-554c-4329-96ae-63bdf50dd8a9_1024x789.jpeg)

*Butcher’s case showing ceiling prices and point values per pound, 1943. Source: Alfred T. Palmer, Office of War Information, Library of Congress*

***Consumers’ role in enforcing prices**.* To enforce ceiling prices across millions of grocers and retailers, the OPA employed approximately 60,000 paid staff and relied on 200,000 volunteers working through nearly 6,000 local War Price and Rationing Boards.[^24] These Boards were community-level bodies that administered both rationing and price control for their own towns and neighborhoods and were staffed by area residents.

In March 1943, the OPA’s administrator, Prentiss Brown, directed each board to establish a price panel staffed by volunteer assistants, most of them women recruited locally. An assistant was assigned a set of twelve designated food staples, and recorded the prices for these items in neighborhood stores, then compared them against OPA ceilings, and returned the next week to verify that above-ceiling prices had been corrected. The model had been developed in Connecticut by Chester Bowles, who ran the state’s price program before succeeding Brown as national OPA administrator later in 1943, at which point he extended the price-panel system nationwide.[^25]

The Home Front Pledge campaign supported this work. In its best-known poster, a woman in an apron raises her hand beside the words: *“Keep the home front pledge: pay no more than ceiling prices, pay your points in full.” (See image at the beginning of this post).* Another poster made the request more explicit. It asked customers to pay no more than ceiling prices and to volunteer for price panel work.[^26]

During the war, the U.S. government asked buyers to take an active role in monitoring and maintaining prices they helped establish. It told them not to pay more than the ceiling price, described that refusal as patriotic, and encouraged them to report sellers who overcharged. The emphasis on patriotism also served a practical administrative purpose. An agency with 60,000 employees could not patrol several million retail establishments on its own. What it could do was mandate that clear ceiling prices be posted in every store for cost-of-living commodities and teach customers to spot an overcharge and act on it. Thus, the communication was itself part of the enforcement. The OPA campaign provides a compelling instance of something we have seen throughout the series: the idea that the credibility of a price claim is bolstered by the audience’s ability to verify it.

***Structural facilitators of price control**.* John Kenneth Galbraith served as the OPA’s deputy administrator for price policy from 1941 until he left in 1943 under pressure from Congress and industry. He spent much of the next decade analyzing the experience and narrated his account in *A Theory of Price Control* (1952), a short book he later called the best he had written and the least read.[^27]

Galbraith’s central argument was that price control worked far better than prewar economists had predicted, and that it worked best in a particular kind of market. Before the war, most economists held that fixing prices was both unwise and, in the face of excess demand, impossible. This is because a controlled price below the market-clearing level would simply give buyers and sellers a shared incentive to trade above it, and the black market would grow until it became the only market. Galbraith argued that this prediction was more applicable to certain types of markets and less so for others. It applied to markets that approached pure competition, with many small buyers and sellers, but failed for the imperfect or oligopolistic markets that make up most of the modern economy, where a small number of large sellers already administered their own prices.

In a market with few sellers, the price is set by the seller rather than discovered by buyers through bargaining. In such cases, sellers have reasons of their own to keep prices stable and to obey the ceiling. Such markets also tend to carry excess capacity and inflexible, norm-based or convention-based prices. Thus, a price freeze mostly ratifies an existing price structure. Buyers and sellers are already accustomed to the price level and its associated discounts and differentials (what we would call “price waterfall structures” today). This makes an existing structure far easier to hold through controls than to check the upward surge in a competitive market. Galbraith reduced this powerful insight to a one-sentence aphorism: “*It is relatively easy to fix prices that are already fixed.*”

He also offered a concrete illustration of his point. The OPA controlled the prices of all steel mill products with far less manpower and trouble than it needed for a much smaller dollar volume of steel scrap, because steel prices were administered by a few large firms, whereas scrap was priced in fragmented buying settings involving bargaining among many buyers and sellers.[^28]

The same distinction applies to price communication, which is of greater interest to us. A price that is administered and changed infrequently is noticed when it changes. The buyer knows or seeks out information about the old price, the new price, the effective date, and the identifiable party responsible for the decision. (As a thought experiment, imagine what would happen if Costco changed its \$1.50 price for a hot dog and soda.) On the other hand, a change in a price that varies frequently, is personalized, or opaque will draw less attention.

Analyzed in these terms, the wartime state communicated prices effectively because it first made them communicable. The General Maximum Price Regulation converted a mass of negotiated, discounted, and fluctuating transaction prices into a single stated ceiling for every item in every store, which had to be posted, and then checked and disputed. That conversion, more than any persuasive messaging, was key to the success of price controls in this period. Persuasive methods such as posters and events worked only because prices were verifiable.

Galbraith’s second lesson is about consent. He pointed out that price controls could not, and did not, remove the underlying unmet demand; they simply held it in abeyance, as a levee holds back water. What kept the pressure of demand in check was a combination of taxation, saving, rationing, and a public willingness to accept the controls, which in turn rested on the widely held belief that the sacrifice was shared and temporary. When the war ended, that supporting belief weakened quickly. The campaign to liquidate price controls in the summer of 1946 and the meat shortage that followed the lifting of meat ceilings brought the OPA to an end.[^29] We saw the same thing at the end of the First World War: a price justified by an emergency loses legitimacy when the emergency is over.

***Price communication in Great Britain during the 2^(nd) World War.*** Interestingly, Great Britain faced the same challenge as the United States during the Second World War and adopted many of the same solutions, such as price ceilings, rationing, and a large administrative apparatus. But it solved the price communication problem in a different way. There, food rationing began in January 1940 with bacon, butter, and sugar. Lord Woolton, a former department store executive, became Minister of Food in April and led a ministry with about 50,000 employees and more than 1,000 local offices.[^30] Woolton understood that rationing had to be explained and accepted by British citizens for it to work over the long term. He used radio broadcasts, prepared households for food shortages before they occurred, and even attached his name to *[a vegetable pie](https://the1940sexperiment.com/2016/03/13/the-original-lord-woolton-pie-recipe-no-151/)*.

The ministry’s *Food Facts* advertisements, which appeared in national newspapers from 1940 onward, came closest to a modern mass advertising campaign for explaining price and product changes. The ads used a consistent format in paid space beside commercial advertising. They combined practical instructions with appeals to wartime duty.[^31]

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/cd57e07f-cba2-4218-a001-6d58de6c8707_966x2657.jpeg)

Food Facts Newspaper Advertisement September 1941, Warwick Digital Collection

The British approach also leaned on commercial brands to carry the government’s explanation. Wartime controls stripped manufacturers of much of their control over their own products. Under “zoning” regulations, a manufacturer could only sell its products within an assigned region, leading to national brands disappearing from store shelves in a significant portion of the country. Its “pooling” regulations required firms to combine their output and distribute it jointly, erasing the distinctions between competing brands. And “utility” schemes specified what a product had to contain and how it could be made, leaving manufacturers little control over the ingredients and formulations of their products. These constraints also led to significant changes in advertising. For intance, academic research on wartime advertisements in the British newspapers *Daily Express* and *Daily Mirror* has found that many brands used paid ads to announce withdrawals of their products from the marketplace, to explain why certain sweets and desserts were available in limited areas, and explain to customers why the formulation of some of the most familiar products had changed. Cadbury, for example, explained that milk shortages required a special ration chocolate in place of its usual bar.[^32]

### **The Impact of the World War Era on the Five Price Vocabularies**

Recall the working hypothesis that is foundational to this series. I proposed that over time, sellers have drawn on five distinct vocabularies to legitimize a price or a price change. A price or price change can be justified by authority, by the seller’s virtue or fair dealing, by the necessity of costs (or other inputs) beyond the seller’s control, by an impersonal mechanism that sets the price, or by an appeal to future value that the price will help deliver to the buyer. I also proposed that each emerged in a particular period, and once available, none of the five has ever fully disappeared; later communicators reach back to whichever suits their purpose, using a slate of vocabularies to suit it.

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/7c1bc3a3-e1c5-448e-bad2-87ff3fbefdac_1280x720.png)

Five Vocabularies of Price Change Communication

All five vocabularies were already in use at the start of the twentieth century; what the two World Wars and the intervening turbulent period did was change their relative significance. Authority and virtue took center stage, as necessity became less important, mechanism became part of the standard explanation, and future value largely disappeared from popular notice during this era. The process of legitimizing prices and price changes changed significantly when the state became the prime mover in justifying prices to its citizens.

With the advent of the First World War and then continuing again through the Second World War (with a respite in between when the market ruled), authority asserted its dominance in forms that would have looked familiar to Diocletian nearly two thousand years ago. The state set maximum prices, published them, and punished sellers (in different ways in the two wars) who didn’t comply. The main difference between Diocletian’s era of the third century and this one of the twentieth century was that the states invariably had the resources, administrative capacity, and competence to enforce their dictates. For instance, General Max was effective because it imposed criminal penalties and license suspensions on sellers who violated it, while the OPA harnessed the merchants’ own records, the communal and patriotic instincts of the local boards, and the general public in ways that Diocletian’s Edict was never able to do.[^33] The vocabularies of virtue and authority both became prominent during both wars. Virtue was recast in the specific mold of patriotism and directed mainly at the consumer rather than the merchant. Adhering to the government-set prices became an expression of loyalty to one’s country, and paying more than the ceiling price just to get more of the rationed items was seen as shameful and mean, a gross failure of one’s role as a good citizen.

Contrarily, the significance of the necessary vocabulary declined considerably relative to authority and virtue in this period. The wartime state rarely argued that a price had to rise because input costs had risen or fall because costs had fallen. Instead, its stark message was that prices had to be kept from rising to support the country’s war effort and each other’s welfare. Mechanism played a key role in creating and implementing the price structures used during the wars to manage limited food supplies. Everything from the March 1942 base date used to establish price ceilings, the point values used to allocate rations, the formulas for new commodities, and the required use of “Ceiling Price” were all part and parcel of mechanism. They located the justification for prices squarely with the government and communicated the logic behind them transparently to merchants and consumers alike. The future value vocabulary that rules today was almost entirely absent in this era, supplanted by sacrifice and shared pain.

### **Conclusion**

I will conclude this post with three insightful lessons for pricing practitioners from this era. First, when communicating prices and justifying them to the audience, verifiability matters far more than the method of communication. We have seen this idea throughout this post, with the OPA’s implementation providing a vivid illustration. OPA used blunt, in-your-face messaging, with numerous executions such as posters, banners, etc. Many modern price communicators would call this propaganda. But it worked because it was accompanied by clearly verifiable prices and a clear understanding of how those prices were derived and whose authority lay behind them. Virtually all modern price communications fall short in this regard, providing euphemistic verbiage about “price adjustments” but without means of verifying the company’s assertions or, often, even clear explanations.

Second, beyond the content of a communication, the form of a price communication can itself be a policy decision variable. We saw that Section 1499.13 focused on the nitty-gritty of price presentation, such as what wording to use, where to place the numbers, and so on. It treated how a price should be shown as seriously as what the price was. Today, this very question arises in legal squabbles over drip pricing, junk fees, and subscription renewals. The main message is that everyone involved with pricing, managers, frontline employees, entrepreneurs, and consultants alike, needs to pay attention to the form of pricing communications.

Third, price communications don’t always have to be about price increases. In the main case in this post, an extensive, federally run, nationwide operation was implemented to persuade Americans *not* to accept a price increase. It’s worth noting that such an arrangement is historically unique. Every other communicator we have covered so far in this series, from Diocletian carving his stipulated prices on stone columns to the 1880s department store establishing fixed price policies nationally with price tags, has wanted buyers to buy at their stated prices. Furthermore, with state backing, consumers were in charge during the two wars. Various mechanisms have emerged since then, including regulatory agencies that support consumer welfare (CFPB and BCP), price comparison sites, and price-tracking tools. But they have nowhere near succeeded in shifting the balance of power to the consumers’ side.
