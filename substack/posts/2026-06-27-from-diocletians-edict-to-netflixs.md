##### ***Summary.*** From Diocletian’s stone edicts to Netflix’s carefully worded emails communicating price increases, this post introducing a new series traces how price changes have been communicated across history. It argues that price-change communication has always involved legitimacy, power, blame, transparency, and audience resistance, and introduces five recurring vocabularies of pricing legitimacy: authority, virtue, necessity, mechanism, and future value.

Diocletian was the emperor of Rome from 284 to 305 CE and is [credited with restoring](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Diocletian) Roman government to stability and efficiency after a century of political, military, and economic turmoil. Near the end of 301 CE, he issued an Edict on Maximum Prices, which set maximum prices for over 1,200 goods and services, including commodities, animals, services, and wages. The Edict was inscribed in stone and posted in public squares throughout the eastern Roman empire. The very first passage from the preamble of his Edict reads as follows[^1]:

“We must check the limitless and furious avarice which with no thought for mankind hastens to its own gain. This avarice, with no thought of the common need, is ravaging the wealth of those in extremes of need. We — the protectors of the human race — have agreed that justice should intervene as arbiter, so that the solution which mankind itself could not supply might, by the remedies of our foresight, be applied to the general betterment of all… Therefore, we have decreed that there be established a maximum so that when the violence of high prices appears anywhere, avarice might be checked by the limits of our statute[^2].”

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/b859cc17-96d6-413b-b701-aaeb6d6328e4_1187x1029.jpeg)

Piece of Edict on Maximum Prices: A molded Copy in the Antikensammlung Berlin/Pergamonmuseum, Property of the Münzkabinett Berlin, Source: Matthias Kabel, Wikimedia Commons.

The Edict made it crystal clear that anyone caught selling at prices higher than the listed maximums was to be executed. In some versions of the discovered Edict fragments, capital punishment also applied to anyone caught buying at black-market prices and to any seller who tried to withdraw goods from the market in protest[^3]. In a nutshell, the Edict laid out a detailed narrative for the price change, identifying the villains (greedy sellers), the injured (the public, and \*wink wink\* the just Emperor himself), the solution (price controls backed by justice), and the consequences (capital punishment).

This next passage is a statement from Netflix in its letter to investors dated January 21, 2025, reporting on its Q4 2024 performance and describing its goals going forward[^4]:

“As we continue to invest in programming and deliver more value for our members, we will occasionally ask our members to pay a little more so that we can re-invest to further improve Netflix.”

A customer email announcing and explaining an earlier price increase in December 2023 was consistent with this ask. In an email message titled “We’re updating our prices – here’s why,” the company explained:

“We hope you’re enjoying everything Netflix has to offer. We’re updating the price of the Premium plan to \$22.99, bringing your new monthly total to \$22.99 on December 20th, 2023…This update will allow us to deliver even more value for your membership — with stories that lift you up, move you, or simply make your day a little better.”

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/45536a49-495b-41e9-a9a7-428bbef76866_651x1084.jpeg)

Excerpts from email sent by Netflix to customers, November 2023

These two communications, Diocletian’s Edict and Netflix’s email, were issued seventeen centuries apart and came from entirely different worlds. One capped prices to curb inflation in the name of quelling greed. The other raised prices in the name of serving customers more effectively. Yet they share many interesting similarities worth exploring. Each explains a price change, and the writers are well aware that they are about to convey rather unpleasant news to their constituents. Each expects a negative reaction and resistance from those affected and tries to forestall it.

Both communications provide an explanation. Specifically, within the context of their social norms and the broader environment, each had to justify the price change, in effect, arguing that it was neither arbitrary nor unfair. The communication had to make the change appear legitimate and reasonable, and we might even say, benign, to those who would have to bear its consequences. The Roman emperor resorts to moral indignation against merchants and to the threat of capital punishment. Our present-day FAANG mega-corporation reaches for banal customer experience standbys, asserting that it does what’s best for customers by investing, delivering value, and improving continuously.

The vocabulary, the audience, the power dynamic, the stakes, and the ultimate goals are all very different in these two instances. But the basic communicative task of explaining a price change to people who have to live with it and will be affected by it is the same. In a series of forthcoming posts, I want to explore the phenomenon of communicating price changes, mostly price increases, across the centuries. This first one serves as the introduction, setting the stage for the series.

### Tracing price-change communications through history

I have written before on this blog about specific historical cases and research in pricing, including [the medieval Catholic Church’s sale of indulgences](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/when-the-coin-in-the-coffer-rings?), [Robert Lanzillotti’s 1958 study of pricing restraint in large American firms](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/when-firms-wielded-their-pricing?), and [Eli Ginzberg’s 1936 work on the left-digit bias](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/tracing-the-history-of-left-digit?). Readers of these posts will know I have an enduring interest in finding useful lessons for today’s pricing practitioners from older sources covering pricing strategy. Every chance to confirm the truism that “there is nothing new under the sun” is also a chance to learn and understand current pricing practices more deeply.

This new series is a deliberate, longer-form extension of this interest to the specific domain of the history of price-change communications. Over the centuries, these communications have taken various forms, from the imperial proclamation to the guild ordinance to the posted notice to the printed price current to the trade journal column, the patriotic poster, the [“Dear Valued Customer” letter](https://nj.pseg.com/-/media/pseg/public-site/documents/paymentassistance/bgs-electric-rate-increase-21225.ashx), the subscriber email, and, most recently, the in-app alert and [the “your price is going up” text](https://www.reddit.com/r/Frugal/comments/1k1t137/i_just_got_a_text_from_aaa_auto_ins_saying_my/). What I want to trace across all of them is the question of how sellers, and the various entities that set prices on their behalf, have explained themselves when the price went up, or in some cases, when the amount or quality went down while the nominal price remained unchanged.

We will move through the history of price communications chronologically in these posts. I want to go as far back as possible, and begin in antiquity and the medieval world, where the most visible surviving formal price communications came from some authority, whether it was an emperor, guild, or religious institution, and the communication typically occurred via public proclamation, and was often accompanied by threats of severe punishment for non-compliance. Newly forming markets, customs, haggling, local scarcity, and temporary abundance all played significant roles in the price communication process at this time.

Diocletian’s Edict is a prototypical example of this form of price-change communication. Other examples include the price decrees codified in the Code of Hammurabi, nearly two thousand years before Diocletian[^5], and even older Mesopotamian price regulations from Eshnunna. The medieval English Assize of Bread provides a different and especially insightful example from this era. It linked the weight of a bread loaf to the price of grain, in such a way that when wheat prices rose, the loaf’s price would remain unchanged while the loaf shrank according to a public formula, an ancient publicly-declared precursor to today’s shrinkflation[^6].

From there, the series will move into the early modern period, when merchants began writing letters and printers began publishing commodity price currents, essentially news sheets listing prices for a variety of traded goods, exchange rates, and other market information that look surprisingly modern in their structure and substance. Historians of commercial and financial journalism have traced the earliest surviving commodity price currents to the 1530s in Antwerp. These documents are fascinating because they quote prices as facts, effectively stating that these are the market prices for the given products. In a nutshell, the price currents shifted responsibility from a named authority to the market itself[^7].

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/dc36f322-bbf2-43b7-8ead-146f05575479_943x2751.jpeg)

Antwerp General Commodity Price Current, 1797. Published by Mollet & Co. Source: International Institute of Social History. This is a later descendant of the price-current tradition that began in sixteenth-century European commercial centers.

Then we will examine price communication through the industrial age, when trade journals, the telegraph, and posted-price retailing laid the foundation for the price communication we know today. The ubiquitous price tag merits special attention. Posting a price on a product carries moral and institutional connotations, signaling that the price is fixed and offered on the same terms to all customers. Fixing the price publicly also opened the door for a plethora of public price changes, such as discounts, coupons, loyalty programs, personalized offers, and hidden fees, the mainstays of today’s pricing strategy, and diverse ways to communicate these changes.

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/a0ea822f-d703-4d4a-9f2a-59b1b7d1316b_1672x941.png)

A small-town grocery ad evokes late-19th-century price communication when public, uniform, and highly visible prices became common.

Next, we will examine price-change communications in the twentieth century, focusing on the two World Wars. Price-change communication during this time was issued primarily by national governments and used a variety of means, including posters, radio announcements, pamphlets, ration books, enforcement notices, and public campaigns. Wartime price-change communications explained the logic behind controlling prices and rationing goods and services, and exhorting citizens to support these painful policies. Patriotism became an important underlying motivation, driving price-change communications in this era.

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/a8f69442-428e-4a24-b9bc-856050de46ae_960x1306.jpeg)

Poster on Cost of Living 1918-1944. Office for Emergency Management. Office of War Information. Domestic Operations Branch. Bureau of Special Services. (03/09/1943 - 09/15/1945). Source: Wikimedia Commons.

Following that, we will consider the stagflation era of the 1970s. This is when the B2B “Dear Valued Customer” letter took on its modern form, and when practices such as fuel surcharges, cost-pass-through explanations, and price-escalation clauses in long-term commercial contracts became common due to the volatility of input costs. During this period, necessity was commonly given as the justification for price increases.

Then, we will examine the deregulation and revenue management era spanning the 1980s through the 2000s, when airlines, cable companies, and eventually Amazon transformed the very notion of a fixed, justified price into a volatile, shifting, negotiable number. During this period, the battle between customer outrage at frequent, unfair, and illogical price changes and companies’ attempts to leverage technology to change prices more frequently took center stage. The communication shifted from explaining a new price to explaining an entire pricing system, and from dealing with concerns about fairness to addressing customer value logic.

Finally, we will conclude this exploration with the current platform and AI era of the past fifteen years, where even the most straightforward price communication is segmented, A/B tested a dozen different ways, legally reviewed, localized, personalized, and delivered through a plethora of channels. It may arrive as a new fee, a modified tier with fewer benefits, a reduced level of service, a smaller package, a more restrictive usage policy, or a redesigned bundle. The defining characteristic of today’s price communication often lies in what the seller leaves unsaid or unspecified, rather than in what is explained or justified to the buyer.

### Five vocabularies of pricing legitimacy

To maintain a common structure across these investigations, each post will focus on several core issues, starting with the communicator. As we will see, the main actor has changed over the centuries and at various times has been an emperor, a guild, a merchant, a retailer, a regulator, a sales rep, a brand, a platform, or an algorithmic system. The second core issue has to do with the audience. Paralleling the communicator, the target audience has shifted from the peasants and subjects to customers, merchants, regulators, channel partners, journalists, and the general public, the so-called citizens. Of course, throughout history, unintended audiences have played a starring role in many cases, with unintended consequences.

The third core issue concerns the legitimacy behind the justification for price increases (and, in rare cases, decreases) stated in the price communication. The main argument has relied on many different motivations, from costs and compassion to the rejection of greed or other virtues, to shifting or changed valuation, patriotism, the health of society, necessity, and sometimes nothing discernible. What is deemed ok and what leads to pushback, and why, are important questions across history.

Fourth is the issue of transparency of price communications. The earliest price communications were public. Diocletian’s Edict was literally carved in stone and displayed in public squares, while Price Currents circulated freely among European trading communities, generating common knowledge of prices. A shared knowledge based on a transparency-generating system was the cornerstone of historical pricing communications. In contrast, many of today’s price communications are completely hidden or personalized, and customers receive different prices for the same product or service, often without explanation or justification for the variation. For many customers, the shared basis for price knowledge that existed in the past has been replaced with a bewildering, lonely sense of confusion when trying to understand why prices are what they are.

Finally, I will consider the implicit relationship, especially the nature of power, between the communicator and their audience. This is an important issue, and in today’s world, we tend to wave our hands and pay it less attention than we should (e.g., it’s a free country; if you don’t like Netflix’s price increase, drop the subscription and go watch HBO Max or Apple TV+). The reality is that much of what we view as “communication style” across history is really a proxy for how much power the recipient has (or doesn’t have) to do something about the change. Diocletian could threaten merchants with execution because he had the heft to follow through; Netflix has no such luxury, it has to live with manifested outrage, and its CEO has to answer furious investors at the next quarterly call if too many subscribers drop their subscription. The point is that, relative to the past, today’s pricing communication seems much more indirect, deferential, and essentially inauthentic, because the balance of power between the two parties is more even than it used to be.

My hypothesis is that, at its core, the history of price-change communication is a story of the changing legitimacy of the communicator and the development and use of new vocabularies to make price changes acceptable in each era[^8]. Over time, these vocabularies have multiplied, and at present we can identify five broad categories used to justify price changes, summarized in the figure below.

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/5d2b4e0c-b945-46b2-80a3-a764497ca9c7_1280x720.png)

The Five Vocabularies of Pricing Communication

***Authority.*** The earliest approach, common from antiquity through the medieval period, was a vocabulary of authority. The seller (or the institution setting prices on the seller’s behalf) communicated the price change as a matter of fact, with no choice or flexibility given to the recipient. The edict, the ordinance, the posted rule, and the regulatory notice all belong here. The authority could arise from law, custom, or administrative necessity.

***Virtue.*** The second vocabulary of pricing legitimacy, layered on top of authority by the high medieval period and especially salient through the early modern era, was one of virtue, often centered around justice. According to this justification, virtue requires that this be the price; to charge more or to charge less is a sin that will be punished by God or the King or the entity with authority. In Diocletian’s Edict, for example, authority was supported by justice. He set maximum prices using his authority as Emperor, and believed they were just because they rejected avarice, supported the common good, and mitigated harm to society.

***Necessity.*** The third vocabulary, which became dominant during the industrial age and resonates in many quarters even today, is one of necessity. This justification says that this is the price because rising costs, scarcity, war, taxes, supply chain disruptions, less or more immigration, or any number of other factors have left no alternative. This is the language of the cost pass-through (e.g., Don’t blame me, I am not responsible, your groceries cost more because gas prices have risen to \$4.50 per gallon).

***Mechanism.*** The fourth vocabulary of pricing legitimacy is one of mechanism. This is the one I think pricing practitioners should study more carefully, as it has been invoked throughout the ages and remains a potent means of communicating price changes today. A mechanism justification shifts the responsibility for the price change away from the communicator to the mechanism, in effect saying, “I did not increase this price, I am not responsible. Instead, it was \_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_ that was responsible, the blank being any one or more of: the formula, the index, the contract, the market, the meter, the auction, the yield-management system, the algorithm, and the list goes on. There are virtually infinite mechanisms that can be held accountable. It is an effective approach because it obfuscates agency, diffusing it or putting it in a black box, making it hard for the audience to ascribe blame, demand a reversal, or express outrage.

***Future Value.*** Finally, the fifth vocabulary, which dominates today and is evident in Netflix’s email, is future value, often using an aggressive form of performative empathy. This justification goes something like, “We’ve held off as long as we could, we want to give you the best, we really care about you, we want to invest for you… yada yada yada… but the only way we can do amazing things for you is if you pay the higher price we just raised for our product.” This form is the most common today, but it leaves a lot to be desired for the recipient.

An interesting aspect of these vocabularies of price legitimacy that I want to probe further in this series is their persistence and overlapping use. Although each justification seems to have appeared sequentially, they all exist today and are used liberally, even sometimes all at once. Take the example of a SaaS company that is about to raise its prices. It may use all five vocabularies in a single email to its customer. It may lead off with authority by saying the new price takes effect on a specified date. It may add virtue by pointing out that “we have held prices steady for three years and have tried to be fair to customers.” It may invoke necessity, pointing out that cloud infrastructure, talent, cybersecurity, and compliance costs have all risen due to higher inflation over the past two years. It may use the vocabulary of mechanism, by stipulating that your new price is tied to usage, seats, a published tier, or a contractually specified escalation clause. And finally, it may conclude its notice with a call to future value by assuring the customer that “we have added features, integrations, reporting, support, and security, and the price increase will allow us to develop innovative features of value to you.”

This layering of multiple price legitimacy vocabularies can be effective, but it can just as easily create a credibility problem. Today’s savvy customers know that many price increase notifications are written to sound caring, but their main goal is to protect or even increase the company’s profit margin, and increases based on cost pass-throughs are rarely reversed when costs fall. Greedflation is a thing. Using too many vocabularies, chock-full of justifications, may make the communication feel too loaded and inauthentic, and in the long run, lead to more harm than good for the communicator. How many, and which, vocabularies are most effective in a given context is a useful and practically important consideration.

We will explore these five vocabularies of pricing legitimacy in greater detail in the rest of the series, considering the contexts in which they were used and the extent to which they were effective, beginning with pricing communications in antiquity and the medieval world. Through these investigations, we will seek to uncover the most insightful lessons for communicating price changes within a framework that recognizes power, blame, fairness, and acceptance among the involved parties.
