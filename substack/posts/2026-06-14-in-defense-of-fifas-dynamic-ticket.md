##### ***Summary***. FIFA’s 2026 World Cup ticket pricing has been attacked as another case of dynamic pricing gone wrong. In this piece, I argue that this criticism may be misplaced for several reasons. FIFA’s dynamic pricing approach did raise prices over time, but those increases also reflected better information and greater value for later buyers, choppy demand, host-market realities, and secondary-market pressures. FIFA’s real pricing challenge lay less in the price fluctuations everyone is angry about and more in things like opaque ticket allocation, potentially misleading seat maps, and an extraordinarily complex pricing task burdened by a conflicting set of objectives.

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/e2826650-a91a-4d00-b999-86ef064cf6b1_1672x941.png)

Every time there’s a major sporting event, the media headlines are awash with outrage about the pricing strategy, usually pointing to some outrageously priced tickets. For example, before this year’s Super Bowl, there were numerous stories about [how expensive](https://www.usatoday.com/story/sports/nfl/super-bowl/2026/01/27/super-bowl-60-ticket-prices-trends-when-to-buy/88379729007/) it was to [buy a Super Bowl ticket](https://www.espn.com/nfl/story/_/id/47820271/tickets-super-bowl-lx-high-cost-seahawks-patriots-nfl). The same for the [2026 NBA Finals](https://www.espn.com/nba/story/_/id/48986912/new-york-knicks-2026-nba-finals-madison-square-garden-ticket-prices). And this has been the case for the 2026 FIFA World Cup, as the sampling of recent media headlines illustrates.

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/368c4b2e-b294-4a25-9d9a-8517b1984449_1280x676.png)

Headlines about FIFA’s ticket pricing from April - June 2026.

In most of the coverage about FIFA’s ticket pricing this time around, [dynamic pricing](https://www.nytimes.com/athletic/6593901/2025/09/03/world-cup-2026-tickets-fifa-dynamic-pricing/) (or variable pricing, I will treat them as interchangeable here and use “dynamic pricing”[^1]) is seen as the main culprit for negative outcomes such as [price gouging](https://www.wsj.com/sports/soccer/fifa-world-cup-price-gouging-bee6a171), high prices for [certain games](https://www.wsj.com/sports/soccer/fifa-world-cup-price-gouging-bee6a171), limited access to affordable tickets for authentic fans, many [empty seats at matches](https://www.nbcnews.com/sports/soccer/empty-seats-world-cups-opening-day-renew-ticket-price-concerns-rcna349748), and so on.

Generally speaking, I am not a [big fan of dynamic pricing](https://hbr.org/2015/12/everyone-hates-ubers-surge-pricing-heres-how-to-fix-it), because I think many of its features [can harm customers](https://hbr.org/2015/07/the-risks-of-changing-your-prices-too-often). However, in the present case, dynamic pricing seems to have generally worked well for FIFA and for soccer fans, and it seems to me that much of the media outrage may be misplaced and visceral. More importantly, I think many of the complaints stem from either an improper or incomplete understanding of the dynamic pricing concept or of how FIFA’s pricing strategy works. In this post, I want to explore FIFA’s dynamic ticket pricing for the 2026 World Cup in detail, studying its specific features and the ticket allocation and sales process to understand its ramifications for price levels, consumer behavior, and consumer welfare.

### FIFA’s adoption of dynamic pricing is a significant change from the past

The first important point to understand is that FIFA’s dynamic pricing approach for the 2026 World Cup is significantly different from that for previous World Cups and recent Olympic Games. While dynamic pricing has been [used extensively](https://sports.yahoo.com/nba/article/death-of-the-cheap-seat-how-sports-figured-out-they-could-charge-you-almost-anything-for-a-ticket-153025119.html) for pricing [Major League Baseball](https://www.mlb.com/royals/tickets/dynamic-pricing) games, as well as for many [NBA](https://lakersnation.com/lakers-ticket-prices-and-how-dynamic-pricing-impacts-what-you-will-pay/), [NHL](https://ideas.repec.org/h/spr/semchp/978-3-319-67922-8_9.html), and [NFL](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mnsc.2022.4588) games, it hasn’t been used for World Cups and Olympic Games until now. In a September 2025 call with reporters, a FIFA official perfunctorily remarked that they would use “variable pricing” and would “adapt prices as per the demand we see, as per the remaining inventory,”[^2] introducing dynamic pricing to World Cup ticket pricing. Before we get to the details about how they applied dynamic pricing, I want to look at how complex sports events of this stature are usually priced.

### The 2026 World Cup price structure is actually quite conventional

I have taught Marco Bertini’s fantastic case on the [2012 London Olympic Games](https://hbsp.harvard.edu/product/510039-PDF-ENG) in my MBA pricing strategies class for many years. It is full of excellent insights into how to formulate a pricing strategy for something which seems unbelievably complex – pricing millions of tickets to earn hundreds of millions in revenue, for an event that occurs only once every four years, and typically just once for a host nation[^3]. There’s little room for error in this pricing task, but there’s also the temptation to adopt a transactional mindset and [maximize revenue](https://sports.yahoo.com/nba/article/death-of-the-cheap-seat-how-sports-figured-out-they-could-charge-you-almost-anything-for-a-ticket-153025119.html), since it’s a one-time deal. Issues of fairness, accessibility, affordability, and transparency also come into play, and the pricing task requires applying the concept of “[scaling the house](https://www.inderscienceonline.com/doi/abs/10.1504/IJRM.2014.067334)” across dozens of events, sessions, and stages.

The pricing task and price structure adopted by FIFA for the 2026 World Cup at the outset look very similar to the Olympic Games (and prior FIFA) approach and also quite reasonable. The prices for the [6.5-7 million available tickets](https://www.nytimes.com/athletic/7341387/2026/06/08/world-cup-2026-ticket-prices-saga-controversy/) were structured around a tiered pricing approach with ranges, virtually identical to that used by previous World Cups, the Olympic Games, and events of this caliber[^4].

There were three tiers of ticket prices, each with a range. These ranges varied by group stage (cheapest), opening matches (higher to account for greater early enthusiasm), and later rounds, with the highest ranges for the final. See the table below for the prices from the December lottery.

[Embedded content: datawrapper.dwcdn.net](https://datawrapper.dwcdn.net/Djage/1/)

Observers quickly noted that these prices were far higher than those at previous World Cups. Therefore, as is reasonable for these types of events, FIFA adjusted the price structure to explicitly factor in affordability and access for soccer fans. In mid-December 2025, so-called supporter tickets were added at significantly lower prices as a fourth category. They were to be sold to fans of participating teams through their respective national football federations. For example, the Supporter Entry Tier was priced at \$60, and [approximately 1,000 tickets](https://sports.yahoo.com/soccer/article/fifa-creates-60-world-cup-ticket-category-for-small-group-of-supporters-after-facing-uproar-over-pricing-210832999.html) were available at this price for every World Cup game.

### Understanding FIFA’s dynamic pricing process: Three lotteries and a “last-minute sale”

Many people have the idea that FIFA has been raising prices constantly to gouge fans. This is far from the case. The logic behind FIFA’s dynamic pricing, explained by the FIFA official as “adapt prices as per the demand we see, as per the remaining inventory,” is actually straightforward. They released three tranches of tickets sequentially using lotteries, and a fourth tranche of “last-minute ticket sales,” with moderate price increases for each successive tranche.

Fans applied for the first tranche (with the lowest prices) by a lottery called the “Visa Presale Draw” that ran from September 10-19, 2025, a second lottery called the “Early Ticket Draw” (still without knowing the draw or specific matches) ran from October 27-31, 2025, and a third lottery called the “Random Selection Draw” ran from December 11 to January 13, 2026, for specific games after the schedule had been announced. After this, there was what they called the “Last-Minute Sales Phase,” which started on April 1 and will run through the end of the tournament, during which fans could buy tickets on a first-come, first-served basis.

This concept of releasing tickets in consecutive tranches has been used at the Olympic Games and at prior World Cups. For example, tickets for the [Paris Olympic Games](https://www.olympics.com/en/news/mark-your-calendars-roadmap-for-first-phase-of-paris-2024-ticket-sales-unveiled) were released in stages, starting with multi-ticket packages, then single tickets, and finally real-time resale. The upcoming LA Olympic Games in 2028 also have a similar multi-stage structure, with so-called drops (Drop 1 is over; Drop 2 begins in August, with future drops unspecified).

With this background in mind, let’s consider FIFA’s dynamic pricing using a set of six criteria based on various recent criticisms, including the frequency and magnitude of price changes, average price levels, price transparency and pricing process transparency, and empty seats.

### Frequency and magnitude of price changes

As discussed, FIFA adjusted prices across four sales phases, starting with its first lottery. As reported in a [comprehensive assessment in the New York Times](https://www.nytimes.com/athletic/7341387/2026/06/08/world-cup-2026-ticket-prices-saga-controversy/),

Between October and April, FIFA hiked prices in at least one ticket category for 95 of the World Cup’s 104 matches; the average price increase was 35 percent.

By the end of the road, a Category 1 ticket to the final cost \$10,990, up from \$6,730 back in October. Group-stage prices ranged from \$140 in Category 3 for less appealing games to \$890 in Category 1 for Colombia vs. Portugal and nearly \$3,000 for the World Cup opener between Mexico and South Africa.

By the standards of dynamic pricing applications, both the number of price changes (four over a six-month period) and the average price increase (35% across these changes) seem moderate and reasonable. Furthermore, the criticism is based on the implicit assumption that FIFA is charging higher prices for delivering the same value.

The reality is that with every successive lottery, the fan gets more clarity about what they are actually paying for and therefore receives substantially higher value from the purchase. Someone buying in the first lottery [had no clue which game](https://www.cato.org/commentary/dynamic-pricing-fair-play-2026-fifa-world-cup) they’d be watching or where, because the draw hadn’t even been held yet. They were randomly choosing to go and watch some game somewhere. In contrast, someone buying at 35% higher “last-minute” prices was choosing to go to a particular game between specific teams at a specific location. These two are entirely different offerings, and the second one is far more valuable. What’s more, for many fans traveling abroad, planning a vacation around watching football games is far more difficult six or eight months in advance than two months before, another source of significant value. Once you take consumer psychology into account, the higher price comes with more information, clarity, flexibility, and autonomy.

### Average price levels

A common complaint with FIFA’s pricing was that prices were very high. For instance, Henry Bushnell, the Athletic reporter who has investigated this issue extensively over the past year, [noted](https://www.nytimes.com/athletic/7166874/2026/04/02/fifa-dynamic-pricing-2026-world-cup-tickets/):

When FIFA sold its first batch of 2026 World Cup tickets back in October, prices were unprecedented. An upper-deck endline to the World Cup final, for example, cost \$2,790. Across the board, for every match, every ticket category was far more expensive than the previous World Cups. Fans blasted the pricing as “astonishing” and “unacceptable,” and called on FIFA to course-correct.

There are at least two issues with this reasoning that need unpacking. First, it is not clear to me that this criticism uses the correct benchmark or explains the logic behind using it. The previous two FIFA World Cups were held in Qatar (2022) and Russia (2018). It seems rather unfair to compare prices in either country to those in the US, or to expect people to pay the same or equivalent prices to watch a football game here as they would in either of those countries (not to mention inflation in the intervening years). There are any number of relevant differences between these countries, from tourism appeal, within-country demand, greater willingness among Americans to pay higher prices, higher reference prices for American marquee sporting events, and so on, that make higher prices eminently reasonable. Just as important, this sort of variation in prices is common across different Olympic Games. For instance, the average price per ticket for the 2008 Beijing Olympic Games was around \$29, but it was around \$97 for the 2012 London Olympic Games[^5].

Second, the complaint implicitly assumes that the higher prices are unfair and that fans paying them are getting a bad deal. However, this ignores variation in demand across complex events like World Cups, the excessive demand for certain events such as marquee matchups or finals, and the operation of the secondary market. A fundamental characteristic underlying FIFA ticket pricing (which also applies to Olympic Games) is that demand is choppy. There will be far more people interested in seeing Colombia play Portugal or Norway play France (or the USA play anyone) in the preliminary rounds than there are available tickets to watch those games. And there will be many games where demand won’t be as high, say when Uzbekistan plays DR Congo, or Curacao plays Ecuador[^6]. When demand is high and capacity is limited, regardless of whatever prices FIFA charges, the final prices will be dictated by the secondary market. In fact, it can be argued that the lower FIFA keeps its prices, the more profit scalpers will earn in the secondary market.

Furthermore, it should be noted that the issue of high prices for premier sporting events is not limited to the FIFA World Cup. The recent Game 3 of the NBA Finals between the New York Knicks and the San Antonio Spurs had the same issue, as [reported in the New York Times](https://www.nytimes.com/athletic/7342131/2026/06/08/new-york-knicks-nba-finals-ticket-prices/):

The Knicks’ exuberant run to the finals has been a joy for their fans. Many have waited decades to get back to this point. Some weren’t even born the last time the team made the finals in 1999. A lucky few have waited more than a half-century to see the franchise win another title.

They have all celebrated together, with every clutch Jalen Brunson jumper, dominant Karl-Anthony Towns performance and double-digit playoff victory. With 13 straight wins, these Knicks have put together a playoff run for the ages.

It has also created some difficult questions for its fans. Prices for finals games at MSG have skyrocketed. The get-in price on the secondary market sat at roughly \$8,000 late last week.

“I kind of wish the ticket prices weren’t as crazy as they are,” Knicks forward Josh Hart said Sunday. “I feel like a lot of people who have been waiting for this moment for a very long time, unfortunately, aren’t able to get into the building, when the cheapest ticket is 7 or 8,000 dollars. So that’s ridiculous. But it’s just going to be rocking.”

Regardless of whatever pricing strategy is used, and whether dynamic pricing in any form is implemented or not, if fans want to attend a much-desired game or sit in a desirable location in the stadium, they are going to have to pay more money, simply because there are too many fans and not enough tickets.

Finally, making FIFA’s dynamic pricing strategy look even smarter is this recent datapoint. Days before the opening match, ticket prices began to drop. As of June 10, [almost 180,000 tickets remained unsold](https://www.ft.com/content/ac26baee-691d-4e77-9fbf-756aaa463b63?syn-25a6b1a6=1) on FIFA’s official resale platform, of which 176,000 were for the group stage. The median resale price had also dropped 20% during May, so that after the platform’s 26% transaction fee was taken into account, most resales would result in a loss for the original buyer[^7]. It appears that with its approach, FIFA foiled the intentions of many early buyers looking to profit from reselling them. The Financial Times reporter [concluded that](https://www.ft.com/content/ac26baee-691d-4e77-9fbf-756aaa463b63):

In addition to the resales, Fifa continues to list about 15,000 tickets for sale for group-stage games. Empty seats would be an embarrassment for Fifa, the governing body of global football, which opted to price tickets far higher than in previous editions of the [World Cup](https://www.ft.com/fifa-mens-world-cup), which it said was in line with local market conditions.

The logic of this conclusion is hard to follow. Isn’t it the case that people actually bought these tickets the first time around and are now trying to sell them? How is this an embarrassment for FIFA? Rather, it should be a source of pride for outwitting would-be scalpers. And as for the 15,000 tickets still available for sale, FIFA could easily sell them at lower prices and end up with a much higher overall revenue than using an alternative pricing strategy starting with too-low prices and letting scalpers take the revenue difference.

### Price transparency and pricing process transparency

A [key criticism](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/digital-pricing) of dynamic pricing methods is that they are [inherently opaque](https://len-sherman.medium.com/how-uber-became-a-cash-generating-machine-ef78e7a97230), both in the prices set and in the pricing process. Let’s call these [two types of transparency](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/decoding-three-types-of-price-related) price transparency and pricing process transparency for our discussion. Here again, we need to consider the lack of transparency critique two ways, first on its own merits (i.e., has the process for setting 2026 FIFA World Cup ticket prices and ticket allocation across the price categories been explained clearly by FIFA and well-understood by consumers?), and second, in comparison to other benchmarks (i.e., how does the 2026 FIFA World Cup ticketing process compare to those used by other past sporting events not using dynamic pricing?). I will use the 2012 London Olympic Games as the benchmark, both because it didn’t use dynamic pricing and also because there is detailed after-the-fact information available, including an [extensive 2013 audit by the London Assembly](https://www.london.gov.uk/about-us/about-us/london-assembly/london-assembly-publications/price-gold-lessons-london-2012-ticket-sales).

Considering price transparency first, there’s no doubt that while price ranges were made known or easily discoverable through FIFA press releases, social media posts, reporter investigations, etc., the distribution of prices for a specific game has been much more difficult to find. Maximum price transparency would entail something like a [theater seating chart](https://www.broadway-show-tickets.com/theater-seating-chart-guide/) for a Broadway show, where, for any given performance, every seat is marked with the price, and the customer knows exactly what they will pay for a particular seat. In contrast, FIFA has been far more opaque, with many buyers accusing it of misleading them with [stadium maps that misrepresent seat locations](https://www.nytimes.com/athletic/7175652/2026/04/08/world-cup-tickets-fans-stadium-seating-map/) and of [changing seat maps](https://www.texasattorneygeneral.gov/news/releases/attorney-general-ken-paxton-investigates-fifa-ensure-fans-have-access-accurate-and-honest-pricing) to accommodate premium bundles like hospitality packages. See the video below for a news story from Houston about this issue.

A number of state attorneys general, [including Texas](https://www.texasattorneygeneral.gov/news/releases/attorney-general-ken-paxton-investigates-fifa-ensure-fans-have-access-accurate-and-honest-pricing), have recently launched investigations into this issue. On its own merits, there’s clearly a deficiency in FIFA’s price transparency.

Considering pricing process transparency next, the process by which prices are allocated, as well as specific information about how demand is converted into individual ticket allocations, is equally opaque. While FIFA announced how many people [applied for tickets](https://www.fifa.com/en/tournaments/mens/worldcup/canadamexicousa2026/articles/500-million-ticket-requests-submitted) in [each lottery](https://inside.fifa.com/organisation/media-releases/more-than-4-5-million-fans-enter-draw-for-first-world-cup-26-ticket-sales-phase), it provided no information about how many tickets were allocated, at what prices, or how many tickets still remained available. Equally important, there were some recent indications that FIFA relied on moving tickets from their primary channel to the resale channel.

For instance, reports in late May 2026 surfaced suggesting that more than 40,000 tickets abruptly vanished from FIFA’s primary sales platform, and a closely matching volume [then surfaced on its resale marketplace](https://www.newsweek.com/world-cup-tickets-vanish-fifa-resale-sites-12031370) at lower prices, with a significant [number priced at \$69](https://frontofficesports.com/world-cup-fifa-tickets-resale-prices/). Observers conjectured that this was a pricing strategy akin to that used by luxury brands, maintaining high prices in their own stores and selling surplus end-of-season merchandise through unaffiliated outlet stores or the gray market. It would also avoid having to explain lower prices to fans who had paid higher prices early on.

Are price structures without dynamic pricing used by major sporting events more transparent in terms of pricing and the pricing process? The case of the 2012 London Olympic Games provide a resounding “no” answer to both questions. The London Assembly commissioned an in-depth analysis of the ticketing strategy for the Games, and a detailed report was released a year later, in April 2013. It concluded:

LOCOG refused to publish details of how many tickets would be available at different prices before the Games, even after the vast majority of tickets had already been sold. The lack of transparency put public trust at risk, encouraging a sense among many Londoners that the opportunity to attend the Games was outside their reach. It is only now that we have the chance to assess whether LOCOG met its pledges to make the Games affordable. Our findings are sobering. While the organisation of the Games was highly successful and we gave visitors from across the world a fantastic welcome, the cost of attending the Games was clearly prohibitive for many Londoners. There were many affordable tickets on sale, but we found these were extremely limited for the most popular sports and sessions….there were many disappointed people who were unable to see the most popular events or even to attend the Games. Our analysis suggests that this unmet demand was due to a combination of factors, including the relatively small proportion of public tickets for some sessions and the extremely high prices. The ticketing process itself is also likely to have excluded some people. Furthermore, we are concerned that the lack of transparency in the ticketing arrangements affected public trust in the Games. (p. 3-4).

About price transparency and pricing process transparency, the report stated:

LOCOG refused to confirm ahead of the Games how many tickets were being sold in different price categories. This information would have allowed Londoners to see whether LOCOG was meeting its own pledges, and to make informed judgements about which tickets to apply for. LOCOG continued to withhold this information even after the vast majority of tickets had been sold, ignoring the precedent set at Sydney 2000 when a full breakdown of ticket sales was released shortly before the Games. The Assembly found that the reasons given by LOCOG – accuracy, data protection and commercial confidentiality – did not justify this level of secrecy. LOCOG published the requested data on ticket sales over three months after the Games ended…key pieces of information are still not in the public domain \[a year later\]. (p.5).

Essentially, reading the report is like experiencing déjà vu. The 2013 report contains exactly the same set of complaints now being leveled against FIFA. I would like to suggest that the issue of transparency, whether it is about price or the pricing process, has less to do with whether dynamic pricing is used and more to do with the sheer complexity of the pricing task for mega-events like the FIFA World Cup and the Olympic Games and the bias of the decision makers involved to favor revenue generation over other pricing objectives.

### Empty seats

As the FIFA World Cup started, another concern surfaced, this time about empty seats. For example, news reports said FIFA recorded an attendance of 44,985 (capacity: 45,664) for the match between South Korea and the Czech Republic in Guadalajara, yet swathes of empty seats were visible. This led to the (rather convoluted, in my opinion) hypothesis that the actual number of tickets sold may have been lower, which was attributed to ticket prices being too high, in turn due to dynamic pricing. The reality is that when consumer behavior is taken into account, there are many plausible alternative explanations. First and foremost, it could be a simple demand explanation: this is a match between two non-host countries with smaller local supporter bases in Guadalajara.

Second, many citizens and diaspora of these countries (and others) who bought tickets to this particular game months in advance may not have been able to use them for a variety of reasons. Third, as [posited by FIFA](https://www.thestar.com/sports/soccer/fifa-blames-empty-seats-at-world-cup-match-in-mexico-on-fans-in-the-concourses/article_824bd0a7-9219-5020-bd43-6ee8918c94a8.html), “Official attendance figures reflect the number of tickets scanned, and spectators present within the stadium footprint, rather than visual assessments of seating occupancy at any given moment during the match…Please note that, during last night’s match in Guadalajara, several ticketed fans could be seen standing in concourses rather than staying in their assigned seats throughout the match.” And finally, refuting the link between high prices due to dynamic pricing and empty seats in the stadium were the actual get-in prices for the South Korea vs. Czech Republic game and the Mexico vs. South Africa game the same day, where the stadium was full to bursting.

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/64a8ada6-cd4a-4102-bb55-263013f0572c_7936x8000.png)

Ticket prices in secondary market for Mexico v. South Africa and South Korea v. Czech Republic matches. Source: ticketdata.com.

As can be seen from the charts, the secondary market ticket prices for the Mexico vs. South Africa game (including the final get-in price) were approximately six times higher than the prices for the South Korea vs. Czech Republic game. This suggests that the empty seats at the latter game were certainly not because of high prices. It was far more likely due to the relatively lower interest in that specific match. In fact, the same problem of empty seats occurred during the Beijing Olympics in 2008, and at least one reason given was that [ticket prices were too low](https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/cm200809/cmselect/cmcumeds/25/8120905.htm), so many Chinese consumers bought ticket bundles but only used some of them that they were really interested in watching, or even framed them as souvenirs.

### Conclusion

Ticket pricing is routinely a punching bag for major sports events. See below for a sampling of headlines about previous Olympics and World Cups. Add dynamic pricing to the mix, and the outrage quotient grows even higher. I will admit to throwing a [few punches of my own](https://prospect.org/2024/06/07/2024-06-07-urge-to-surge/) over the years. But with in-depth consideration, FIFA’s approach to the 2026 World Cup pricing comes across as far more reasonable and balanced than the headlines suggest. The frequency and magnitude of the price changes were moderate, with price increases corresponding to the significantly higher value being offered to ticket buyers. The price levels reflected the choppy demand and secondary-market realities that any major global sporting event must contend with, and the much-maligned price drops in the final days could be attributed to FIFA outwitting scalpers rather than any evidence of failure. Where FIFA fell short, in the transparency of prices and of the pricing process, the [audit of the 2012 London Olympic Games](https://www.london.gov.uk/about-us/about-us/london-assembly/london-assembly-publications/price-gold-lessons-london-2012-ticket-sales) pricing strategy reminds us that this is a chronic challenge for complex, one-time global sporting events with revenue targets of hundreds of millions of dollars, not a consequence of dynamic pricing.

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/5ffa4d9f-6318-4a34-8cf7-0b0ff6f6354f_2616x2436.png)

The real culprit, I would argue, is not the pricing mechanism but the combination of an extraordinarily complex pricing task and decision makers whose instincts tilt slightly in favor of revenue over fairness, accessibility, and transparency. Swap in a different pricing process (without dynamic pricing) and those same pressures would produce many of the same complaints, as they did in London and elsewhere.

Finally, I should be clear about one thing. I firmly believe that many forms of dynamic pricing can indeed harm customers, even significantly so, and specific aspects of FIFA’s opacity deserve the scrutiny it is now receiving from the [attorneys general’s offices](https://www.houstonpublicmedia.org/articles/news/texas/2026/06/09/554054/texas-ag-ken-paxton-opens-investigation-into-fifa-over-2026-world-cup-ticket-sales/). However, the next time a major sporting event comes around (the 2028 LA Olympics, for instance) and the outrage machine starts winding up again, before we join the angry mob, we would do well to ask the question: Is it really the pricing strategy that is the real culprit, or is it the extremely complex pricing task and the priorities of the people doing the pricing?

FIFA has explicitly stated they are [using variable pricing, not dynamic pricing](https://www.rte.ie/brainstorm/2026/0612/1578071-dynamic-ticket-prices-fifa-world-cup-2026/), and in some respects, this is true. But as discussed in detail here, the price changes correspond to demand changes, so we wouldn’t be wrong to call their pricing strategy dynamic pricing.
