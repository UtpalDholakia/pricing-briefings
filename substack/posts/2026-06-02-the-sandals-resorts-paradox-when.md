##### ***Summary:*** While pricing decision makers have long relied on flat-rate pricing (or all-inclusive pricing in the resort industry) to drive initial customer choice, a tension arises during the actual consumption experience. Prepayment of the all-inclusive price removes the immediate transactional pain of paying, but it also removes the “hedonic brake,” or the small friction that helps a consumer notice when they have had enough. By making cost invisible at the point of consumption, an all-inclusive price structure risks letting customers consume past the point of enjoyment, potentially undermining the very satisfaction the flat rate was meant to deliver.

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/27b99ea6-d281-4178-94c9-a7dbd63bbc40_1672x941.png)

Consider two couples vacationing in Saint Lucia this past winter. Both are successful professionals who were looking to escape the cold for a while and relax and recharge. By the end of their respective week-long vacations, they spent approximately the same amount of money, but one couple felt much, much better about their experience than the other. Let me explain.

The first couple, Sarah and Mark, booked a week at the all-inclusive [Sandals Grande St. Lucian](https://www.sandals.com/saint-lucia/) for \$8,400. Every aspect of their experience, including top-tier meals, unlimited (top-shelf) adult beverages, numerous activities, and even tipping the resort staff, was included in this price. The other couple, Elena and David, chose [The Landings Resort & Spa](https://www.landingsstlucia.com/), a comparable luxury resort next door to Sandals on the same stretch of white sand along the Pigeon Island Causeway. Their luxury villa cost much less, \$4,900 for the week, but they had to pay for everything else à la carte.

An intuitive judgment would suggest that Elena and David made the smarter choice. They retained autonomy over how much to spend and what to spend on throughout their experience, and weren’t restricted to resort restaurants, activities, or amenities. Yet the [all-inclusive price structure thrives](https://www.hotelinvestmenttoday.com/Regions/Caribbean/Why-Mexico-Caribbean-all-inclusives-are-thriving) in the resort industry. For instance, Sandals routinely charges a [15–30% premium](https://caribbeanmag.com/is-sandals-worth-it-an-honest-breakdown-after-visiting-all-17-resorts/) over its five-star resort neighbors. The standard explanation for this is the so-called flat-rate bias[^1], the idea that consumers prefer fixed prices to metered ones, even when metering would cost them less for an equivalent experience. Put differently, a couple like Sarah and Mark is paying for the freedom to stop keeping tabs on every transaction during their relaxing vacation.

While this aspect of flat-rate pricing (i.e., the expectation of a better psychological experience) explains the preference for it, it is well known. However, the equally interesting question of whether this preference for flat-rate pricing persists during and after the experience is less well understood, and that is what I want to focus on in this post.

### **The preference for flat-rate price structures**

When Elena and David arrived at their luxury resort, they encountered a transparent, menu-based price structure; for instance, beachside mojitos cost \$17 apiece, dinner at the upscale restaurants costs about \$180, and so on. Each time they purchased something, they signed a bill showing exactly how much they’d spent. However (and this is key to the preference for flat rates), each transaction also carried an additional psychological burden beyond the financial cost. Research on the pain of paying has shown that parting with money is itself an aversive experience, one that can diminish the pleasure of what the money buys[^2]. Furthermore, this discomfort is amplified by the visibility and frequency of payment[^3]. Each time the couple had to pay, they experienced a small aversive moment, and over the course of their resort stay, it added up.

Sarah and Mark were insulated from this negative experience because of the payment structure. They had paid the entire amount before they arrived at their all-inclusive resort (even if they charged it to a credit card), meaning their pain of payment had been dispensed with, and its associated discomfort had already been depreciated, a concept known as “prospective accounting.” At the resort, they could consume to their heart’s content with no associated bills to sign, and therefore no pain to experience, only pleasure. In effect, Sarah and Mark paid a premium in advance to insure themselves against the steady drain of moment-by-moment spending decisions and the associated transactional pain of payment.

The assumption underlying this difference (and the argument in favor of flat-rate price structures) is that decoupling the payment from the experience is always better, and that the less a customer notices what they spend, the more they will enjoy the consumption of their purchase.

### **How long does the preference for flat-rate price structures persist post-choice?**

The existing research on flat-rate pricing is almost entirely concerned with the decision itself, often considering whether consumers choose the all-inclusive or the à la carte option and why they tend to prefer the fixed price even when a metered price might serve them as well (or even better, using the measure of total amount paid). Far less attention has been given to the customer’s post-choice preference, specifically their assessment of the purchase during and after the consumption experience. Here, I argue that satiation needs to be considered. My hypothesis is that during the consumption of an all-inclusive priced experience, the two mechanisms of satiation and the decoupled pain of payment may begin to have countervailing effects for the consumer.

Starting with the all-inclusive case, as an example, drinking the first mojito on a hot summer afternoon may be bliss, but by the time Mark gets to the fourth one, his pleasure quotient will be much lower, and if he gets to the seventh, there will be little pleasure left. Yet, there’s no hedonic brake to stop him as there would be if he were signing the bar tab for each drink. The all-inclusive price structure has decoupled the pain of paying at the point of consumption. Thus, Mark is in a strange (and not necessarily pleasant) situation where he’s not experiencing pain with every mojito that’s handed to him, but he’s not experiencing much, if any, pleasure either. Essentially, Mark (and also Sarah) can keep drinking mojitos, well past the point where the drinks bring them any real pleasure, with nothing in the experience to mark where they should have stopped.

In the à la carte case, in contrast, the pain of payment serves to constrain behavior, acting as a brake. Each time they have to sign the bar tab, Elena and David will have the chance to ask themselves, “Do we really want yet another mojito?” Thus, the friction introduced by the metered price structure might give them the opportunity to consume closer to their optimal level, stop earlier, and have a more positive overall experience than the other couple. Thus, we can ask the intriguing question:

***Does the all-inclusive price structure that makes the early stages of the resort stay feel more pleasurable undermine the customers’ experience later on? And could the couple who experienced greater pain from transactional payments under à la carte pricing have a more positive overall experience?***

There are reasons to doubt that simply removing the price tag always improves the experience. Research has found that consumers’ preference for prepayment is not universal; rather, it holds for pleasurable, experiential purchases like a concert or a vacation, so the experience can be enjoyed unburdened by having to deal with payments. However, this preference weakens for durable, functional purchases like washing machines, or when the terms of the transaction become less attractive, such as when a vacation deal starts to look like a bad one[^4]. More recent work further complicates the picture. An EEG study of mobile payments found that a sufficiently frictionless transaction does more than remove the discomfort of paying for consumers; the very ease of paying can produce a small pleasure of its own[^5]. Whether the same holds for friction lowered by prepayment prior to the experience is an open question. If it does, the “free” feeling from all-inclusive pricing might both reduce pain and generate pleasure of its own. Furthermore, positive sensations, like the mojitos, are subject to satiation. Whether the positive feeling experienced by the consumer on account of prepayment fades over the course of the week, and how quickly, is, as far as I know, something yet to be examined.

### **The hybrid price structure does not solve this problem**

Why not draw upon the benefits of all-inclusive and à la carte pricing and use a hybrid price structure? Indeed, many resorts using all-inclusive or à la carte price structures have increasingly gravitated towards a hybrid price structure with two parts: an all-inclusive price that covers the basics plus [selective unbundling for many experiential components of the service](https://thepointsguy.com/news/all-inclusive-resort-upcharges/), such as a premium upgrade fee to dine at the upscale restaurant, an [upcharge for cocktails made with top-shelf liquor](https://www.nationaltraveller.com/hidden-fees-at-all-inclusive-resorts-that-arent-actually-included/) or champagne, a surcharge for the cabana, and so on. From a managerial standpoint, this may reduce the pain of payment and grant consumers some autonomy in designing their experience while staying within a budget. From a consumer psychology standpoint, however, this price structure combines the problems of both price structures. The consumer forfeits the peace of mind that comes with a true all-inclusive price (even when they don’t use the services with upcharges), while feeling manipulated for being nickel-and-dimed, turning a relaxed frame of mind into a vigilant one.

But the two-part hybrid price structure also reveals something the pure all-inclusive structure conceals. It is that a price signal, even an unwelcome one, is useful to the consumer, prompting them to pause (even if it is briefly) and give some thought to each incremental purchase. The weakness of the hybrid structure lies in the fact that its signals are arbitrary and focused on extracting customer economic value at the margins of their experience, precisely at those places where a good customer experience could become amazing if left friction-free.

This points to a distinction that is easy to get backward. The guest’s irritation at the hybrid price structure could be interpreted by the resort company as evidence that cues from the price structure to pause and assess detracted from the customer experience, and that its goal should therefore be to remove them as completely as possible. However, given the satiation mechanism also at work, what is needed is a price signal that aligns with the customer’s natural satiation during the experience, present when consumption has stopped adding much, but absent when it hasn’t. Whether a well-designed price structure improves the customer experience rather than simply reducing consumption remains an open question. But this discussion does suggest that the lowest-friction price structure might not be the one that delivers the best customer experience.
