#### **Case Summary**

Nathan Parker, a newly minted MBA and product manager at Maverick Drilling Technologies, is tasked with recommending a price for the company’s newest offering, the NaviRig + PADS directional drilling system. This case occurs in November 2022, as Maverick is preparing to enter the independent operator segment with a solution pairing its advanced NaviRig drilling hardware with the proprietary Precision Automated Drilling System (PADS) software.

Maverick is currently the market leader in the premium directional drilling equipment segment, supplying major oil companies. Its R&D group developed PADS as a differentiator to enhance the drilling system’s performance and has achieved significant gains: up to 4x in shale, 2x in sandstone, and 1.5x in carbonate formations. Now, the company must determine how to price this breakthrough technology for a new type of customer that is a bit unfamiliar to them: independent operators who are significantly more price-sensitive than Maverick’s traditional clients.

In providing his pricing recommendation, Nathan must evaluate four different pricing strategies: the company’s legacy pricing approach that involves offering PADS at no additional cost, competition-based pricing, cost-based pricing, and value-based pricing. His recommendation must balance internal expectations, competitive pressure from TEI (which dominates the standard segment with a 60% market share), and the challenge of articulating the value of an innovative product to technically savvy but cost-conscious customers.

This case is designed for second-year MBA students in pricing strategy, marketing strategy, or general management courses, though it also works effectively in executive education programs focused on B2B marketing or technology commercialization. It requires students to have foundational knowledge of basic financial concepts, including net present value, cost structures, and profitability analysis, making it most appropriate for students who have completed core finance and accounting coursework.

#### **Key Learning Objectives**

By analyzing this case, students will learn to evaluate multiple pricing approaches and select the most appropriate strategy based on market conditions, customer characteristics, and competitive dynamics. The case demonstrates how to quantify and communicate customer value, particularly for B2B technology products with measurable performance benefits. Students explore the strategic implications of bundling complementary products and the challenges of pricing intellectual property. The case illustrates how pricing strategies must adapt when entering new market segments with different customer characteristics and value drivers. Additionally, students learn to anticipate and plan for competitive reactions to pricing decisions in concentrated markets while understanding the importance of aligning pricing strategy with sales execution and marketing communication.

#### **Pricing Calculations for the Four Methods**

***1. Legacy Pricing Approach***

Under the legacy pricing method, Maverick would charge only for the NaviRig drilling equipment and bundle the PADS software at no additional cost. This approach maintains the traditional industry practice where software is considered a value-added service rather than a revenue-generating product.

**Calculation:**

Base NaviRig price (established by Davis): \$2,000,000

PADS software cost: \$0 (bundled free)

**Total Legacy Price: \$2,000,000**

This approach fails to recover the \$6,000,000 development investment in PADS and essentially gives away the primary source of competitive differentiation. While this pricing strategy might accelerate market penetration and maintain consistency with industry norms, it sacrifices significant potential revenue and positions PADS as a commodity rather than a premium technology solution.

One point to note is that this method of giving away software to lock in customers to the company’s hardware seems to be the standard operating procedure in this industry. It has also been common in many other high-tech B2B industries for decades, and is still the norm in many situations today. Students shouldn’t dismiss it out of hand; in fact, asking specific questions about why this approach has remained popular for so long may generate interesting discussion around the role of cross-subsidization and brand building through value generation for customers.

***2. Competition-Based Pricing***

Competition-based pricing would set the NaviRig + PADS system price equivalent to what customers would pay for comparable drilling capacity using TEI's DrillPro systems. This instruction is given in the case and should be followed.

**Calculation for Shale Formations (4x performance improvement):**

One NaviRig + PADS replaces four DrillPro systems

DrillPro price: \$1,900,000 per system

Equivalent cost: 4 × \$1,900,000 = \$7,600,000

**Competition-Based Price (Shale): \$7,600,000**

**Calculation for Sandstone Formations (2x performance improvement):**

One NaviRig + PADS replaces two DrillPro systems

Equivalent cost: 2 × \$1,900,000 = \$3,800,000

**Competition-Based Price (Sandstone): \$3,800,000**

Like the legacy method, this is a rather cookie-cutter pricing approach that involves simply matching the competitor’s pricing without any consideration for whether it makes sense or could be improved. The obvious problem is that this approach ensures competitive positioning but may not fully capture the additional operational benefits that PADS provides beyond simple capacity replacement. A second problem is that it will be quite difficult to convince customers of these improvement ratios (even if technical evidence is provided).

***3. Cost-Based Pricing***

The cost-based pricing calculation begins with the NaviRig manufacturing cost and adds the allocated PADS development cost recovery.

**Step 1: Calculate Expected PADS Unit Sales**

• 2023: Market size 550 × 4% share = 22 units × 50% with PADS = 11 PADS units

• 2024: Market size 850 × 9% share = 76.5 units × 50% with PADS = 38 PADS units  
• 2025: Market size 1,130 × 14% share = 158.2 units × 50% with PADS = 79 PADS units

• Total PADS units over 3 years: 11 + 39 + 79 = 128 units

**Step 2: Calculate PADS Development Cost per Unit**

• Total PADS development cost: \$6,000,000

• PADS cost per unit: \$6,000,000 ÷ 128 = \$46,875

**Step 3: Calculate Total Cost and Apply Markup**

• NaviRig price (including markup): \$2,300,000

• PADS development cost allocation plus markup: \$46,875 \* 1.3 = \$60,937.5

• **Cost-Based Price: \$2,360,937.5**

It is important to point out here that the case tells us that Nathan’s boss, Samuel Davis, “had already established a round price point of \$2,000,000 for the NaviRig system before Nathan came on board (page 5). This would imply a 13% markup, not 30% based on the cost of making it (based on Exhibit 2). Exhibit 2 also tells us that the NaviRig, based on a 30% markup, should be \$2,300,000. So which is it? The case suggests that Davis has a preference for a lower price. Which NaviRig price Nathan uses is his call. If he goes with his boss’s favored \$2,000,000, then the cost-based price for the system will be \$2,060,937.5. Either way, students should understand that Nathan is not responsible for pricing the solution; he is, in fact, only pricing the PADS software. His boss is calling the shots for the hardware, and he has no say.

***4. Value-Based Pricing***

When applying value-based pricing, use the six-step approach from the Value Pricing Framework as shown in the next figure.

The definition of value-based pricing is useful here, also from Dholakia (2017):

> “Value-based pricing is the method of setting a price by which the company calculates and tries to earn the differentiated economic value of its product for a particular customer segment when compared to its competitor.”

Step 1: The target persona is given to us, Lone Star Energy Partners. That is the target customer that the NaviRig+PADS solution is targeted towards.

Step 2: The competitor and its offering are also clearly given in the case, making this step straightforward. TEI’s DrillPro is the focal competitor against which the value-based price will be calculated.

Steps 3 and 4: The head-to-head comparison reveals a superior performance of the scale of 4:1. This is obviously the key and only differentiator that generates economic value. There are no deficiencies in Maverick’s offering relative to the TEI offering. Furthermore, we are told that “given the conservative nature of many of the senior managers involved in the decision making process, \[Nathan\] needed to compare two NaviRig+PADS systems to four standard DrillPro systems. So the ratio to be used for both purchase and operating costs is 2:1, not 4:1.

Step 6: Calculate the value-based price. This gives us a PADS value of \$23,300,000 - \$11,750,000 = \$11,550,000.

This step also includes rationalizing the price, i.e., assessing whether it makes sense in the context of the other inputs in the Value Pricing Framework, such as costs and reference prices (competitors’ prices as well as the company’s own prices).

Note that based on this assessment, the \$11,550,000, while correct, is an exorbitantly high value. And in fact, if we were to consider the multiple-year life of this system, even accounting for some costs to upgrade the software, the PADS value would be much, much higher! The machine itself costs \$2,000,000, and the software would be nearly six times that much with this math if Nathan tried to price to extract the entire economic value.

When compared to his boss, Samuel Davis’s preference for not charging anything, this price looks even more daunting. What specific value-based price should Nathan recommend? That will be up to the student, but it should be clear (and the instructor should drive home this fact) that Nathan and Maverick will only be able to extract a small fraction of the economic value generated by the software for its users.

Most pricing decision makers will end up closer to the cost-based price than the value-based price in this scenario.

#### **Potential Discussion Questions and Some Notes for Potential Answers**

***1. What are the key characteristics of the directional drilling equipment market, and how do they influence pricing decisions?***

This question might be particularly useful in a class that has a lot of oil and gas industry students (which is often the case at Rice, where I teach). Many of them may be well-versed in the industry and will be able to provide useful context for the pricing decision.

The directional drilling equipment market exhibits a number of characteristics that significantly impact Nathan’s pricing decision. First, the market demonstrates concentrated competition, with TEI holding 60% market share in the standard segment, making competitive response a critical consideration for any pricing strategy. Second, customers are technically sophisticated and have deep industry experience, enabling them to readily evaluate technical specifications and economic benefits, which supports value-based pricing approaches for products with demonstrable performance advantages. But it’s another matter whether they will be willing to pay for those benefits, especially when they are so parabolic as in our case. Like it or not, sophisticated and traditionally-minded buyers mean that most of PADS’ value will be retained by them, not by Maverick.

This sector of the oil and gas industry, like most of its other sectors, operates as a relationship-driven marketplace where reputation and long-term partnerships of both the company and longstanding executives and managers within it matter tremendously. This dynamic favors established players and requires new entrants to demonstrate not only technical superiority but also reliability and service capability over a lengthy period of time to garner trust. Furthermore, capital-intensive purchase decisions typically involve multiple stakeholders and extensive evaluation processes, particularly for high-value transactions exceeding \$2 million, as in this case. Customers focus intensively on operational efficiency and drilling economics, creating opportunities for value-based pricing when products deliver measurable performance improvements. The market segments naturally by geological formation types, creating distinct value propositions and price sensitivity levels that may support differentiated pricing strategies.

***2. Evaluate the four pricing approaches Nathan is considering. What are the advantages and disadvantages of each?***

Legacy pricing, which bundles PADS software at no additional charge, offers the advantage of maintaining consistency with established industry and Maverick pricing norms and simplifying the sales process by avoiding complex value justification discussions. It is also Samuel Davis’s preferred approach; so, from Nathan’s perspective as a new product manager starting out, this recommendation would be low-risk, offering the path of least resistance. Additionally, this approach could accelerate market share capture and reduce customer resistance to adopting new technology.

However, on the negative side, legacy pricing (obviously) fails completely to recover the \$6 million development investment in PADS and essentially commoditizes advanced technology that provides substantial customer value. The old adage goes that when something is given away, people appreciate it much less. This also applies to customers getting invaluable directional drilling software for free. And finally, this approach also sets a dangerous precedent and relational norm in this commercial context that sophisticated software should be provided without charge, potentially undermining future pricing flexibility for technology innovations.

Competition-based pricing ensures competitive positioning relative to TEI's DrillPro systems and provides customers with familiar price benchmarks that reduce objections during the sales process. The approach acknowledges market realities and competitor capabilities while avoiding potential price wars that could emerge from premium pricing strategies. Nevertheless, the method is too crude and simplistic. Competition-based pricing completely ignores the superior performance benefits that PADS delivers by not considering second-order savings (which are massive here) and fails to capture value for differentiated capabilities. This approach may also trigger competitive responses that lead to overall market price erosion.

Cost-based pricing offers the advantage of ensuring cost recovery and maintaining target profit margins while providing internally defensible pricing logic that satisfies financial management requirements. Many students (and even instructors) may be surprised that the recommendation it comes up with is actually a pretty reasonable one. Like every application of cost-based pricing (and a key reason it is so popular), this pricing method delivers predictable revenue and profit outcomes that will support business planning and performance measurement. However, cost-based pricing completely ignores customer value creation and market dynamics, potentially overpricing relative to customer willingness to pay or underpricing relative to value delivered. (In this case, it is slight underpricing or possibly, even fair pricing). The approach also fails to consider competitive positioning or strategic market development objectives.

As the six-step application earlier in this teaching note showed, value-based pricing aligns the solution’s price with the economic benefits delivered to customers, supporting premium positioning while capturing a fair share of the value created through PADS technology. In theory, this approach maximizes revenue potential and reinforces the perception of PADS as advanced, high-value technology rather than a commodity add-on. However, as discussed earlier, the challenge with implementing value-based pricing is that you still have to persuade customers that the ludicrous-seeming price premium is still fair value. Another broad benefit of value-based pricing is that it provides flexibility to adjust pricing based on customer-specific value drivers and formation types. For example, Maverick could go to different independent operators and give them customized price quotes based on the type of formations they are drilling in. The primary disadvantages include the requirement for sophisticated value communication and sales processes, potential customer resistance to premium pricing, and dependence on accurate value quantification that customers accept and understand. All of these concerns apply to the Maverick/ Nathan situation in spades.

***3. What pricing strategy would you recommend for Nathan, and why?***

After students have worked out the prices using the four methods, they should be struck by the range of values obtained. The flip answer to this question is to recommend value-based pricing with formation-specific pricing tiers that capture the varying performance benefits PADS delivers across different geological formations. The logic would indicate that with value-based pricing, Maverick is able to capture the substantial economic value that PADS creates for customers while maintaining competitive positioning and supporting Maverick's premium brand strategy.

Thus, under an endorsement of value-based pricing, for shale formations where PADS provides 4x performance improvement, the recommended price of \$4,500,000 will capture approximately 22% of the total economic value delivered to customers. Even this would imply a software price of \$2,500,000 (or just over \$200k per month, assuming some kind of subscription). Analogous prices would be \$3,500,000 for sandstone formations and \$2,500,000 for carbonate formations, which don’t benefit much from PADS. Even in this last case, the software costs half a million dollars to customers.

If you’ve gotten this far, it should be clear to you that no reasonable customer is going to pay anything close to the value-based prices, even when 25% of the value is delivered to customers. The opposite is more likely to work, that is, giving 75% to 80% of the value to customers and keeping the smaller fraction for the company.

But a bigger point is necessary here. The price recommendation that Nathan provides does not need to be, and in fact, should not be, a recommendation of a particular pricing method. It is counter-productive, or even meaningless, to say, “I recommend that value-based price” or “I recommend the cost-based price.” The relevant recommendation is of a particular number that could be anything within the range established by the four pricing methods, or even outside it, using some logic that is not articulated in the case. After showing the Maverick executives all his calculations and explaining the logic behind them, Nathan might say, “Based on all the analysis, I recommend a price of \$2,240,000 for the solution, which will mean a software subscription of \$20,000 per month or approximately \$1,200 a day, something that seems reasonable to potential customers. For a \$2,000,000 hardware, the software price is usually a small fraction, no matter how amazing and differentiated the software is. Thus, the price recommendation should entail the manager putting themselves in the customer’s shoes and thinking like a customer rather than simply crunching the numbers and putting forward one particular method. This is the main learning from this case.

***4. How should Nathan manage the sales team transition to value-based pricing? What training and support will they need?***

There are a lot of issues that can be probed here, depending on the makeup and interest levels of the class. Maverick’s sales team faces a fundamental shift from selling premium-branded equipment based on an established reputation and well-known features among technically competent customers to consultative selling that emphasizes quantified customer value in a more skeptical customer base.

Currently, Maverick's sales representatives are experienced in selling to major operators using relationship-based approaches where technical superiority and brand reputation drive purchasing decisions. The transition to independent operators will require different skills, as these customers are likely to demand detailed economic justification and shorter payback periods while maintaining sophisticated technical evaluation capabilities. What’s more, they may simply have hard budget constraints that prevent them from buying this solution simply because they can’t afford it (and don’t have the ability to raise funds to buy it). This practical issue should also play a role in the pricing decision.

Sales training must focus on developing financial analysis skills that enable representatives to calculate and present customer-specific ROI scenarios. Representatives need tools such as formation-specific ROI calculators, operational savings models, and comparative total cost of ownership analyses that demonstrate PADS value across different drilling scenarios. Training should emphasize consultative questioning techniques that uncover customer-specific value drivers, capital constraints, and operational priorities that influence purchasing decisions.

The compensation structure also requires careful consideration under these new scenarios, as pricing that incorporates value in any shape or form typically results in fewer but higher-value transactions compared to volume-based selling. The current 65% base salary and 35% commission split may need adjustment to support longer sales cycles and more complex value justification processes. Commission structures should reward value realization rather than simple volume, potentially including bonuses for achieving target price levels or customer satisfaction metrics.

Sales support tools must include competitive battle cards that position NaviRig + PADS against multiple DrillPro systems, technical documentation that substantiates performance claims, and case study materials from pilot customers. Representatives will need access to engineering support for technical discussions and financial modeling tools that enable real-time scenario analysis during customer meetings. The sales process should incorporate formal value assessment meetings where technical and financial stakeholders evaluate total economic impact rather than initial purchase price alone.

***5. How should Nathan address potential competitive responses from TEI?***

TEI will likely respond aggressively to the NaviRig + PADS launch given the threat it poses to their dominant market position. Anticipated responses include immediate price reductions on DrillPro systems to maintain cost advantage (this is the most likely scenario that is likely to occur quickly depending on the competence and alertness of TEI’s managers), accelerated development of competing software solutions (unknown and hard to deal with from Nathan and Maverick’s perspective), and intensified marketing emphasizing their “Made in America” positioning and established customer relationships. TEI may also explore bundling strategies that combine DrillPro systems with complementary services or favorable financing terms. In other words, Maverick should expect that they will try everything possible to undermine NaviRig + PADS.

Maverick’s defensive strategies to deal with potential TEI response should focus on protecting the core competitive advantage provided by PADS. Ensuring comprehensive patent protection for PADS technology will create legal barriers to direct copying, while developing long-term service contracts and software update programs will create customer switching costs, reducing vulnerability to competitive pricing. Continuous R&D investment in next-generation capabilities is also recommended because it will maintain technological leadership and provide ongoing differentiation opportunities. Market education efforts that establish PADS as the industry standard for advanced drilling software will create switching costs for customers who invest in training and process adaptation.

Some offensive ideas are also warranted here. These should leverage Maverick's technological advantage for rapid market penetration. Aggressive sales and marketing efforts targeting key independent operators can potentially capture market share before TEI develops competitive responses. Partnership development with complementary service providers will create additional value for customers while concurrently building barriers to competitive entry. Geographic expansion into new basins where TEI has limited presence will reduce direct competition while building market position. Publishing case studies and technical papers that document actual performance benefits will build credibility and industry recognition for PADS technology.

The timing of competitive response also requires careful consideration from Maverick executives. TEI's ability to develop competing software solutions will require significant time and investment, creating a window of opportunity for market penetration. However, their established customer relationships and pricing advantages will provide defensive capabilities that Maverick must overcome through a clear demonstration of superior value delivery.

***6. What are the key implementation challenges Nathan must address?***

There are both internal and external implementation challenges to consider. To the extent that value-based pricing is part of the final value proposition pitched to customers, the internal implementation challenges will center on developing organizational capabilities required for value-based pricing success. The sales team will require extensive training to transition from feature-based selling to value-based consultative selling, including the development of financial analysis skills and customer value quantification techniques. Creating effective value communication tools such as ROI calculators, performance models, and case study documentation requires coordination across engineering, marketing, and sales organizations. Establishing pricing flexibility frameworks that accommodate formation-specific and customer-specific value drivers while maintaining pricing discipline represents a significant operational challenge.

On the external side, introducing PADS will encounter customer resistance to new pricing models, as many will remain accustomed to traditional cost-based approaches. Educating independent operators will prove critical, requiring them to embrace value quantification and shift to evaluating total cost of ownership over initial costs. Managing competitive responses from TEI will demand vigilant monitoring of market dynamics and strategic adjustments to maintain Maverick’s position while avoiding destabilizing price wars.

Effective performance measurement will rely on robust systems to monitor financial and market acceptance metrics, including pricing realization, customer adoption of value propositions, competitive win rates, and sustained satisfaction with PADS performance. Regular feedback loops and agile strategy updates will be vital to adapt pricing models to evolving market conditions and competitor actions.

Delivering PADS’ promised benefits will necessitate significant investment in customer support, training, and field service capabilities. Ensuring consistent performance in real-world conditions will be crucial to uphold credibility and justify premium pricing. Any failure to meet performance expectations can erode the value-based pricing strategy and weaken Maverick’s market standing. This risk is something the executive team needs to be mindful of.

***7. How should pricing strategy evolve as the market matures?***

The pricing strategy for PADS will evolve through distinct phases, adapting to shifting market dynamics and competitive conditions. In the launch phase during Year 1, premium pricing will target early adopters, capitalizing on limited production capacity and first-mover advantages while prioritizing technology performance validation and the cultivation of reference customers. Success stories and validated performance will bolster premium positioning and lay the groundwork for broader market expansion. You should emphasize to students the tension between short-term revenue goals and long-term competitive positioning here, especially given what is at stake here for Maverick, encouraging discussion on how PADS can balance premium pricing with future cost considerations, using case examples to illustrate impacts on market share and profitability.

During the growth phase in Years 2-3, gradual price adjustments will become necessary as competitors respond and market acceptance grows. Volume-based pricing for larger customers will likely be required to secure major accounts, while geographic and application expansion will unlock new revenue streams. Service-based revenue models will gain prominence as customers demand ongoing support and software updates, generating recurring revenue to complement equipment sales. You should direct students to analyze how PADS’ pricing strategy must adapt to competitive responses and customer expectations in this phase, prompting debate on the risks and benefits of international expansion versus domestic market focus.

As the market matures in Year 4 and beyond, commodity pricing pressures will emerge as competitive alternatives proliferate and customers grow more price-sensitive. Cost reduction efforts will be critical to preserve margins, while next-generation technology development will offer opportunities for renewed differentiation. Platform strategies leveraging PADS technology across multiple product lines will create economies of scale and sustain competitive advantages. International expansion will open new markets with varying competitive dynamics and pricing potential. Developing markets will present premium pricing opportunities, while established international markets will demand competitive positioning. Acquisitions will offer access to complementary technologies or customer bases, supporting sustained pricing power.

This evolution will require a delicate balance between maximizing short-term revenue and profitability and investing in long-term competitive positioning. Technology lifecycle management will grow critical as PADS capabilities become industry standards and new innovations arise. Strategic decisions on market share versus profitability trade-offs will shape overall business objectives and ensure sustainable competitive positioning.

#### **Teaching Suggestions**

The case works effectively as a 90-minute class session that begins with 15 minutes of context setting and industry overview, followed by 45 minutes of analytical discussion covering the pricing methods, particularly cost-based pricing and value-based pricing in detail. The decision phase requires 25 minutes for pricing recommendation development and implementation planning, with 5 minutes reserved for key learning synthesis and real-world application discussion.

Quantitative analysis should emphasize students' calculation of economic value using provided data, reinforcing analytical skills while demonstrating value-based pricing principles in practice. Role-playing exercises that assign students to represent different stakeholders explore internal alignment challenges and decision-making dynamics. Competitive simulation exercises where teams develop pricing strategies for both Maverick and TEI enhance understanding of competitive dynamics and strategic interaction effects.

Case comparison with other technology pricing situations reinforces learning about B2B pricing strategies and helps students recognize common patterns across different industries and competitive situations. The mathematical calculations required for the four pricing approaches provide concrete analytical practice, while the strategic recommendations require synthesis of quantitative analysis with qualitative market factors.

#### **Key Takeaways**

Value-based pricing will be most effective when products deliver clear, measurable benefits to customers, but its implementation will demand advanced tools for communicating value and adapting sales processes. Also, common-sense constraints about absolute values need to be taken into account. Students should realize that most of the economic value generated by products and services must be given away to customers. Pricing strategies will need to align with the unique characteristics of market segments and the decision-making processes of customers, acknowledging that diverse customer groups will require tailored value propositions and pricing models. Bundling technology will offer opportunities to capture value, but it will necessitate careful evaluation of how complementary products interact and whether customers perceive greater value in integrated or standalone offerings.

In concentrated markets with entrenched competitors, planning for competitive responses will be critical. This will involve developing defensive strategies to safeguard advantages and offensive strategies to gain market share before competitors can mount an effective counter. The success of the pricing strategy will hinge on internal alignment and capability building across sales, marketing, and technical teams, underscoring the need for organizational preparedness to execute the pricing approach.

The case will illustrate that pricing decisions will extend well beyond basic cost-based calculations, encompassing strategic positioning, competitive dynamics, customer value creation, and organizational capability needs. You should guide students to recognize that an effective pricing strategy will integrate financial analysis, market insight, competitive intelligence, and operational execution.
