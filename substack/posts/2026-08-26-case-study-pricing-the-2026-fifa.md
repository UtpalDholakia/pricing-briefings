### **Introduction**[^1]

It was September 8, 2025, and Heimo Schirgi was poring over a table of numbers on his computer screen with grim concentration, checking and double-checking them. In less than 48 hours, they would be scrutinized by billions of football supporters worldwide. As the COO of the FIFA World Cup 2026, Schirgi’s responsibilities included stadium readiness, transportation, safety, and security. But none was more significant and subject to public opinion than pricing the 7 million World Cup tickets. Adding to its significance, this would be the first men’s World Cup to be co-hosted by three nations and the first to expand to 48 teams, further increasing the profile of this already high-profile global sporting event. The first ticket sales window was slated to open on September 10. It would be a presale draw for Visa cardholders, and would operate on a pricing principle unprecedented in the tournament’s 96-year history: World Cup ticket prices would not be fixed by FIFA, but would vary.

The decision to let prices vary with demand, which the press had already begun (erroneously) calling “dynamic pricing,” was a radical departure from prior World Cups. In every World Cup from USA 1994 to Qatar 2022, FIFA had published a fixed schedule of prices by seating category months in advance. Now, FIFA was proposing to abandon fixed prices and replace them with an online market where tickets could be repriced based on what fans were willing to pay. The rationale for varying prices was that the significant premiums normally enjoyed by scalpers and others in the secondary market could be harvested instead by FIFA to reinvest globally in footballing initiatives. But the move was clearly risky. FIFA had long described the tournament as belonging to “the whole world.” A pricing model resembling an online travel agency threatened that ideal.

*“I think it’s key to highlight FIFA’s mission and FIFA’s objective of providing funding, providing opportunities, providing growth to our sport across all of the 211 member associations. And as part of that mission, which we take very seriously, we’re looking at optimizing the revenue, but also optimizing attendance in the stadium, right? It’s always a balance between different factors.” *— Heimo Schirgi, COO, FIFA World Cup 2026, media briefing, September 10, 2025 (Philadelphia Inquirer)

On that September morning, the key question of whether that balance had been struck remained open, with little leeway afforded to Schirgi and his team. Unlike his predecessors, who set ticket prices, published them, and lived with the ramifications, Schirgi and his colleagues were about to implement a pricing model where prices could be adjusted over time. The flexibility was the whole point, but also the primary source of serious risk. If they set prices too high, they would be seen as betraying ordinary fans; but if they were set too low, FIFA would leave on the table the revenue that funded football in 211 countries. What’s more, every step of the pricing exercise and its outcomes would be under a harsh public spotlight, discussed in real time all the way through to the World Cup’s end.

### **Revenue Expectations for the 2026 World Cup**

The Fédération Internationale de Football Association (FIFA) was founded in 1904 and is based in Zurich. It is a nonprofit association governed by Swiss law. FIFA has no shareholders; instead, it comprises 211 national associations across six regional confederations. The FIFA Congress is its highest authority, with each member association having one vote. Between Congresses, a 37-member Council sets FIFA’s strategy. President Gianni Infantino, who has held the position since 2016, serves as the organization’s public face. FIFA plans and reports its finances in four-year cycles that end with the men’s World Cup, rather than by calendar year.

Along with the Olympic Games, the FIFA World Cup is one of the world’s largest recurring sporting events. For the 2023–2026 cycle, FIFA had forecast earning approximately \$11 billion in total revenue. FIFA called this figure “unprecedented,” given that it was approximately \$4.6 billion higher than revenue in the previous cycle. The money was to be generated from four main sources (see Exhibit 2). Television broadcast rights were expected to generate about \$4.3 billion, marketing and sponsorship rights about \$2.7 billion, and licensing about \$0.7 billion. The remaining \$3.1 billion was to come from hospitality rights and ticket sales. While ticketing generated less revenue than either broadcasting or sponsorship, it directly impacted fans’ pocketbooks. In contrast, broadcast and sponsorship deals were negotiated years in advance and contractually fixed. Most football fans never saw or were concerned with these costs.

### **An Unprecedented Sporting Celebration**

As if pricing 7 million tickets weren’t enough of a challenge, other contextual factors surrounding the 2026 FIFA World Cup made the pricing task even more formidable. The tournament would be staged across the United States, Canada, and Mexico in 16 host cities with significantly different costs of living. Furthermore, it would be the first World Cup with 48 teams and 104 matches, up from 32 teams and 64 matches in prior World Cups. It would begin on June 11, 2026, at the storied Estadio Azteca in Mexico City and end with the final match on July 19 at MetLife Stadium in New York (see Exhibit 1).

This larger size increased the challenge of the ticket pricing strategy. With 104 matches spread over five and a half weeks and across 3 time zones, demand would vary widely. A group-stage match between Curacao and Ecuador, or Uzbekistan vs. DR Congo, would be very different in terms of demand when compared to a knockout match between the US and Belgium or Mexico and England. A flat pricing approach would not work or make sense with this dynamic. FIFA obviously expected demand to be driven by the residents of the three host nations, as well as from tourists around the world. One easy thing to do was to treat each host country as a single segment, given differences in income levels and reference prices for what a ticket to a sporting event should cost.

As in previous World Cups, FIFA also offered upscale packages called “Official Hospitality” for the 2026 FIFA World Cup. These packages included tickets, gourmet meals, parking, and access to special lounges, among other amenities, with prices in the tens of thousands of dollars per person for the most popular matches. Because they were targeted at ultra-wealthy fans around the world, they would prioritize affordability and accessibility for the remaining tickets. For purposes of the ticketing strategy, these packages were excluded from the analysis.

### **The History of World Cup Ticket Pricing**

FIFA had used the same category-based pricing structure for World Cup tickets for decades. For each World Cup stadium, FIFA established three or four price categories. Category 1 usually had the best seats, and Category 3 had the cheapest seats, farthest from the action. There was also a Category 4, reserved for the host country’s citizens, with the lowest prices. Its goal was to provide locals with access. FIFA published its price schedule by category and competition stage well in advance of the ticket sales dates. Because of high demand, FIFA used one or more lotteries with application windows to sell tickets. This also allowed it to gauge demand and allocate tickets across the price categories to manage its revenue and sales goals.

Average ticket prices rose over time, but while the most expensive seats increased substantially, the cheapest tickets remained within reach of ordinary fans (see Exhibit 3). At USA 1994, for instance, first-round tickets started around \$25, and the most expensive final seats cost a few hundred dollars. By Russia 2018, the cheapest non-resident group-stage tickets were priced at around \$105, and a top-category final seat cost about \$1,100. At Qatar 2022, group-stage tickets started at \$69 for foreign visitors, and were as low as \$11 for residents, while the top final category was priced at \$1,617 (see Exhibit 4). The pattern across three decades was thus consistent, with a wide range between the cheapest and most expensive seats, an explicit discount for locals, and fixed prices that were known months in advance.

With its new pricing approach, FIFA was moving away from the predictability of ticket prices. The reasons for this were straightforward. Setting prices for millions of tickets well in advance, even before the schedule was set or the actual participants were known, was a huge gamble. It made more sense to price each match rather than to price categories across all stages of the tournament. That way, FIFA wouldn’t let scalpers keep the huge markups from marquee matchups, nor would it leave seats empty in stadiums for relatively low-profile group matches. As with the Olympic Games, the secondary market had grown into a large, unofficial repricing system for World Cups, with thousands of participants, many of them organized groups, skimming value from official ticket prices, resulting in undesirable outcomes such as fraud and organized criminal activity. FIFA saw demand-based pricing as a way to circumvent some of these problems and retain more of the World Cup’s economic value.

### **Introducing Demand-Based Pricing to World Cup Tickets**

Although revolutionary and controversial for World Cup ticketing, demand-based pricing is an established pricing model. Its modern origins lie in the airline industry, where, after deregulation in 1978 in the United States, carriers led by American Airlines developed so-called yield management methods. The airlines adjusted fares frequently to fill seats while charging full price to those willing to pay. Hotels followed suit, and eventually so did live sports and live entertainment offerings. In 2009, the San Francisco Giants, a Major League Baseball team, adopted dynamic pricing by varying ticket prices for individual games based on the opponent, time of day, day of the week, and weather. The team reported that their revenues increased substantially after adopting this pricing approach. When selling concert tickets, Ticketmaster introduced a separate “Platinum” price tier, based on demand. In both examples, the seller’s logic was that selling tickets at a fixed price for what is essentially a variable demand offering doesn’t make sense. Depending on the drivers of demand, the seller will either underprice and leave scalpers or others to reap the windfall or overprice and leave many tickets unsold.

Compared to these versions, FIFA characterized its demand-based pricing as far more moderate. In the case of the 2026 World Cup, ticket prices would increase or decrease, not just increase. Thus, when tickets for a particular match were not selling, they would get cheaper. They also claimed that price changes would be moderate relative to variable pricing in other sports or to prices on third-party platforms like StubHub. One FIFA representative explained that, “We are regulating the access to tickets…the curves of how the prices are adjusted are not as steep” as they might otherwise be.

This meant that FIFA did not publish a full price table for the 2026 World Cup that applied throughout the World Cup. Before the first ticket sale, it disclosed only the price ranges for the four ticket categories: \$60 for a group-stage match on the low end and \$6,730 for the final on the high end. They would decide the intermediate prices within the range as sales progressed (see Exhibit 5). FIFA President Infantino said he viewed this pricing approach as market-driven rather than something FIFA controlled. “I didn’t know about \[dynamic pricing\],” he said as ticket sales approached, “but it means that the prices can go up or can go down. For us, the important element is to fill stadiums to give opportunities to people to come.”

To manage the sales of nearly 7 million tickets, FIFA used a series of sales events rather than selling them all at once (see Exhibit 6). First, a presale lottery for VISA cardholders would begin on September 10, 2025, followed by a so-called early ticket lottery, a third random selection lottery after the teams playing in the group stage matches had been decided, and finally a fourth and final sale of all leftover tickets on a first-come, first-served basis, in which buyers could choose specific seats.

Individuals were limited to purchasing four tickets per match and 40 across the tournament. Further, rather than leave resale (and potential profits from surging open-market prices) to third parties, FIFA would run its own official Resale Marketplace. To participate in the marketplace, the buyer and the seller each had to pay a 15% facilitation fee to FIFA. There was no ceiling on resale prices in the US and most of Canada; in jurisdictions with local laws capping sales at face value, FIFA followed them. FIFA didn’t want to cap prices where it didn’t have to, because it believed sellers would just go to StubHub and other such platforms, defeating the Resale Marketplace’s purpose.

### **The Checkered History of Dynamic Pricing**

Despite its seemingly superior and football-friendly economic logic, demand-based pricing came to FIFA with significant public relations baggage. The first issue was that terms such as dynamic pricing, surge pricing, demand-based pricing, and variable pricing were used interchangeably by the general public and the mainstream media, without appreciation of or concern for the nuanced differences among these methods (see Exhibit 7 for examples). In 2022, when Ticketmaster applied demand-based “Platinum” pricing to Bruce Springsteen’s tour, for instance, some seats reached \$4,000 to \$5,000. Not surprisingly, fans voiced their outrage, even though the company explained that a vast majority of tickets had sold at fixed, lower prices. A U.S. congressman demanded answers. Two years later, the reunion sale for the British band Oasis evinced a similar uproar, as the cheapest standing-room tickets more than doubled in price while fans waited in online queues to buy them. The UK’s competition regulator opened an investigation and later required Ticketmaster to disclose tiered prices more clearly and to give fans advance notice. The regulator’s chief executive, Sarah Cardell, said that “fans who spend their hard-earned money deserve to see clear, accurate information, upfront.”

These episodes pointed to two significant problems FIFA would have to manage with its 2026 ticket pricing. The first challenge was fairness. In theory, letting prices rise to meet demand results in the allocation of scarce resources to those who value them most; in practice, it means that tickets would be purchased by the richest consumers and businesses. It could also be argued that this approach will help generate more revenue, which FIFA could then funnel into football initiatives in developing countries. But fairness has to do with more than economic justifications; it must also consider fans’ perceptions of fairness and its consequences. The experienced fans knew what a World Cup ticket should cost based on their past purchases and on attending soccer matches in their own countries. Any price much higher than what fans considered reasonable would make it seem as though FIFA was exploiting the occasion to overcharge.

The second problem was transparency. The opacity of the pricing method was the culprit behind the Springsteen and Oasis cases that aroused fans’ ire. They did not understand how a price had been established in the first place, how quickly it would change, or what would drive the change. FIFA could say it was sharing only the price ranges rather than publishing a full pricing schedule to allow for some flexibility. However, this was a risky proposition.

### **The Key Pricing Decisions That Remained**

By September 2025, most of the price structure had already been established. However, one important decision still being debated was how much weight to give to the objective of maximizing revenue versus other competing goals. An agreement still hadn’t been reached, which meant several questions remained unanswered.

#### ***How many low-priced tickets to sell and how to distribute them across the tournament?***

FIFA had already announced that its ticket prices would range from \$60 to \$6,730, with hospitality packages costing even more on a per-ticket basis. Having such a wide range gave FIFA the flexibility to decide how much to cross-subsidize cheaper tickets with sales of more expensive tickets. For every ticket costing thousands of dollars, FIFA had the latitude to offer more tickets for under \$100. The big question remained: How many low-priced seats should FIFA offer overall, and how should it distribute them across the tournament? The related issue was how to manage public relations surrounding ticket prices because no matter how many cheaper tickets they offered, the media and influencers would still focus on the most expensive tickets and try to manufacture outrage.

#### ***Should FIFA use actual dynamic pricing (like an airline or Uber) or just stick with variable pricing?***

There was a fundamental difference between dynamic pricing as practiced by airlines and rideshare companies, and the more restrained, graduated form of price change in response to demand that companies in the CPG and professional services space practiced. So far, FIFA had favored the restrained form of variable pricing that wasn’t really dynamic, but it still had the leeway to decide if this made sense for its Resale Marketplace. The key question was: How often should prices change in the Resale Marketplace and who decided what those prices should be?

#### ***How should FIFA serve local fans through its ticket pricing?***

In previous World Cups, FIFA had used the fourth category of its pricing schedule to offer a significant discount to local fans. Essentially, local fans got Category 1, 2, and 3 seats at discounted prices. This time round, there was an additional complexity. There were three host nations, very different from one another in willingness and ability to pay for football game tickets. What sort of pricing strategy would work to support American, Canadian and Mexican fans, as well as fans from all over the world who had likely saved up for years for this once-in-a-lifetime experience? The tension lay in the fact that FIFA wanted these fans to buy and use the tickets, not for opportunistic people to buy low and then resell for a handsome profit.

#### ***How should FIFA manage the resale marketplace?***

In launching its own marketplace, FIFA had made the strategic decision to control the ticket resale process as much as possible. However, its decision to charge buyers and sellers 15% of the ticket price each, and additionally leave prices uncapped in the US market and most of Canada, meant that it could be accused of money-grabbing by the media and fans. Would it appear as if FIFA was trying to take advantage of fans by profiting twice (or more) from ticket sales, by selling the ticket once and then taking a healthy chunk of the resale price as well? What, if anything, could FIFA do, to streamline the ticket sales process in its Resale Marketplace to reduce such negative perceptions?

### **FIFA’s Many Competing Constituents**

FIFA’s ticket pricing choices were further complicated by the fact that FIFA served many different constituents, each with competing priorities. FIFA’s 211 national federations all depended on the ticketing revenue earned by the World Cup. This money would directly impact football development budgets worldwide for several years, so the revenue earned was important. Broadcaster and sponsors had other priorities. They had paid FIFA billions of dollars to publicize their brand, so they wanted full stadiums with enthusiastic crowds cheering, not large swaths of empty seats that had been a source of embarrassment to the organizers of previous World Cups and Olympic Games. The governments of the host cities wanted local residents to partake in the once-in-a-lifetime experience and would react negatively if locals felt like they’d been priced out. And supporters’ organizations spoke on behalf of the traveling fans who provided much of the vibes and who were least able to pay a five-figure resale price to watch a match.

It would be impossible to satisfy everyone. Maximizing revenue ran counter to the goal of fostering affordability. Affordability undermined the goal of disintermediating scalpers and secondary-market sellers. Filling stadiums argued for lower average ticket prices, while funding the sport’s development budget argued for higher prices. As the organizers of several recent Olympic Games had found out, no matter what they did, FIFA would leave some groups unhappy and a few seething with fury. They could only decide which trade-offs to make to satisfy most of their constituents to the greatest extent, and equally important, how to defend their decisions in the ruthless court of public opinion.

### **Two Days To Go**

As he worked with grim concentration to finalize the ticket pricing strategy, Schirgi knew that whatever approach FIFA used would be judged repeatedly over ten months of phased sales, through comparisons between supporters’ expectations (often too low) and the actual ticket price. FIFA’s leadership was confident that the logic of its pricing approach was sound and that, by using a modern, demand-based pricing model, it would be able to fill seats and capture more of the economic value that scalpers and resellers normally pocketed. Yet in the Springsteen and Oasis concert ticket sales, a similar pricing logic had resulted in angry fans and widespread angst.

The ticket presale lottery was less than 48 hours away. There was still room to adjust the pricing structure for the 2026 FIFA World Cup. No matter what approach it used, tickets would sell because there was more demand than seats to be filled at virtually any price. The key challenge was what kind of tournament the pricing approach would create.
