###### ***Summary.** *It’s a time of turmoil in pricing AI products. Virtually every SaaS company, not to mention companies selling pure AI products, is grappling with which pricing model to use. However, an even greater challenge lies in developing the organizational capability to keep the selected pricing model aligned even as the product, its costs, customer value, usage, and the competitive context change quickly. In this post, I develop the *AI Pricing Alignment Framework* (AIPAF), which organizes the AI pricing challenge around six linked concepts: customer value, costs, metrics, risks, usage, and realized prices. Drawing upon recent academic research and the recent pricing decisions by Workday, GitHub, Cursor, Atlassian, and UiPath, I discuss the specific ways in which the AIPAF helps organizations understand and develop their pricing capability. The main lesson is that AI pricing models have a short shelf life in the current dynamic AI pricing landscape, and no single model is dominant. The AI vendors pricing most effectively are those who can measure the relationships among the AIPAF constructs, evaluate them rigorously, monitor the performance of their pricing model, and make adjustments quickly before a small misalignment turns into a major economic or customer problem.

### **Introduction**

Consider a SaaS company working on its first renewal of one of its flagship AI offerings with a leading customer. When it initially signed the customer, the company used a conventional seat-based pricing model that had been in place for over a decade. But over the previous year, pretty much everything about the product and the customer has changed. The product now has a significant agentic component, enabling interdepartmental functionality with significant value to the customer that no one had envisaged before. The usage and, correspondingly, the costs associated with serving this customer have skyrocketed to the point where it’s no longer sustainable. Inside the SaaS company, there’s a strong push to move to a hybrid model where consumption is metered and priced in some way, while the product folks want the opportunity to keep building out their offering and adding more features (and hopefully more value to customers). The salespeople are floundering because their previous SaaS-based benchmarks are virtually useless, and customer value delivery use cases are evolving so fast that they’ve been hard to pin down and convert into usable sales pitches. This sort of situation is a hot mess, but it’s one that is all too common at the moment in the world of pricing AI products.

Pricing models were relatively settled in the pre-AI SaaS world, established on super-low marginal costs. Things like free trials, tiered pricing based on seat count, quantity discounts, annual subscriptions, and high gross margins were the bread and butter of the industry for decades. AI changed every part of this price structure. In the new world, two customers who used to cost about the same to serve can now be orders of magnitude different. Seat-based pricing becomes irrelevant when part of the AI product’s value proposition is the ability to deploy agents by the hundreds (or tens of millions) to take over whatever the “seat” and everyone else around was doing. And worst of all, with new features and capabilities constantly being added to AI products, who knows what the costs and customer value will look like in a few weeks, let alone at the next renewal?

In this post, I argue that rather than debating about which pricing model is better, e.g., whether seats are a better unit of pricing or are credits a better unit, the bigger pricing challenge that enterprises selling AI products (and that includes both traditional SaaS and newer pure-AI) must deal with is an organizational pricing capability gap. Academic researchers have argued that a business can sell products delivering substantial value but still fail to capture this value if its internal pricing resources, routines, skills, and authority structures are lacking.[^1] AI increases the difficulty of this already formidable challenge by bringing dynamism into the mix. Because everything is changing so quickly, the key pricing decision inputs of costs, customer value, reference prices, and the value proposition (my so-called four pillars of pricing decision making in the [Value Pricing Framework](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/pricing-tool-the-value-pricing-framework)) need to be constantly considered and adjusted. I call this concept “AI pricing capability” and take a stab at thinking through what it means.

To build out this argument, I propose and develop the AI Pricing Alignment Framework (AIPAF) in this piece. The AIPAF begins with customer value and costs, which serve as the primary economic inputs into the pricing decision, within a particular competitive and market context. The AI product vendor then chooses a metric and allocates risks between itself and the customer. These choices determine how customers use the product, ultimately influencing the price the vendor can realize. The AIPAF is deliberately recursive in that usage and realized prices reveal whether the earlier assumptions about value, costs, metrics, and risks were reasonable, thereby laying the groundwork for the next pricing decision. AI pricing capability is defined here as the organization’s capacity to keep these six relationships aligned as the product offering and its use by customers evolve.

### **Case Study: Workday’s Predicament**

Workday is a prototypical example of an enterprise SaaS company dealing with these issues. It’s a traditional SaaS company that grew fat on a relatively stable, high-quality offering with a subscription pricing model largely tied to customer workforce size. Before agentic AI, Workday used its customers’ employee counts as a proxy for deployment scale and value delivered, quoting prices largely based on the number of employees or workers covered and the specific applications purchased. This made sense. More employees generally meant more employee records, payroll transactions, benefit elections, recruiting activity, and other work for Workday’s products to support.

But with the infusion of AI into Workday’s products and customers’ workflows, the link between employee count and the value delivered by Workday’s offerings has weakened significantly. This is because two customers with the same employee count may derive vastly different amounts of value (and incur costs to serve) based on how widely, frequently, and intensively they use agents. To be clear, employee counts may remain a reasonable proxy for value when agent activity scales predictably with workforce size. It becomes much less informative when similarly sized customers use agents in very different ways and enjoy different outcomes.

Even worse, AI agents can substitute for human labor. A customer might reduce the number of employees performing HR, finance, recruiting, or administrative work while increasing the amount of work performed by Workday’s agents. In that case, sticking to employee-based pricing would result in a price reduction even as the value delivered by its products and the cost incurred to serve them skyrocket. Thus, the core question about Workday’s value changed from “How many employees does Workday’s product cover?” to “How much work is the system performing, and what is it worth?”

To address these quandaries, Workday introduced a hybrid pricing scheme called Flex Credits, which it characterizes as a universal, fungible credit that customers purchase annually in bulk and apply across eligible agents and platform innovations as they see fit. The company explains this change transparently, stating that credits are intended to be charged for completed actions and tasks, rather than for the number of employees covered by or accessing the software, or for tokens burnt during use.[^2] In Workday’s words, “Most usage is metered when a task is successfully completed in production.” Now, with this pricing model, there’s greater alignment between the price and the value. The customer still makes an annual financial commitment to Workday, creating predictability on both sides. But underlying that fixed commitment is a flexible arrangement that allows the customer to use Workday’s evolving product flexibly, in ways that make the most sense to them. Two additional structural aspects of Workday’s pricing are relevant. First, as of this writing, Flex Credits are complementary to Workday’s employee-based subscription pricing model rather than a full-on replacement, with an allotment that varies by company size; second, unused credits expire annually rather than roll over.

The Workday case shines a light on the core challenge with pricing AI products. One might think of Flex Credits as a creative way to calculate and charge a price for agents, but there’s more nuance here that the model leaves unaddressed. Before Workday can figure out an agent’s price for a task, it must make determinations about (1) what constitutes an economically meaningful task (that can be charged for), (2) whether failed or incomplete tasks should count, or conversely, a precise definition of a task-to-be-charged-for, (3) the relations between different tasks and number of credits, (4) how to help customers forecast their credit usage, and (5) how their revised pricing model (and value delivered) maps on to the older model that the customer has been familiar with for years. Also needing consideration is the impact of product evolution on pricing. As new agents are introduced and existing ones improve, how should they be accommodated into the pricing, and whether changes in their credit requirements constitute changes in price? Rather than simple pricing decisions, these are business strategy decisions that involve product design, engineering, finance, sales, contracting, customer success, and governance. Thus, the AI pricing strategy is an organizational capability. In terms of the AIPAF, Workday is trying to strengthen the relationship among customer value, costs, and the metric used to charge for the product. Flex Credits are its visible pricing response, but the company’s AI pricing capability lies in detecting the break, redesigning the metric and risk allocation, and revising them as usage changes.

### **The Variability in Customer Value Delivered by AI Products**

Much like SaaS offerings, the value of an AI product derives from how customers use it. Customers’ capabilities with respect to data, employee proficiency, and organizational processes all impact how it is used and the value derived from it. In the AI context, academic researchers have referred to these as “dynamic complementary assets” to acknowledge that these reflect “whether and to what extent organizations are capable of updating complementary assets that allow them to take advantage of AI tools even as the technology develops rapidly” (p. 427).[^3] A large field experiment has found that AI improves performance on tasks within its capabilities while reducing accuracy on tasks beyond its capabilities. A separate line of research finds that the advantages associated with AI often arise from combining the technology with complementary human and organizational capabilities rather than from its use alone.[^4] Thus, two customers can buy the same AI product and realize radically different amounts of value from it.

What’s more, as with any new technology, the value the product delivers to customers will change over time. On the customer side, employees will learn which tasks to delegate to agents, and managers will redesign workflows and assign agents to multidisciplinary tasks. On the vendor side, the AI products will evolve rapidly, adding new features and capabilities at a blistering pace. For pricing decisions, the important distinction lies among the potential (unmeasurable) value that could be generated by AI products, the value actually realized by the customer, and the portion of that actual realized value captured by the AI vendor at the price. With learning and improvements on both sides, it seems natural that all three value characteristics will change, and, consequently, pricing will need to adjust quickly. This instability is why customer value and costs are the starting inputs in the AIPAF and are also recursively affected through feedback. Thus, they are inputs that must be recalibrated in this volatile environment.

### **AI Pricing Capability Requires Translating Usage Parameters into Customer Value and Understanding Customer Behavior**

While Workday moved the unit of pricing closer to completed actions and thus to customer value delivered, GitHub moved in the opposite direction with its pricing strategy, announcing in April 2026 that its premium-request metric would be replaced by GitHub AI Credits in June. These credits are denominated in dollars and consumed based on token counts at each model’s listed API rates, thereby tending towards a cost-based pricing model.[^5] GitHub’s premium-request metric was based on the assumption that one customer request was comparable to another. When this assumption became economically untenable, value-based pricing had to be ditched. Where pricing capability is concerned, the important lesson is that even a broadly sensible pricing metric can quickly become obsolete as technology changes. An AI vendor may have to shift dramatically from emphasizing customer value to protecting itself against rapidly escalating costs. It must have the culture, information, decision rights, and processes required to recognize when such a change is necessary and implement it before the existing model becomes economically unsustainable. In AIPAF terms, the request metric stopped tracking costs, forcing GitHub to redesign the pricing model even though the replacement metric may track customer value less closely.

Such significant shifts also require the vendor to anticipate how the pricing model change will change customer behavior. Cursor discovered this issue last year when it introduced usage-based pricing without making the consequences sufficiently clear, and subsequently had to apologize and offer refunds for unexpected charges that users had incurred over the preceding three weeks.[^6] Emerging academic research is finding that customer behavior does change in response to pricing changes in AI products. In a recent study, when costs from a usage-based pricing model were made salient to users, the authors found that:[^7]

“increased costs lead to greater use of HDS and lower use of GDS, suggesting that the shift is not driven solely by cost minimization. Rather, it reflects users’ desire for controllability. Users craft more precise prompts, reduce solution diversity, and provide more active guidance to the system. As a result, they adjust the search boundary more frequently and adopt GDS mainly in later stages, when the boundary is usually more refined and probabilistic tweaks are more effective.” (Abstract)

Here, HDS and GDS refer to human-guided delegated search and genAI-based delegated search, respectively. This was even though the labor cost per hour was higher for human use than for gen-AI, suggesting the intriguing possibility that changes to pricing models may give customers’ employees a reason to delegate less authority to AI and take back some of the control they perceive themselves as losing. Thus, a company with strong AI pricing capability must therefore evaluate a pricing model on three dimensions at once: (1) whether it reflects costs, (2) whether customers can understand and control them, and (3) whether the behavior it encourages increases or diminishes the value realized from the product. In the AIPAF, a metric is therefore both a device for charging the customer and a mechanism that affects usage, with consequences for realized customer value and realized prices.

### **AI Pricing Capability Requires Stress-Testing Metrics Before Price Implementation**

Atlassian’s approach to Rovo credits illustrates another dimension of AI pricing capability. The company has introduced credit allowances, variable consumption rates, and administrative dashboards, but it is not currently charging customers when general Rovo usage exceeds the included allowance.[^8] As of this writing, Atlassian promises at least 90 days’ notice before imposing such charges and requires customers to opt in before incurring them. This approach allows the company to test the pricing metric before fully starting to charge in accordance with its pricing model. During the test, Atlassian can see how usage behavior varies across its customer base, which product features consume the most credits, the extent to which allowances are predictable or fluctuate, and how effectively customers monitor and control their usage. Likewise, customers can learn how best to use Rovo credits to achieve their goals and get a sense of how much they might need to spend in the future when Atlassian starts charging them.

Where pricing capability is concerned, the key lesson from the Atlassian approach is that measurement needs to precede monetization. In the typical AI setting that is rife with uncertainty, a vendor can use practices like nonbinding allowances, provisional credit systems, or discretionary refunds of charges incurred to determine whether its proposed pricing metric balances costs and customer value, generates predictable bills for customers, and can be administered without excessive complexity in communication or execution. The AI product vendor may forgo some immediate revenue, but will get useful information to reduce the risk of a pricing rollout that generates outrage and causes reputational harm. Note that even this cautious, customer-centered approach to changing price models or adding pricing components is not a panacea. Once customers have incorporated AI usage activities based on the current pricing into their workflows, activating overage charges is still likely to be experienced as a price increase (not necessarily to users, but to those paying the tab). Thus, pricing capability also requires processes and safeguards to discover and test new metrics, and to use those that pass the test to create new pricing models without making customers feel that the original value proposition has been diluted. Seen through the lens of the AIPAF, Atlassian is using the experimental period to observe the relationships among a new metric, risk sources, customer usage, and eventual realized prices before fully committing to revising its pricing model.

### **AI Pricing Capability Requires Careful Definition of the Pricing Unit**

UiPath’s pricing for ScreenPlay illustrates another dimension of AI pricing capability, that is, the need for the vendor to carefully define what constitutes a unit of agent work. Under ScreenPlay’s current pricing model, a single run includes up to five user-interface actions, with additional groups of five actions consuming additional runs.[^9] There’s a second layer based on tiers, which imposes consumption or units at different rates (see the image below). This pricing structure spares customers from having to monitor and track every token or model call, but still leaves things relatively opaque (e.g., why is a run of five actions and not seven?). Essentially, what UiPath has done here is to generate a unit that converts complex technical activities into measurable, explainable pricing units.

In terms of AI pricing capability, this example shows the difficulty and importance of defining pricing units. Even after selecting the pricing unit, its definition needs to be tuned. Questions such as whether an agent that attempts five actions and fails should consume the same number of units as one that completes its task successfully, whether a workflow that requires six actions should cost twice as much as a workflow with five actions, and whether a premium model should consume more units when its higher quality reduces the number of retries, still need to be answered. Establishing each constraint in the pricing model shifts costs and risks between UiPath and its customers and creates incentives for UiPath’s product teams to design agent workflows in particular ways. Thus, AI pricing capability requires the AI product vendor to define, test, and revise the pricing unit as it learns how that unit balances between reflecting costs, corresponding to customer value, or encouraging efficient internal practices. From the AIPAF’s perspective, the five-action run connects costs and customer value to usage and risk allocation. Its suitability cannot be judged independently of those relationships.

### **The AI Pricing Alignment Framework (AIPAF)**

What is the makeup of an AI vendor’s pricing capability? The insights derived from the case studies considered thus far suggest that six constructs play a significant role: customer value, costs, metrics, risks, usage, and the realized prices. The AI pricing alignment framework explains how customer value and costs inform the choice of pricing metrics and allocation of risks; how these choices influence usage; and how the resulting realized prices provide information for subsequent pricing adjustments as AI product features and customer behaviors evolve rapidly.

As in the Value Pricing Framework I’ve developed and used for years, customer value and costs are the two principal inputs to the pricing decision (there’s a third input, “reference prices,” having to do with competition, more about that below). The AI vendor must estimate the incremental benefits the product can create for customers over the baseline, whether that baseline is a traditional previous-generation SaaS offering or another technology, and the costs it will incur in delivering these benefits to customers. Customer value sets the upper limit of a viable price, while cost sets its lower limit. These two limits establish a range of prices that customers may consider reasonable based on competing products, previous prices, employee costs, and other ways of performing the same work. The vendor’s value proposition determines how it weighs these considerations. For instance, a product positioned as an inexpensive productivity tool will be priced differently from one promising to complete a valuable business process. #### **How the AIPAF extends the Value Pricing Framework**

While a detailed exposition of the Value Pricing Framework is beyond the scope of this piece, it is still useful to establish the relations between the two. The AIPAF is based on the pricing logic in the Value Pricing Framework. Customer value and costs remain fundamental inputs to pricing decisions, and competitive and market contexts continue to influence customers’ reference prices and the range of feasible prices the AI vendor can charge for its offerings. The pricing model (characterized by price levels and price structures) continues to influence customer responses and commercial outcomes. A key difference lies in the amount of detail required to manage these relationships for AI products.

The AIPAF fleshes out the Value Pricing Framework’s ‘price structure’ concept into two explicit pricing design choices: the metric, which forms the basis for how the customer pays for the AI product, and the allocation of risk between vendor and customer. It treats usage as a distinct customer response because the price paid can encourage or suppress experimentation, delegation, and adoption. And like the Value Pricing Framework, the AIPAF focuses on realized prices as the relevant outcome, given that a slate of incentives determines how much value the vendor actually captures for AI products. The recursive path is equally important in both models, but takes on more significance given the rapid changes in this industry. AI makes customer value, costs, usage, and product capabilities sufficiently variable that observed outcomes must continually update the economic inputs and pricing design. Thus, relative to the Value Pricing Framework, the underlying pricing logic remains the same in the AIPAF, while the monitoring and reconfiguration requirements become much more demanding.

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/55a4fe7e-5063-4488-93f3-c8462e92f0a4_2933x1650.png)

Compared with other products and services, customer value and costs are both far less stable for AI products. As discussed earlier, the potential customer value can differ substantially from the value actually realized due to substantial variability in AI performance across tasks, workflows, users, and organizational contexts. Furthermore, AI value (at least at present) is invariably coproduced, in the sense that the vendor provides the model and product, while the customer contributes its data, workflows, employee skills, supervision, and willingness to redesign its existing processes. Customer readiness is therefore a core aspect of the value-producing mechanism in AI products. In a nutshell, the same AI product can create substantially different value for two seemingly comparable customers because of the differences in these complementary resources.[^10]

Costs are equally unstable, especially when considering costs and pricing decisions from the vantage of a traditional SaaS mindset. Variable costs are generally higher for AI products, and their variability is greater across customers and applications. This is because costs can vary substantially with the choice of model, the mode of retrieval, tool use, retries, supervision, implementation, and support. Consequently, two customers paying the same subscription price may derive very different amounts of value and incur very different costs for the vendor.

In the AI Pricing Alignment Framework, metrics and risk allocation are influenced by customer value and costs. The metric in the AIPAF corresponds closely to the price structure in the Value Pricing Framework. Its importance lies in the fact that it forms the basis of the AI product’s price. A variety of metrics are being used across the industry at present, including those based on employees and seats (as in the Workday case), tokens and credits, actions, completed tasks, and specific outcomes. None of these dominates at the moment; each one involves pros and cons. This is because of their complex relationships with customer value and costs. An ideal pricing metric should co-vary with the mechanism that generates customer value and correlate with the vendor’s costs. Very few pricing metrics can achieve both objectives simultaneously; consequently, the vendor has to make a trade-off when choosing a metric. When a metric closely aligns with customer value delivered, as in outcome-based pricing, it faces attribution challenges, and when it correlates with the vendor’s costs, it becomes harder to communicate the pricing logic to customers or justify the price.[^11]

As with pricing models in any other industry, pricing models for AI products must allocate the risks involved between vendor and customer. Each pricing model does this differently. For instance, a fixed subscription pricing model shifts most of the risk of extremely high usage to the vendor, whereas a credit- or token-based pricing model shifts it entirely to the customer. In contrast, outcome-based pricing may align the price paid more closely with the value the customer receives; however, it can create disputes over whether an outcome has occurred, its causes, and who should receive credit. This attribution problem is especially pernicious for AI products because multiple drivers of outcomes are distributed across the vendor and the customer. These may include, among others, the vendor’s model, the customer’s data and workflow, employee behavior (particularly their affinity to adopt AI for their day-to-day activities), managerial supervision, and external conditions.[^12]

Furthermore, the choice of pricing model influences customer usage in the AIPAF. As one example, given the rapid changes in the AI industry, customers’ propensity to experiment with the product will be adversely affected by its pricing. To understand the customer’s usage, the vendor must look beyond usage levels and examine its drivers. For instance, a customer with high usage may be someone who has successfully adopted the AI product across the organization, or it could reflect that the customer is having difficulties with inefficient use (e.g., employees are writing inefficient prompts or the agents are not well-designed). Similarly, a customer who reports low usage may be one who simply doesn’t derive value from the AI product, or has protocols in place that inhibit exploration and enthusiastic use by employees.

The importance of metric selection in the AIPAF lies in the fact that each metric provides some information to the vendor but omits other information. As an example, with token or credit pricing, the AI vendor and customer can see how much computing resources were consumed, but have little visibility into how much useful work the product helped the customer perform. On the other hand, with task-based pricing, completed activities are enumerated, but there is no insight into their importance or the cost to deliver them. Thus, each metric sheds light on one part of the value-creation process but leaves the others in the dark.

As shown in the figure, the realized price is the final commercial outcome in the AIPAF. The realized price (also known as the pocket price in the pricing vernacular) is the actual price earned by the vendor net of all incentives, which may take many forms such as discounts, allowances, pilot costs, service concessions, and payment terms. As in other B2B settings, the gap between the quoted or listed price and the realized price can be significant, reflecting both the industry context and the specific vendor’s sales and pricing negotiation capabilities. When evaluating a pricing model, it is important to consider both the recommended quoted price and the realized price to gain a complete sense of the model’s effectiveness for a given application. Realized price is increasingly used as a metric for business performance alongside other KPIs such as cost to serve, gross margin, churn rate, and lifetime value.

In the AIPAF, the relationships between the six primary variables are evolving. For instance, as customer value or costs shift, the suitability of an existing metric or risk allocation may also need to be changed. These, in turn, affect usage, which in turn affects realized value, cost, and price. Thus, a strong AI pricing capability requires four related activities: (1) sensing when these relationships are changing, (2) designing an appropriate pricing model, (3) executing it effectively, and (4) reconfiguring it when the original assumptions no longer hold. What’s more, these activities work in synchrony, so that better sensing has little value if the company cannot restructure its contracts with customers to accommodate the changes in inputs, and an analytically sound pricing structure won’t help unless salespeople can explain or implement it effectively.[^13] Finally, AI pricing capability lies in managing the tension among the variables in the AIPAF over time without compromising the product’s performance or the company’s reputation.

### **The AIPAF Changes the Decision Frame for Diagnosing Pricing Model Failures**

AI companies typically evaluate pricing models by examining their outputs, following common consulting practice. They ask whether the price generated sufficient revenue, produced an acceptable gross margin, supported adoption at a reasonable pace, and was received well during the sales process. These are obviously important questions to ask, but the AIPAF suggests that they may be insufficient for AI products. By the time an unwelcome outcome is translated into impacts on revenue, margin, or churn, the pricing model may already have been misaligned for months.

The AIPAF shifts attention from the final price to the relationships between the model variables by explicitly stating that the AI pricing model rests on a series of linked assumptions. The company assumes it understands the pricing decision inputs of customer value and costs, that the metrics and risk allocation approach it has chosen reasonably characterize these inputs, that the resulting pricing model will encourage the appropriate level of usage by customers (i.e., not suppress it), and that the gap between the quoted or listed price and the realized price will be modest and managed. A weakness in any one of these relationships can undermine the AI vendor’s pricing strategy.

The cases discussed earlier in this post illustrate specific interactions between the AIPAF’s variables. Workday’s employee-based metric lost relevance as customers adopted agents, weakening the relationship between workforce size and customer value. The request-based metric in GitHub’s pricing model became problematic when usage across customers began consuming vastly different amounts of computing resources, increasing the cost to serve for many customers beyond sustainable levels. Cursor’s pricing change affected customer perceptions because they could not anticipate or control their spending. UiPath’s ScreenPlay pricing required the company to decide how to group technical actions performed by its products into a pricing unit that made sense. Atlassian took a cautious approach, measuring the effects before implementing overage charges so that it and its customers could observe usage before the metric became part of the pricing model in use. Seen through the AIPAF’s lens, each challenge represents a different break in the chain connecting customer value, costs, metrics, risks, usage, and realized prices. Understanding where the break has occurred is necessary to choose the appropriate remedy.

Thus, the AIPAF provides a way to diagnose a pricing model failure before changing the price structure or level. It changes the pricing decision frame from “What pricing model should replace the current one?” to “Which relationship in the current pricing model has stopped working, and why?” Only after answering this deliberately can the company determine whether it should change the price, redefine the metric, reallocate risk, influence usage, reduce costs, improve value realization, or strengthen price execution.

#### **How to align the AIPAF factors to develop an effective pricing strategy for AI products**

In theory, adopting the AIPAF suggests that the AI product vendor should find the model’s weak link or links within their organization and work diligently to fix them. In practice, this can be difficult because the information needed to evaluate the six relationships is often scattered across the organization. For instance, product managers may understand the product’s features and most effective use cases, engineers may be knowledgeable about the model’s technical aspects, and customer success managers may be most attuned to customer usage. Similarly, financial performance will be tracked by finance, salespeople on the front lines will observe their clients’ reactions to prices quoted and how many incentives they have to offer, and legal and security teams will be conversant with the risks in the contract. No single organizational function has all the information required to evaluate the pricing model, and these functions often have adversarial relationships that discourage information sharing.

Thus, adopting and using the AIPAF requires more than collecting information from different functions. The framework describes an ongoing process that requires adjustment; consequently, the company has to first learn what is happening to the six relationships, then evaluate how a proposed pricing model will affect them, observe what happens after it is implemented, and respond when the original relationships among the AIPAF variables no longer hold. Using the AIPAF effectively requires the AI product company to answer four questions: (1) What is happening across the six relationships among the model constructs? (2) How will a proposed pricing model affect each of them individually as well as all of them jointly? (3) What happens after the pricing model is implemented with the customer? (4) And when does the evidence justify adjusting, or even completely revamping, the pricing model? I will consider each of the issues briefly here, noting that a larger discussion is warranted.

Consistent with traditional pricing best practice, the first requirement is to measure the six relationships in the AIPAF at the appropriate level of granularity, usually the customer-account level. Product-level averages can conceal customers that are realizing little value, workflows that consume resources disproportionate to the value they generate, and contracts whose realized prices no longer cover the vendor’s costs. Much like pocket price analysis, which is best done at the account level, the company should be able to connect customer value, costs, usage, and realized prices in the AIPAF for specific customers, while monitoring competing offers that may alter customers’ reference prices. Pricing decisions should be chosen and evaluated using joint criteria. A proposed credit, action, or outcome should be assessed not only for its measurability, but also for how closely it corresponds to customer value, affects cost variability and usage, allocates risk, and can be explained transparently to customers.

The company must continue monitoring these relationships after the pricing model is introduced. Customers need forecasts, dashboards, spending controls, and clear definitions of billable units. The vendor needs to know realized prices and contribution margins by customer account, and to understand customer usage. Atlassian’s decision to carefully measure how Rovo’s usage would affect its customers before launching overage pricing is an excellent example of how implementation can yield insights into customer behavior and the suitability of the pricing metric. The vendor must also establish when such evidence warrants changing some or all aspects of the pricing model. For instance, it may be enough to change how credits are converted into customer-performed tasks, while leaving the headline price unchanged. Frequent model changes are (obviously) not recommended; no customer likes instability. But waiting until renewal (which could be years away in many cases) may not be economically sustainable when the product offering and the economics behind it have changed substantially. Because the relevant information is often available in different business functions, as discussed earlier, AI pricing capability is best seen as an organization-level capability rather than something possessed by its pricing department.

### **A Six-Question AIPAF Review**

I will conclude this post with a brief but (in my view) highly useful mini-audit. The purpose of this six-question survey is to expose assumptions that many AI executives and managers might have about how the different functions in their organization work together. If the six answers come from different functions and have never been reconciled, the company has an AI pricing capability challenge on its hands. Before introducing or renewing an AI pricing model, the leadership team should be able to answer six questions:

**1) Customer value:** What incremental benefit is the customer actually realizing, compared with what credible alternative and baseline?

**2) Costs:** Which customers and workflows generate the costs, and how do these costs vary with customer behaviors?

**3) Metrics:** Why should the pricing unit move with customer value and costs, and under what conditions will that relationship stop working?

**4) Risks:** Which party in the transaction controls and can verify the conditions determining performance, usage, and outcomes?

**5) Usage:** What customer behaviors will the pricing model encourage, discourage, or reward, intentionally or unintentionally?

**6) Realized prices:** How much of the quoted or list price is earned after all the incentives are taken into account?

Much of the recent debate on AI pricing models has been conducted as though there were a correct pricing model waiting to be found. As the industry experience with pricing AI products accumulates, it’s becoming clear that there’s no single answer. Every metric has certain strengths, providing visibility into one part of the value-creation process while obscuring others, and the balance keeps shifting as products evolve and customer sophistication increases. The main lesson is that any AI pricing model has a short shelf life in the current dynamic AI pricing environment. The AI vendors pricing most effectively will be those that can measure the relationships among the AIPAF constructs, evaluate them rigorously, monitor the performance of their pricing model, and make adjustments quickly before a small misalignment turns into a serious economic or customer problem.
