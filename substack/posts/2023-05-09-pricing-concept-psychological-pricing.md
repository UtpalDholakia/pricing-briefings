*At the core of behavioral pricing research is the contention that price can be considered a physical stimulus, much as light and sound.* ― Lillian Cheng and Kent Monroe[^1].

Many of the posts on the Pricing Conundrum pay a lot of attention to consumer response to prices and the underlying psychology behind it. Supporting this emphasis is a point-of-view and a vocabulary that considers and seeks to understand the role of consumer psychology in pricing activities in detail. In this post, I want to give you a solid understanding of the psychological pricing perspective by defining it and considering its foundational aspects.

When managers started applying pricing theory to business decisions systematically, well before the popularity of social psychology and behavioral economics-influenced thinking, they routinely used a unidimensional (and some might argue, ethically questionable) consumer model on which to base their pricing strategies.

Essentially, they saw consumers as powerless, ill-informed, and myopic creatures, ripe for manipulation and money extraction. The goal of pricing during this early era (mainly 1950s and 1960s, but vestiges to this day) was to hoodwink customers and to earn the highest possible profit from each and every transaction, customer satisfaction and welfare be damned. Under the dominant microeconomic paradigm of the time, revenue and profit maximization for every transaction was worked out to be a shrewd and legitimate way to pursue business success.

Unfortunately, however, because it was so divorced from reality and considerations of sustainability, this naïve consumer model did not account for all the adverse effects, particularly economic ones, that occur when a business poisons its well by extorting from and alienating its core customer base. Needless to say, this pricing approach led to questionable pricing decisions and adverse financial consequences for many companies.

The model of the consumer we adopt here on [the Pricing Conundrum](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/) could not be more different. We see our customers as empowered human beings whose decisions and behaviors have a basis in volition and self-determination, even when they are habitual or made with little effort. Our customers are governed by their thoughts, motivations, self-identity constructions, routines, and emotions. Their value assessments and decision processes are dynamic, context-dependent, and changeable. Our customers are both susceptible to social influence, and they desire to influence others. They put the requisite amount of thinking and due diligence into their purchase decisions, but they are constrained in different ways. With everything else going on in their lives, they have limited time, cognitive resources, and money to buy and consume our products. The model of the consumer in psychological pricing practice

The way consumers respond to the price is equally complex. On the one hand, price is a physical stimulus subject to psychophysical laws. Consumers are hardwired to follow precisely specified rules in how they compare prices, tell them apart, and identify that a price has changed. On the other hand, more often than not, prices also produce visceral emotional responses. Shoppers [feel pain](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/cfp2.1010?casa_token=4vXYfkAHTrYAAAAA:O6Z1jaWtQcYMy69CDMcnArVfl5YIjZtx3eUEV8KUTTqEyAcJjqjptAz_apHhKIFhNB8BxytxcGej9jeNVw) when they have to spend money to buy something and [happiness when they score a deal](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0148296303001450?casa_token=9xd1XmrVBcoAAAAA:XnCZvE8AqwCHXnXZnveXOG5MCDYazBmX-PYNnBntU7gFP4i8jvYkON9ygDYlQQg-OlvYhEypHQE1) or buy something particularly meaningful. In one study, consumers whose brains were scanned while they saw very high prices showed increased activation in the insula region that is associated with pain[^2].

In conjunction with this complex, rich, and ecologically valid consumer model, the way we view pricing strategy is that, as managers, we want to sell products and services at prices that will deliver value to consumers. Instead of tricking or manipulating them, we want to listen to them, accommodate their voices in our pricing decisions, and share the value generated by our offerings with them equitably. Our fundamental goal is to initiate and maintain mutually beneficial, long-lasting relationships with customers where the value created by our offerings is maximized and shared. With these consumer and pricing strategy models as our guiding framework, we can define psychological pricing as follows:

***Psychological pricing is a set of strategic and tactical managerial pricing actions designed to influence consumers’ perceptions, decisions, and behaviors through thinking and feeling processes. Its goal is to deliver a high degree of value to target consumers while concurrently generating healthy revenue and profit for the business.***

Defined in this way, psychological pricing requires a principled approach to creating and sharing value with customers, along with a solid understanding of social psychology and behavioral economics principles, particularly as they pertain to how consumers perceive, evaluate, identify with, and share information about prices. Armed with an understanding of the different psychological roles and functions of price, the manager will be able to thoughtfully and deliberately develop pricing actions to influence customers in positive ways.

The true value of psychological pricing actions lies in the fact that many (but not all) pricing actions can be performed with relatively minuscule or no expense yet generate significant measurable effects on customer response that, in turn, impact the company’s revenue and profit. Effective psychological pricing methods possess the following five properties:

#### **1.** **Psychological pricing is goal-oriented.**

A psychological pricing action is designed to achieve one or more specific business goals that the manager defines beforehand. Goals can be to support a certain image for your product or brand, to make your offer appear to be an attractive deal to your customers, and to influence the customer’s economic valuation of the product or service. For example, upscale restaurants deliberately exploit the [symbolic information conveyed by prices](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/1938965511421168?casa_token=eH4HEbtXAWsAAAAA%3A1E7GpgygNZipi5oBVPxWr7KiDUPuvw_UMirRnr5kIlgUg7VmTghwUc8HkIwUEFB1cpKAAhuF7STEKDM) to create an aura of luxury by using even or non-standard numbers, writing out prices, and using particular fonts in their menus.

#### **2.** **Psychological pricing is rigorous.**

Despite the negative news about potential academic dodginess [by high-profile behavioral economists](https://www.npr.org/2023/07/27/1190568472/dan-ariely-francesca-gino-harvard-dishonesty-fabricated-data) and the unsettled nature of the [decade-old replication crisis](https://www.vox.com/future-perfect/21504366/science-replication-crisis-peer-review-statistics) in social psychology, there is a [vast amount of robust, well-replicated reliable research](https://www.experimental-history.com/p/im-so-sorry-for-psychologys-loss?) that provides actionable ideas for psychological pricing activities. This research spans a century, continents, and theoretical traditions, and is based on well-accepted psychological theories and empirical findings using the scientific method.

It is this body of work that provides us with the foundation to design and execute psychological pricing actions. Furthermore, by its very nature, the practice of psychological pricing allows [managers to adopt an experimental mindset](https://hbr.org/2011/03/a-step-by-step-guide-to-smart-business-experiments) and constantly test their application of ideas and effects recommended by psychological pricing.

#### **3. Psychological pricing is synergistic with the** ***Value Pricing Framework*****.**

To use psychological pricing tools and insights successfully requires that the business have a well-crafted and effective marketing strategy. It also requires the manager to have a grasp of the [four pillars of pricing](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/pricing-tool-the-value-pricing-framework). If you do not know your costs and your competitors’ prices, have a sense of which product features and benefits provide the highest value to your customers, and if you don’t have a compelling value proposition, psychological pricing will not take you very far. Rather than choosing and employing these ideas on an ad hoc basis, psychological pricing methods should be used to capitalize on and support your company’s marketing strategy, in conjunction with the *[Value Pricing Framework](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/pricing-tool-the-value-pricing-framework)*.

#### **4. Psychological pricing is measurable.**

For marketing managers, one of the greatest strengths of a pricing decision is that its effects on key performance indicators can be measured reliably. When prices are changed, for instance, the impact on sales can be pinpointed. This is also the case with other psychological pricing activities beyond price changes. Psychological pricing produces outcomes that can be measured with little ambiguity using methods such as controlled experimentation. Their desired or threshold levels can also be defined in advance. Examples of relevant outcomes of pricing activities include the change in sales, the shift in the customer’s brand recall or brand preferences, effects on revenue and profit margin, the change in the level of satisfaction or repurchase intent, the repurchase rate, and so on.

#### **5. Psychological pricing is impactful.**

Finally, psychological pricing produces significant outcomes. In other posts of case studies and concepts, I have (and will) describe many of the outcomes that occur from judicious and creative psychological pricing interventions. The American fast food chain [Subway serendipitously discovered](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/how-price-points-can-trick-shoppers) the \$5 price point for a fast food meal and exploited it for close to two decades with its “\$5 footlong” sandwich promotions to become one of the largest fast food chains in the world. 99 Cents Only Stores [built a multi-billion business](https://www.latimes.com/local/obituaries/la-me-dave-gold-20130427-story.html) by embracing the 99 price ending while De Beers turned [buying an expensive diamond engagement ring](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/how-smart-pricing-can-influence-how) into a completely normal ritualistic purchase by providing consumers with an acceptable price threshold of “two months’ salary.” Psychological pricing actions are levered. Many of them require relatively small investments and produce disproportionately large returns to the business.
