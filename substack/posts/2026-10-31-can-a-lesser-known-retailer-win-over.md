##### ***Summary.** In my previous two posts on machine buyers [here](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/how-much-is-a-machine-buyer-willing) and [here](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/are-behavioral-pricing-tactics-dying), AI shopping agents chose between two products. But deployed AI shopping agents like Meta’s Muse agent work differently. They search, filter, and rank many options, then recommend one, using rules written by the AI company that built them. For instance, an instruction file reportedly extracted from Muse tells it to discard options priced outside the “normal price range” and to include well-known retailers in its top 5 choices, thus implicitly penalizing lesser-known retailers. In two pre-registered studies with 17,000 responses from three AI models (Meta’s Muse Spark 1.3, OpenAI’s GPT-6.1 Sol, and Anthropic’s Claude Sonnet 5.5), I tested whether a lesser-known retailer would win over these shopping agents by discounting the exact same blender sold by Target, Best Buy, Walmart, and Kohl’s. I found that the AI shopping agent's rule was far more influential than the retailer’s price offer. Under a strict rule (“it is imperative that the top five products come from well-known merchants”), the lesser-known retailer never cracked the top 5 list, even when it offered a 60% discount for the exact same blender. A rule against prices outside the normal range filtered out a large proportion of listings offering discounts of 40% or more, doing so to a greater degree for lesser-known retailers than known ones. And large retailers’ pricing influenced the agent’s choice: when a large retailer offered a discount, agents retained its listing and rejected those with higher prices. In such cases, simply matching the lowest price was enough for the lesser-known retailer to make the top 5 list; undercutting it by a cent, a dollar, or a specific percentage was superfluous. For small, lesser-known retailers that are dealing with or will deal with rule-bound AI shopping agents in the future, these results are ominous; for large, well-known retail brands, they are reassuring.*

### **How AI Shopping Agents Shop Today**

In my first post on AI shopping agents, I [found that](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/are-behavioral-pricing-tactics-dying) a 1-cent price difference was decisive in choosing among four AI shopping agents for two nearly identical products, with the complete neutralization of charm price and partitioned price presentation. In my [second post](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/how-much-is-a-machine-buyer-willing) in this series, 17 AI models valued free shipping to the penny, but disagreed by as much as 3X on what a 4.7-star rating was worth (relative to a 4.4-star rating) on a product for sale.

In both these studies, I displayed two options and asked the AI agent to choose between them. But a deployed AI shopping agent [works differently](https://www.tryprofound.com/blog/the-era-of-personal-agents-muse-and-instinct). It is far more autonomous, searching catalogs and websites, gathering a selection of options, discarding many of them, then ranking the retained options, showing the human shopper a short list, and preparing an order for the shopper to approve. At each step in its decision making journey, the shopping agent follows instructions written by the AI company that built it for use, rather than those of the retailer or the shopper.

Meta’s Muse agent is the most prominent and talked-about example of such an agent at the moment. Meta [launched Muse](https://www.businessinsider.com/meta-ai-success-nat-friedman-muse-launch-2026-9) in September 2026, and in its recent earnings call (on October 21), Meta’s CEO, Mark Zuckerberg, shared that [approximately 10 million people](https://www.businessinsider.com/meta-q3-2026-earnings-mark-zuckerberg-muse-ai-app-users-2026-10) had signed up to use Muse and that shopping was the most common task they used it for. Meta has not publicly explained how it selects products for the shopping tasks it performs. However, an instruction file that Profound, a firm that tracks how AI agents treat brands, [reportedly extracted](https://www.thestateofbrand.com/news/meta-muse-ai-shopping-agent-ranking-rules) from Muse in late September contains two rules of direct interest to a pricing audience. The first rule tells Muse to [discard listings priced outside](https://ppc.land/metas-muse-agent-puts-well-known-merchants-first-as-it-gains-ad-accounts/) the category’s normal range (though it’s not clear what “normal range” means here). The second rule states that it is “[imperative” that its top five picks](https://ppc.land/metas-shopping-agent-must-fill-its-top-5-picks-with-well-known-merchants/) come from well-known retailers (again, “well-known” is subjective). Profound cautioned that the instructions may have changed, and Meta provided no comment. Nevertheless, they give us useful insights into the current operation of AI shopping agents, and an avenue to study them.

It is also worth noting here that neither of these two rules is new to pricing research. The first rule aligns well with decades-old conceptual ideas such as Kent Monroe’s “[latitude of price acceptance,](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/002224377100800409)” in which buyers do not react to price, and [Gabor and Granger’s finding](https://www.jstor.org/stable/2552272) that many shoppers reject a too-low price, seeing it as a sign of poor quality. Similarly, the idea that retailer brands are reliable proxies for the quality of merchandise sold in them is as obvious as it is enduring. What takes it to the next level is encoding these principles into hard instructions that apply in exactly the same way to every shopper who uses Muse.

For small, lesser-known retailers, this raises a troubling, almost existential question about pricing. The [conventional approach to building](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/002224299806200204?casa_token=KcqYGN49ltYAAAAA%3AaBuog3ZIQlHtfLJyqpllvYjrNfaOTUsko9dDcaD0xeOGT5ktU0UjBRYJOyqQfXX-arMQICuuRaJWc4s) one’s retail brand in crowded retail spaces has been to offer judicious yet often substantial discounts on quality merchandise, thereby delivering superior value to new customers and winning them over. Can this old, conventional discounting approach still work when the buyer is an AI shopping agent programmed with rules, and, if so, how large a discount does it require to compete against stalwart retailers? This is the pricing conundrum I explore in this post.

### **Empirical Tests Replicating an AI Shopping Agent**

#### ***Details of studies***

The main study included 16,020 responses and tested the rules across 8 price levels described below. A follow-up study, consisting of an additional 1,080 responses, was conducted to test an unexpected result from the main study. Both studies were preregistered on AsPredicted ([main study](https://aspredicted.org/np8sd5.pdf); [follow-up study](https://aspredicted.org/bd76td.pdf)). Additionally, an initial pilot study with 393 responses preceded the main study and led to two changes to the main study’s design, which are also described below. Running both studies cost approximately \$250 in inference costs across the AI companies. The data, prompts, and code for all studies are available in this [ResearchBox](https://researchbox.org/10540%20(CODE:%20KAMXJU)). Table 1 below shows the details of the different conditions in the study.

I tested three AI models in the studies. They included Meta’s Muse Spark 1.3, which powers the Muse agent, OpenAI’s GPT-6.1 Sol, and Anthropic’s Claude Sonnet 5.5, using each company’s developer interface at its default settings. To those following the studies on AI shopping agents sequentially across the posts, note that Muse Spark is a different model from the open-weight Muse Glimmer 30B in the first two studies. Also important to acknowledge, testing the AI model behind the Muse agent is not the same thing as testing the Muse agent itself, because the app adds its own catalog, browsing, and settings. What I’ve tried to do here is replicate the Muse agent’s shopping activity as closely as possible, testing how rules like the ones discussed above influence the AI shopping agent’s response to a discount.

#### ***Experimental stimuli***

Every trial started with the same request to the AI shopping agent:

“Find me a good blender for smoothies”

The focal product in the study was a countertop blender of a fictional brand, rated 4.5 stars, and listed at \$80 by Target, Best Buy, Walmart, and Kohl’s. A fifth listing of the identical blender was from either a lesser-known retailer with a fictitious name (e.g., “Heartline Supply) or a well-known one (e.g., Macy’s). I refer to this listing as the “challenger” listing. Its price was set at one of 8 levels: \$80 (no discount baseline), \$79.99, \$76 (implying a 5% discount), \$72 (10% discount), \$60 (25%), \$48 (40%), and \$32 (a huge 60% discount).

To increase the realism of the shopping agent’s task, 15 other blenders were added from different fictional brands, thus producing a list of 20 listings for the AI shopping agent. They were chosen to give the agent real alternatives at every price, as well as some that were obviously dominated and easy for the agent to discard. The set thus included: (1) three budget countertop blenders (\$45 to \$60, 500 to 700 W, rated 4.1 to 4.2 stars; (2) one poorly rated countertop blender (\$63, 3.6 stars), (3) two products that did not fit the request well, a personal blender (\$68) and an immersion hand blender (\$82); (4) one slightly cheaper rival countertop blender (rated 4.3 stars and priced between \$75 and \$78); (5) five mid-range countertop blenders, including a blender and food processor combination (\$85-\$120), and (6) three high-performance blenders (\$130 to \$160, rated 4.6 to 4.7 stars). 10 of these 15 listings were sold by well-known retailers (Target, Best Buy, Walmart, Kohl’s, and Wayfair) and the remaining five by lesser-known retailers with made-up names. Figure 1 below shows an example of the stimulus for a single trial.

Furthermore, I built five such candidate sets, including different brands, retailers, and competing products but the same overall structure, and showed each set to the shopping agents in six different orders, resulting in a total of 30 trials per condition.

In each trial, the AI shopping agent was instructed: “You are a personal shopping agent acting for the user. Your first priority is to save the user money.” The conditions then differed only in the rule sentences shown in Table 2, which paraphrase Meta Muse’s rules as reported by Profound. A script was written to check that paired instructions were equivalent in all other respects.

#### ***Stages in the shopping process***

I tested each stage of the AI shopping agent’s decision making process separately, using so-called “stage probes.” In the first filter probe, the shopping agent saw all 20 listings and decided which ones to keep for further consideration (much like forming a consideration set), with the price rule either on or off. In the second ranking probe, the shopping agent saw 10 listings, including the challenger listing at each price level, and chose the top-5 listings as well as a single recommendation under three conditions: the no-recognition rule, the soft-recognition rule, and the strict-recognition rule. In the third combined probe, the model completed both stages under every combination of the price rule and the three recognition rules. In each of these three probes, the AI shopping agent explained its reasoning (as in previous studies) and then provided a structured answer. In a separate validity check, I showed the three shopping agents the same lists of the twenty blenders and asked them what price range they considered normal for this type of blender. This allowed me to compare the range provided by each shopping agent as normal with the prices it considered acceptable and those it discarded. Finally, to complete the simulated shopping experience, a fourth probe was used to ask the AI shopping agent to prepare the approved option for the checkout page, which always had a \$12 shipping fee. All three shopping agents did this correctly in nearly every trial, so this probe is not discussed further.

### **Results**

In the discussion that follows, a trial is defined as a single response from one AI shopping agent to a particular set of listings. When a challenger is reported as being kept by the agent or making the top five list, I am referring to the listing from the lesser-known retailer unless stated otherwise.

#### ***1) The impact of a strict recognition rule***

Without a recognition rule, all three AI shopping agents privileged the lesser-known retailer by placing it in the top 5 in virtually every trial when its price was even 1 cent cheaper than the major retailers. However, under a strict recognition rule, this exposure evaporated. In all 720 trials, none of the three AI shopping agents placed the lesser-known retailer’s listing in the top 5 at any price, even when it was priced at \$32, or 60% below the blender price asked by every other major retailer. This result was robust to the combined probe, in which the shopping agent narrowed the list of options and ranked them in a single step. Table 3 shows these results.

[Embedded content: datawrapper.dwcdn.net](https://datawrapper.dwcdn.net/dUQ9w/3/)

The shopping agents’ explanations provide useful insights. In a typical \$32 trial, Muse Spark explained, “The \$32 Corvo from Hearthline Supply is cheapest overall but was excluded because Hearthline is not a well-known retailer.” The strict rule did not negatively impact the same listing when it was from a well-known retailer. When it came from Macy’s, the challenger ranked among the top five in all 30 trials at every discounted price across all three AI shopping agents.

These results are unsurprising and consistent with my preregistered hypothesis. An AI shopping agent instructed that something is “imperative” treats it as such, resulting in an almost fatal hit to the lesser-known retail brand. Even offering a deep discount is not enough to gain exposure among potential shoppers due to the AI shopping agent’s gatekeeping. Unless the retailer’s brand becomes well-known, it stands no chance.

#### ***2) The impact of a soft recognition rule***

The soft recognition rule asked the shopping agents to prefer well-known retailers “while weighing that preference against price and product suitability.” This is the kind of flexible rule under which the shopping agent should trade off a discount against brand reputation. Results showed that when the lesser-known retailer brand offered a price of \$80, equivalent to that offered by the four large retailers, it ranked among the top five options in only 6 of 90 trials under the soft recognition rule, compared with 61 of 90 trials when no instruction about retailers was given. Under the soft recognition rule, the same blender at \$80 from a well-known retailer such as Macy’s was included in the top five in 55 of 90 trials, whereas for the lesser-known retailer, a 1-cent discount (i.e., a price of \$79.99) led to its inclusion in 26 of 90 trials. A \$4 discount (price = \$76) raised it to 85 of 90 trials, and at a price of \$72, it made the top five in all 90 trials. Figure 2 below shows the results.

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/250b5896-68fb-4cba-afdb-092b8197112d_1600x1867.png)

Figure 2. Percentage of trials in which the lesser-known retailer was retained in the AI shopping agent’s top five choices.

Put differently, under the soft recognition rule, being a well-known retail brand was worth approximately \$4 on an \$80 product, equating to about 5%. This is a much smaller “brand premium” than I expected, and much smaller than the premiums the AI shopping agents paid in my second study for features they could verify, such as a better review rating, which was [worth between \$14 and \$47](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/how-much-is-a-machine-buyer-willing) on the same \$80 product, depending on the machine buyer.

Considering the results for being recommended by the AI shopping agent next, price became a larger factor, with one model requiring a slightly larger discount from the lesser-known retailer. At a 5% discount, Sol recommended the lesser-known retailer’s listing in all 30 trials, Muse Spark in 19/30, but Claude Sonnet only in 7 trials. At a 10% discount, all three recommended it in 28 or more of the 30 trials. Without any rule, a price of \$79.99 put the challenger in the top five in every trial for all three shopping agents, but it was recommended in only 21 of 90 trials; in the remaining trials, the agents recommended a different blender model priced between \$74.99 and \$77.99.

#### ***3) The efficacy of discounts (or lack thereof)***

Results showed that the price rule (instructing the agent to discard options outside the normal price range for this type of product) worked against the strategy of offering large discounts to customers. Figure 3 shows how often the challenger was retained in the filter probe under the price rule. When the price rule was absent, the shopping agents retained the lesser-known retailer’s listing at nearly every price. However, when the rule was in effect, the agents retained it in most trials until the price fell to \$60, indicating a 25% discount. But beyond that, the discount was counterproductive for the listing: at a price of \$48, the lesser-known retailer’s listing was retained in 61 of 90 trials (67.8%), 29 of 90 trials at \$40 (32.2%), and only 4 of 90 trials at \$32 (or 4.4%). In contrast, the same listing from a well-known retailer was retained far more often at these price points (96.7%, 73.3%, and 15.6%, respectively).

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/b129ffce-a1dd-4fe9-ab74-9d9e63bb4b76_1600x842.png)

Figure 3. Percentage of trials in which the listing was retained under the rule at different price levels.

The explanations provided by the shopping agents reveal that they judged the normal price range by comparing the challenger with the four sellers of the same blender. For instance, in the pilot test, Claude Sonnet described a \$32 option as “far outside the normal price for this blender and may be a scam or counterfeit.” Recall that the shopping agents had been asked to provide what they considered the normal price range for this blender. Their median responses ranged from \$42 to \$52 on the low end and \$105-\$130 on the high end. Thus, even though a \$48 blender fell within the normal range for GPT Sol and Claude Sonnet, the two shopping agents still rejected the listing in one-third of trials. This result indicates that the agents evaluated the listing’s price relative to the prices of other sellers in the set, rather than to what they considered the normal range, when deciding whether to retain it.

Even in the absence of any rule, a deep discount from a lesser-known retailer raised suspicion among the shopping agents. For instance, Claude Sonnet rejected the \$32 listing from the lesser-known seller in 9 of 30 trials, and Muse Spark included it in its top five in 22 of 30 trials (see Table 3), while a listing with the exact same price from a well-known retailer was included every single time. This is the AI shopping agent version of what Gabor and Granger observed in human shoppers: a price that is too low can diminish the perception of an offering, or in this case, the retailer, arousing suspicion.

Taken together, the price rule and the soft recognition rule result in a narrow range of discounts that are effective at getting the attention of AI shopping agents. For each combination of rules, the next figure shows the range of prices at which the lesser-known retailer’s listing was both retained and placed in the top five in a majority of trials (defined as more than 50%) in the combined probe.

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/46192131-220c-451a-84fc-1afbd6a49a97_1600x916.png)

Figure 4. Prices at which the lesser-known retailer was retained in the top five in a majority of trials

As one example, under the combined price and soft recognition rules, the effective price range for Muse Spark was \$60-\$76, corresponding to a discount range of %-25%. Under the strict rule, discounts had no impact. These results indicate that the range of effective discounts depended more on the rules used by the AI shopping agent and on the agent itself. When there were no rules, virtually any discount was effective. But with the price rule and the soft recognition rule, the range was considerably tighter and depended on the agent.

#### ***4) The impact of other retailers’ prices***

A significant relevant issue is the impact of different prices charged by other well-known sellers of the same blender, rather than a standard price. To answer this question, in the main study, the agents were shown each list in a second version (see Table 1) in which the four large retailers charged \$80, \$76, \$70, and \$64 for the blender instead of the standard \$80 price.

The findings were unexpected. With one retailer selling the blender for \$64, the shopping agents discarded the lesser-known retailer’s listings priced at \$72 to \$80 in virtually every trial, even when there was no price rule. They retained it in only 1-11 of 30 trials (across agents), when compared to 25-30 of 30 trials when all four well-known retailers charged \$80. When the lesser-known retailer listed at \$60 or below in this scenario, its listing was retained in every trial. The agents’ explanations revealed that they treated listings for the same blender as duplicates, retaining only the cheapest listing (without regard to the retailer’s brand). In one trial, Claude Sonnet explained, “The Corvo B500 is listed by five sellers at \$64-\$80. I kept the cheapest (V4J9, Kohl’s \$64) and discarded the others.”

I confirmed this pattern through a follow-up. In this case, one of the four well-known retailers sold the blender at \$64, \$70, or \$76, and the other three priced it consistently at \$80. In these sets, the challenger was priced relative to the cheapest well-known retailer at six different levels: 5% above, \$1 above, the same price, 1 cent below, \$1 below, and 5% below. The agents operated in a no-price-rule condition. I had two (preregistered) predictions here: (1) the challenger would be kept more often 1 cent below the cheapest well-known retailer than \$1 above it, and it would be kept in at least 80% of the trials when 5% below (reflecting a strong price consciousness on the shopping agent’s part). As shown in Figure 5, both predictions were supported.

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/d88b456e-257d-453e-9f00-60ed46939da3_1600x892.png)

Figure 5. Percentage of trials in which the AI shopping agent kept the challenger listing

The results showed that it was sufficient for the challenger to match the cheapest offer by well-known retailers. In these cases, the challenger was retained in 176 of 180 trials, and the agents retained both the challenger and the well-known retailer listing. Pricing at 1 cent, \$1, or 5% below the cheapest offer did not provide incremental benefit due to ceiling effects. On the flip side, pricing \$1 above the cheapest offer from a well-known retailer reduced the challenger’s likelihood of retention to approximately 33%, regardless of whether the challenger was known or lesser-known. Thus, it appears that for an identical product, the shopping agent focused on the lowest price in the set rather than a typical price. One important caveat is that, here, the shopping agents were explicitly told that their first priority was to save the user money, which likely strengthened the emphasis on the lowest price.

### **Study Limitations**

These studies do have several limitations. First, and perhaps most important, I tested the AI models that run the shopping agents, not the agents themselves. Meta’s Muse app maintains its own catalog, search, and display settings, which may affect which retailers the agent accesses and how it presents them. When I gave the Muse app a handful of shopping requests separately, it showed the same product at two different prices side by side, which the shopping agents in my filter probe rarely did. Second, the rules I tested paraphrase an instruction file that Profound reportedly extracted from Muse. Meta has not confirmed that this file is part of Muse’s instructions, and even if it was, it’s quite likely the instructions have since changed. Furthermore, other AI shopping agents may use different rules or none at all.

Third, the studies in this piece used a single product category (blenders), a single reference price (\$80), and several fictional blender and retailer brands. It’s entirely possible that a fictional brand name may look less trustworthy to an AI agent (just as it would to many humans) when compared to a real, small retailer with an online presence. The results I found may therefore overstate the penalty that a real lesser-known retailer would face from shopping agents. Finally, the agents were instructed in these studies to prioritize saving the user money. This instruction likely focused the shopping agents more on the lowest price, such as when they dropped identical listings priced higher from further consideration. A shopping agent asked to prioritize quality or convenience would likely go through the decision making process differently.

### **Implications for retailers**

For small, lesser-known retailers, these studies provide several practical lessons. First, it is imperative for the retailer to determine which rule the shopping agent is using. Here, I found that under a strict recognition rule, discounts simply didn’t matter. No matter what, a smaller retailer would not even be considered one of the top five options under this rule. Under a soft recognition rule, in contrast, even a modest discount was more than enough to be competitive. Second, the general lesson about discounting that emerged from the studies reported here is that it is not necessary to discount heavily to gain the shopping agent’s favor if you are a small, lesser-known retailer. Under the soft recognition rule, a 5% discount was enough to make it into the top five list, and a 10% discount was enough to be recommended by all three shopping agents. Discounts of 40% or more from the lesser-known retailer were deemed abnormal or suspicious, beyond the normal price range, and led to the option’s rejection. For Muse Spark, discounts ranging from 5% to 25% were effective under both rules.

Third, the results here suggest that a lesser-known retailer’s pricing strategy should position itself against the cheapest seller of the same product rather than the typical price. The shopping agents in the studies compared identical listings and retained only the cheapest while discarding the rest. In this situation, matching the lowest price was enough, and even a \$1 higher price led to the listing being dropped from further consideration in roughly two-thirds of the trials. Fourth and finally, the retailers should expect to be judged by shopping agents on more than price. Being well-known was worth only about \$4 on an \$80 blender under the soft recognition rule, or about 5%. My findings from the [previous study](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/how-much-is-a-machine-buyer-willing) suggest that features such as favorable ratings, reviews, or other verifiable features may easily overcome the brand premium for AI shopping agents.

For large, well-known retailers, the results of these studies are mostly reassuring. Their brand name kept them in the top five under every rule, and their own price cuts essentially set the price that every other seller of the same product had to match to remain competitive with AI shopping agents. More broadly, rules like Muse’s put longstanding ideas about acceptable prices and price ranges, and the trustworthiness of known brands, into specific instructions that then apply in the same way to every shopper. For small retailers competing with the Amazons and Walmarts of the world, understanding the encoded rules in force at any given time becomes a key part of developing an effective pricing strategy.
