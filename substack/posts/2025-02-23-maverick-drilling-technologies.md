### **A New Challenge**

The weekend was approaching, and Houston’s energy professionals were wrapping up another work week and heading out to happy hours around the city. But Nathan Parker wasn’t ready to unwind. A petroleum engineer by training and now a product manager, Nathan was preoccupied with a pricing dilemma. He was tasked with pricing Maverick Drilling Technologies’ ambitious new offering, the NaviRig + PADS directional drilling system. NaviRig was Maverick’s new directional drilling equipment platform designed for horizontal wells, and PADS (Precision Automated Drilling System) was the proprietary AI-powered software that optimized drilling operations by reducing non-productive time and improving wellbore placement accuracy.

As a new product manager at Maverick Drilling Technologies, Nathan was both anxious and excited to have this opportunity. He had worked as a software developer at Baker Hughes for three years after earning his petroleum engineering degree. Then, he returned to graduate school and completed his MBA from Rice University earlier that year. With the energy sector recovering from the pandemic downturn, recruiting was easy, and Nathan had accepted Maverick’s product manager job offer six months earlier, in April 2022.

### **The Strategy Session**

The mahogany conference table in Maverick's executive boardroom reflected the early morning sunlight as four key players gathered for what everyone knew would be a pivotal meeting. Leading the discussion was Samuel Davis, Maverick's drilling equipment division head and Nathan's direct supervisor. Seated across from Nathan was Diana Wilson, the company's Chief Scientific Officer, whose team had spent two years perfecting the PADS software. The third attendee was Wei Chen, Maverick's marketing director, whose market research had identified the independent operator opportunity that NaviRig was designed to capture. Notably absent was Jack Reynolds, Maverick's Sales Director, who was traveling to meet with potential customers. His absence created an interesting dynamic—the pricing decision would ultimately need his team's buy-in for successful execution.

Davis opened the meeting with his characteristic directness. "NaviRig was developed to target the growing independent operator segment that's been driving the shale revolution in the Permian and Eagle Ford," he began. "The key to NaviRig's success will hinge on how effectively we can bundle the PADS software with the NaviRig system and sell the advantage to our target customers." The Precision Automated Drilling System leveraged machine learning algorithms and real-time data analysis to significantly reduce drilling time and improve accuracy for directional drilling operations. Wilson's team had developed it especially for navigating complex geological formations while maintaining an optimal rate of penetration. Running PADS could make the NaviRig system up to four times more efficient, something that many independent operators would value immensely because of their capital constraints and need to maximize return on investment per well. Davis continued, "Make no mistake, we want to aggressively and confidently push NaviRig + PADS into the market." Nathan wondered if this was a challenge or encouragement from his boss.

Wei Chen presented key characteristics of the directional drilling equipment industry and shared his team's uptake forecasts for the NaviRig system (see Exhibit 1). "As you can see, the NaviRig system targets what we're calling our 'standard rig' segment. This segment is small but growing quickly, consisting mostly of independent oil and gas operators who typically drill 5-15 wells per year. Our forecast shows the standard rig segment will have about 550 systems in 2023 and grow with a 43% CAGR through 2025—almost double the growth rate of the major operator segment."

Davis jumped in assertively: "Nathan, as you can see from these numbers, you have a massive opportunity here. Our NaviRig system, equipped with PADS, is our lead into a brand-new segment where we haven't really paid much attention. TEI has dominated the standard rig segment with its DrillPro system from the beginning. But with PADS, we now have a solid competitive advantage. Correctly pricing the NaviRig + PADS will be crucial to leverage this advantage. Consider not only how our potential customers will respond but also how our competitors, particularly TEI, may react. We need to be careful in positioning ourselves in a market where relationships and reputation matter tremendously."

Nathan was tasked with providing his pricing recommendation in about ten days. ENERGEXPO was a month away in Houston. It would be the perfect venue to introduce NaviRig + PADS to the independent operator community.

### **Understanding the Pricing Context**

Over the following days, Nathan compiled relevant information from internal company reports and various external databases to understand his pricing context. He remembered his business school professor’s words: “Nathan, unless you fully understand the contextual factors surrounding the pricing decision, you will miss the target.”

#### ***The company***

Maverick was currently the largest player in the directional drilling equipment industry, carved out as a distinct niche from the broader oilfield services industry. As the earliest dedicated directional drilling equipment maker, selling its first system in the Barnett Shale boom of the early 2010s, Maverick was now considered the premium brand by major operators like ExxonMobil and Chevron. The company held the highest market share in high-performance directional drilling, where its VertexDrill system was the established gold standard for challenging deepwater and ultra-extended reach wells.

#### ***The customers***

About a year earlier, Davis had pushed to extend Maverick's directional drilling line to serve the growing independent operator market. These customers were vastly different from large operators. Most were smaller regional businesses, running a handful of wells, with tighter capital constraints and higher sensitivity to upfront costs. Many were entrepreneurial operators who had broken away from larger companies during the industry downturn. About 40% viewed their initial directional drilling purchase as a stepping stone toward expanding their operations to dozens of wells across multiple basins.

Virtually every customer had buying centers with deep industry experience, committed to operational excellence through commodity price cycles. These customers were knowledgeable and technically minded, with many having worked at major operators before starting their own operations. They relied on industry publications, conferences, and networks for learning about new technologies. They would appreciate PADS's efficiency-boosting value immediately and likely disseminate their opinions through industry networks.

#### ***The competition***

TEI was a formidable competitor in the standard rig market. Founded seven years ago by three former Schlumberger engineers, it had quickly captured approximately 60% market share with its DrillPro system. In side-by-side technical comparisons, DrillPro matched the standalone NaviRig system (without PADS) in performance. TEI emphasized operational efficiency as its core differentiator. Its lean Texas-based manufacturing operation enabled it to proudly carry a "Made in America" label and maintain lower pricing than Maverick. This focus on cost control and agility gave TEI a strong appeal to independent operators. Other competitors were fragmented and small in scale, typically selling only a handful of systems every year. While they did not pose much of a strategic threat, Davis and Chen clearly saw TEI as their main competitor.

#### ***The PADS advantage***

It was clear to Nathan he needed to know the ins and outs of the two competing systems. After reading and digesting numerous technical reports, Nathan learned that depending on the type of formation being drilled, the PADS software boosted NaviRig’s performance by up to four times relative to the system’s baseline performance. This meant that the customer could achieve the same drilling capability by buying one NaviRig system loaded with PADS as by buying four DrillPro systems.

He also found out that PADS’s performance boost varied by rock formation where the drilling occurred. In shale formations, NaviRig equipped with PADS could perform up to four times better than the base system. In sandstone, the improvement was around twofold, and in carbonate dome formations, it was about 1.5 times. This meant that a single NaviRig + PADS system could, in some scenarios, do the work of multiple DrillPro systems. Based on industry research, most independents operated in shale and sandstone; less than 15% of the target segment drilled in carbonate formations.

Based on this assessment, Nathan was confident that his target customers would immediately recognize the economic value of NaviRig + PADS in terms of reduced drilling days, improved wellbore placement, and overall drilling economics. The difficult challenge was how to quote a price that factored in the customers’ consideration of short-term and long-term value. What pricing strategy would work best for a product that could potentially save independent operators millions in drilling costs over its lifetime?

#### ***Industry pricing conventions***

As part of its premium branding strategy, Davis’s drilling equipment division followed the pricing approach used by the oilfield services industry. They focused on designing and making the best directional drilling equipment, while the software loaded on it was seen as a secondary consideration or someone else’s problem. “Our customers pay for the quality of our drilling equipment, they do not expect to be nickel-and-dimed by us,” Davis had often said. What he meant was that the software installed on Maverick’s drilling systems would be offered to customers for free as an added value when buying the system. After all, the entire oilfield services industry had embraced this pricing approach for the past three-plus decades. Then, however, the software installed on drilling systems was relatively generic, much of it built on open-source platforms at relatively low cost. Nathan wondered if this approach made sense for something as powerful, costly, and advantageous as the PADS software, which had required significant R&D investment and offered measurable performance improvements.

#### ***Cost-based pricing***

Even though it sold cutting-edge technology, the directional drilling industry had adopted traditional cost-based pricing methods to set drilling equipment prices in the past. Both NaviRig & VertexDrill were priced using this approach. Davis had already established a round price point of \$2,000,000 for the NaviRig system before Nathan came on board.

According to research conducted by external consulting firms, Nathan learned that TEI had also consistently used cost-based pricing for its products. Exhibit 2 provides relevant information about costs and prices for the VertexDrill, NaviRig, and DrillPro systems sourced from consultants.

“There are some obvious weaknesses with using cost-based pricing here, aren’t there?” Nathan said to himself. “This seems like a perfect situation to use value-based pricing, especially when we can demonstrate visible savings in drilling days and improved wellbore accuracy, but how will Davis react? What will Wei Chen and Jack Reynolds think in an industry that’s been resistant to new pricing models?”

#### ***Customer benefits***

To Nathan, the benefits of bundling PADS with the NaviRig system were obvious after that first meeting. However, there was a major complicating factor to consider, as PADS’s benefits varied significantly depending on the type of formation being drilled. Internal performance tests suggested that those drilling in shale and sandstone formations saw the biggest jump in performance, while others did not (See Exhibit 3 for the results of these tests). Nathan recalled Wei Chen’s observation that “our research shows that most of the customers buying the NaviRig system will use it for drilling in a single formation type, or at most two formation types based on their acreage positions.”

Furthermore, Nathan realized that regardless of the pricing approach he recommended, potential customers would have to be educated about the direct and indirect savings they would enjoy from buying NaviRig + PADS: direct savings from the fact that they would have to buy fewer drilling systems to achieve the same results, and indirect savings from the reduced costs of operation that would occur over the course of using the directional drilling system with the enhanced software, including fewer drilling days, reduced non-productive time, better wellbore quality, and potentially higher production rates.

#### ***Sales execution***

Although not present at the strategy session, Sales Director Jack Reynolds remained deeply involved. He frequently checked in with Nathan, offering informal feedback and gauging early reactions from the field. Traditionally, Maverick had used a relatively high-touch consultative sales approach when selling drilling systems to legacy oil and gas companies. Reynolds’s sales team was competent and experienced by any industry measure. Before joining the oil and gas industry, many of them had a decade or more of experience selling industrial equipment for companies like GE and Siemens. Compensation was split roughly 65% base salary and 35% commission, meaning pricing would directly impact incentives.

Nathan's pricing approach needed internal buy-in. Sales reps would be responsible for communicating the value proposition, handling objections, and building credibility. At the upcoming ENERGEXPO trade show, Nathan would be the point person tasked with talking to prospects and demonstrating the NaviRig + PADS system in Maverick’s booth. What he learned there would help Reynolds develop sales scripts and help Wei Chen generate marketing collateral when approaching target customers. Thus, a consistent, well-rationalized price would be critical for ENERGEXPO and beyond.

### **Thinking Through the Pricing Decision**

Nathan developed a detailed customer persona called "Lone Star Energy Partners" with a comprehensive needs specification (Exhibit 3). For his pricing calculations, Nathan assumed that Lone Star Energy Partners would purchase four base drilling systems and would be interested in minimizing its initial purchase costs (which would be financed through a loan or reserve-based lending) and the overall operating costs of its drilling program. Thus, a relevant consideration for Lone Star Energy Partners would be the total direct and indirect savings obtained from purchasing a single NaviRig system (enhanced with PADS) versus buying four DrillPro systems. He also determined that, given the conservative nature of many of the senior managers involved in the decision making process, he needed to compare two NaviRig + PADS systems to four base systems.

Next, he made a list of four pricing approaches he would pursue in detail:

1.  ***The legacy pricing approach*****:** Use the same pricing approach as that used by Davis and his predecessors; charge only for the drilling equipment and give the PADS software away to customers, eating its costs. In this case, PADS would work as a brand builder and loyalty enhancer.

2.  ***Competition-based pricing approach*****:** Price the NaviRig + PADS system at the same price as the “equivalent” competitor price: what the customer would pay for four DrillPro systems.

3.  ***Cost-based pricing approach*****:** Apply a cost-based approach to pricing the NaviRig + PADS system by marking up the relevant costs. (Nathan’s assumptions for this approach are provided in Exhibit 4).

4.  ***Value-based pricing approach*****:** Charge a price based on the differentiated economic value of the NaviRig + PADS system to the target customer, factoring in both capital and operational savings.

Finally, he considered what he would need for his upcoming pricing strategy meeting with the executive team. First, he needed a crystal-clear set of prices derived from the four methods. Second, he needed to go a step further and provide a clear recommendation for a single price he thought would be the best price to charge for the NaviRig + PADS system, along with a rationale behind his recommendation. Third, he felt he should provide some ideas and specific guidance to the sales team to support executing his recommended price, including how to communicate the value proposition to technically minded customers who focus on drilling economics. Fourth, he needed to think through NaviRig + PADS’s launch pricing strategy, by considering whether to offer price promotions or use other ideas to ease the introduction to the marketplace. And finally, he needed to consider the competitive ramifications of his pricing recommendation. Specifically, how would TEI react to the clear and present danger represented by NaviRig + PADS’s launch? And could something be done at the outset to minimize their aggressive response in a relatively small but growing market where relationships often mattered more than the product features?

There was a lot of work to be done. Nathan knew that NaviRig + PADS’s success and his career trajectory within Maverick rested on what he came up with. He rolled up his sleeves and got to work, casting aside all thoughts regarding Houston’s famous Friday evening happy hours.

**Purchase Process and Dynamics**

1.  **Identification Phase:**

    - Jason Torres typically identifies operational needs and begins preliminary research

    - Alex Johnson authorizes the initial exploration of new technology solutions

    - Both attend trade shows like ENERGEXPO to evaluate new offerings

2.  **Evaluation Phase:**

    - Jason Torres conducts a technical evaluation and prepares an initial recommendation

    - Alex Johnson reviews and expands the evaluation to include operational considerations

    - Rachel Patel initiates the vendor qualification process

    - Cross-functional team, including field supervisors, may participate in demonstrations

3.  **Business Case Development:**

    - Alex Johnson and Jason Torres develop a technical case

    - Sarah Martinez builds a financial model and conducts an ROI analysis

    - Rachel Patel gathers competitive bids and contract options

4.  **Approval Process:**

    - Purchases under \$500,000: Alex Johnson has approval authority

    - Purchases \$500,000-\$2,000,000: Requires Sarah Martinez’s approval

    - Purchases over \$2,000,000: Requires Michael Wei and board notification

    - Current directional drilling system acquisition falls in the highest approval category

5.  **Typical Timeline:**

    - Initial research and evaluation: 2-3 months

    - Business case development: 1 month

    - Financial review and approval: 2-4 weeks

    - Contract negotiation and final purchasing: 3-4 weeks

    - Total process: 4-6 months from identification to purchase

**Buying Committee Dynamics**

- Alex Johnson and Jason Torres generally align on technical matters but can disagree on risk tolerance for new technologies

- Sarah Martinez tends to push back on capital expenditures without a clear payback within 18 months

- Michael Wei gives significant weight to vendor relationships and industry reputation

- Rachel Patel often raises concerns about vendor support capacity for newer companies

- Decision typically requires consensus from Alex, Sarah, and Michael, with Jason and Rachel having strong influence

### Exhibit 4. Assumptions for the Cost-Based Pricing Approach

Nathan assumed that Maverick would want to recover the development costs of PADS, \$6,000,000, over a period of three years. He also made a number of other assumptions to prepare his cost-based pricing model. They were as follows:

1.  The company would manufacture a limited number of standard directional drilling systems due to manufacturing capacity constraints and corporate strategy. It would be able to sell all the systems it could produce over the 2023-2025 time period. This assumption would need to be stress-tested for 2026.

2.  Maverick Drilling Technologies’ market share in the standard system category would be 4% in 2023, 9% in 2024, and 14% in 2025.

3.  PADS would be installed on half the NaviRig systems shipped. The remaining half would be shipped as-is, without PADS installed on them, for customers drilling in complex carbonate formations or using systems for conventional vertical drilling.

4.  Maverick would want to apply a 30% markup rate on its costs, consistent with its business strategy.
