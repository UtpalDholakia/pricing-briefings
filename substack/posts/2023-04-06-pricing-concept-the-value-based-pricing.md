During my two-plus decades of teaching and consulting work on pricing strategies, if I had to name one concept that is discussed, idolized, revered, seen as a magic bullet, reviled, abandoned, and misunderstood more than any other, it is **value-based pricing** (also commonly known as “value pricing”). The method creates more anticipation, confusion, and angst among managers, and even many pricing experts, than virtually any other pricing concept.

In my experience, widely-held misconceptions about exactly how the value-based pricing method works lead managers to first embrace, and then later, prematurely abandon the method for the wrong reasons. Then they settle for what they’ve always done which is either use some variant of cost-based pricing or another ad hoc method.

To clear up the confusion and misunderstanding, I will describe the value-based pricing method clearly and completely in this post. In a companion post, I will write in detail about the different misconceptions surrounding value-based pricing, dispel them, and set out its strengths and limitations in B2B contexts.

### **Defining Value-based Pricing**

My definition of value-based pricing is this:

#### **“Value-based pricing is the method of establishing a price by which the manager calculates and seeks to earn the differentiated economic value of their offering to a particular targeted customer.”**

In most settings where it is used effectively, value-based pricing provides one, but not the only, input into the company’s pricing decision. A useful side effect of employing value-based pricing is to learn your offering’s economic value to a particular customer of interest and then be able to exploit the knowledge during pricing and sales negotiations.

Value-based pricing is a B2B pricing method. Although it is widely used in some seemingly consumer categories like over-the-counter medications or prescription drugs, its main use lies in pricing business products and services. The value-based pricing method has six steps that are shown in the figure below. They can be summarized as follows:

- **Step 1 -** identify the target customer segment or account for which you are calculating the value-based price. This choice is important because the value-based price could be different for each customer.

- **Step 2 –** determine all the competitive offers available to your target customer that they will consider alongside your offer and identify one focal competitor or the closest alternative to your offering.

- **Step 3 –** conduct a head-to-head comparison of your offering with the focal competitive offering, making a list of all the features that the target customer considers relevant for purchase.

- **Step 4 –** identify your offering’s differentiators and deficiencies relative to the focal competitor’s offering. Differentiators are features or affordances that your offering uniquely possesses, while deficiencies are features or affordances it lacks relative to the competitor.

- **Step 5 –** assess the differentiated economic value of your offering using appropriate research methods, essentially putting a dollar value on each differentiator and deficiency and tallying it up.

- **Step 6 –** calculate the value-based price of your offering. Use it as the starting point for quoting and negotiating prices with your target customer.

To understand each step in the process in greater detail, let’s use the example of a commercial display monitor manufacturer, Brand A, who wants to price its latest LED display using the value-based pricing method. Brand A is a leading commercial display manufacturer who is about to introduce a new 80-inch model. It will be the first commercial display of its size in the marketplace. The exercise is conducted from the perspective of Brand A’s manager. The manager wants to set the display’s asking price using the value-based pricing method.

### **Step 1. Identify the target customer.**

Value is customer specific. The specificity is particularly important for pricing in business markets where customers often use the same product as an input or ingredient into vastly different processes or resell the product using different business models. Consequently, the product can have substantially different economic value that is customer-dependent. Where possible, it makes sense to account for these differences in designing nuanced and effective pricing strategies.

For example, quick-drying paint has a superior economic value in a marine application such as painting a boat. The paint’s quick-drying property translates into significant time and labor cost saving when compared to painting the interior of a commercial building where its quick-drying property may not generate the same savings. A naval shipyard and a builder will therefore be willing to pay significantly different prices for the paint and should be treated as different customer types by the paint manufacturer. (Obviously, even if the paint’s formulation is the same, it should be branded and marketed differently to these two customer types to match the different values).

The first step in the value-based pricing method is to identify which customers you are targeting for the pricing decision. As illustrated by the paint example, B2B customers fall into segments based on shared similarities in economic value derived from the product, their buying decision criteria, and processes, and the factors they consider to be important in price discussions.

An oilfield services company has three customer segments. Its marketing and sales organizations distinguish between nationally-owned oil companies like Saudi Aramco, Gazprom, and Petrobras, the major diversified multinational oil companies such as Exxon Mobil, BP and Chevron, and independent oil producers like Hess, Chesapeake Energy, and Murphy Oil.  For pricing purposes, it treats each segment separately and calculates a value-based price for each one. The economic value derived from the products and services, which competitors’ offerings are considered, the relational and power dynamics between the company and its customer, and how the negotiation process is conducted are all different for the three customer groups. These factors are germane to prices.

B2B companies also distinguish between customers based on other criteria such as their level of prior experience with the customer, the size of the account, and so on. The main point here is that ***value-based pricing is a customer-specific method because the economic value is customer-specific to the extent that it can be presented and extracted in that way***.

In the commercial display example, based on differences in buying criteria, the manufacturer identifies three customer segments: (1) small and medium-sized businesses that purchase 1-5 displays directly online, (2) larger organizations like retail and restaurant chains that purchase larger display quantities directly and are serviced by salespeople, and (3) distributors, who purchase displays for resale.

Let’s assume that, for the purpose of this example, the manufacturer is interested in calculating the display’s value-based price for its small and medium-sized business segment.

### **Step 2. Determine all competitive offers and identify the focal competitor.**

When making purchase decisions, most B2B customers consider multiple options. They seek to learn about different options in the marketplace, generate a shortlist of acceptable alternatives, and examine a handful of choices in depth before choosing one. To form a consideration set, many B2B organizations use processes such as a request for proposal, a request for a quote, and a request for tender (for government contracts). These methods differ slightly in how they work, but their main purpose is to identify a subset of suitable options to consider in-depth.

Understanding which competitive offers your customer will consider along with your offering is a crucial component of using the value-based pricing process effectively. This requires a conversation with the customer or some other competitor identification procedure. Once the specific competitive offerings that you are up against are known or identified, it also helps to establish the comparison baseline against which the customer will evaluate the product.

In some applications of value-based pricing, the manager picks two to four other competitive offers that are serious contenders, researches their relevant features, and creates a comparison table describing them, along with the values of the company’s offering and the competitive offerings.

#### ***The focal competitor***

To make the task of eliciting the features and their economic value manageable, a common approach is to identify a single so-called “focal competitor” from the customer’s perspective. The focal competitor is the competitor’s product that the customer considers to be the closest viable alternative to your offering. Some pricing experts call the focal competitor the “next best alternative.”

One note of caution with using the term ‘next best alternative’ is that it can implicitly, and misleadingly, convey the idea that your product is superior to your competitors’ offerings. In reality, the focal competitor could be much superior, significantly inferior, or virtually identical to your offering in its features and the value it delivers to the customer. In cases where you have an established relationship with the customer, the focal competitor might even turn out to be the previous generation of your own product or a superior product from your product line that the customer cannot afford to purchase.

While this winnowing makes the task of understanding customer value more manageable, it has one limitation. Sometimes, it is difficult to identify exactly who the focal competitor is from the customer’s point of view. Many B2B customers may themselves not know the final two options in their consideration set until deep into the purchase decision process. If you wait too long, you may not be able to calculate the value-based price in time to use it for negotiating with them.

It is much easier for the customer, and more realistic, to identify the two or three competitors that are in contention and provide details about these competitive products relatively early on in the decision-making process. A different way to solve the problem of identifying the focal competitor is to use your own judgment or that of experienced colleagues. For example, one specialty chemicals company routinely uses multiple methods including intelligence from key account managers and the knowledge and the ‘gut feeling’ of senior executives to identify the focal competitor relatively early on in its discussions with its significant accounts.

Returning to our example, the display manufacturer conducts research with its small and business-sized business customer accounts and uses its experienced managers’ judgment to narrow down two significant competitors (Display B and Display C). Further, let’s assume it identifies Display B as the focal competitor against which its target customers will evaluate the brand’s new display monitor.

### **Step 3. Conduct a head-to-head comparison of all features.**

This step is the heart of the value-based pricing process and involves the most amount of effort on the manager’s part. It requires you to gather information about your product features and your competitors’ features that are relevant to the customer. Particular attention is given to the focal competitor’s offering to make sure that its features and price are understood accurately.

As can be seen in the figure above, many features are standard for the three display monitors. All three displays are backlit with LEDs, have 4K Ultra HD image quality, and built-in Wifi.

To obtain this information, B2B companies use one or more standard marketing research methods. The display manufacturer may question buyers of its larger customers, talk to its distributors, or hire an independent consultant to conduct a market analysis. Nowadays, in many business markets, obtaining this information is simply a matter of conducting a thorough internet search. Many companies or their channel partners provide detailed information about product features online. Information about prices can be a bit more difficult to find and may require phone calls or conversations.

### **Step 4. Identify the differentiators and the deficiencies.**

Once the comparison chart describing your offering’s features, its focal competitor, and other competitors is developed in step 3, your next task is to assess where your offering stands relative to your focal competitor. The features on which it outperforms are called “value-added differentiators” or points of superiority. They are features with the potential to earn a price premium. On the other hand, there may be other features where your focal competitor performs better. These features are labeled “deficiencies” or points of inferiority indicating that you may have to offer your customer a discount relative to the focal competitor’s price.

Returning to the display’s pricing, the company’s Display A, and its focal competitor Display B, are equivalent in many features. They are LED displays, have 4K Ultra HD definition, built-in WiFi, and a refresh rate of 240 Hz. The figure above shows that Display A has four value-added differentiators and no deficiencies. Its value-added differentiators are that its screen size is 80 inches (vs. 70 inches for Display B), its warranty of 3 years (vs. 2 years), 4 HDMI inputs (vs. 3 inputs for Display B), and the Smart TV feature (which Display B does not have).

Why include additional competitive products in this head-to-head comparison beyond the focal competitor? One reason is to obtain guidance regarding how the customer may value the differences in other features. In our example, notice that one fundamental difference between the two competitive displays, B and C is that Display C has the Smart TV functionality, whereas Display B does not. Even though Display B is larger than Display C (70 inches vs. 65 inches screen size), Display C is still priced \$150 higher than Display B (\$3,199 for Display B, and \$3,349 for Display C), suggesting that customers value Smart TV functionality and are willing to pay extra for it.

### **Step 5. Assess the differentiated economic value of your offering.**

Perhaps the most misunderstood aspect of the value-based pricing process is the process by which the product’s economic value is evaluated. Any product or service has an indeterminate number of features. It is unrealistic to expect any method to be able to assess the economic value of every single feature. Fortunately, you do not need to do this when using value-based pricing. You only need to determine the economic value of your offering’s differences from the focal competitor.

In the example, the display manufacturer would only need to assess how much more money customers are willing to pay for the bigger screen size (80 inches vs. 70 inches), one extra year of warranty (3 years vs. 2 years), one additional HDMI input (4 vs. 3), and the Smart TV functionality.

Specific customer-focused research to obtain this information would require either careful customer interviewing or value-in-use research. The idea is that an early adopter is given the product at a subsidized rate in exchange for letting the company study how they use the product, and the cost savings and the revenue generation opportunities presented by its value-added differentiators and deficiencies. I want to briefly describe two variations for this approach here.

#### ***Credentialed value case study***

One way to discover and document the differentiated economic value of your offering is to cooperate extensively with a single carefully chosen customer and understand your offering’s economic value in the field. The validated information from this exercise is then made available as a business case to salespeople.

The product’s greater economic value may accrue from **cost reductions** **or savings** relative to the competitor’s offer (e.g., simpler or quicker assembly that saves labor costs, fewer materials or cheaper ingredients, more streamlined design, etc.) or from **better performance** (e.g., faster operation, longer life, less downtime, etc.). In exchange for validating the product’s superior economic performance, the customer gets special incentives such as reduced pricing, prioritized access to future new products, a discount on service plans, or special standing in a user group.

#### ***Value calculator***

A value calculator is a spreadsheet- or web-based app that salespeople, technical staff, consultants, or customers use to calculate the differentiated economic value derived from your offering. Value calculators allow detailed calculations covering both initial purchase costs to the customer, and ongoing costs of product operation, service, maintenance, and training.

Value calculators are common in the B2B space with sellers like [Microsoft Azure](https://azure.microsoft.com/en-us/pricing/tco/calculator/), [Amazon AWS](https://calculator.aws/#/), [John Deere](https://tools.deere.com/john-deere-total-cost-of-ownership/en_GB/), and [Hewlett Packard Enterprises](https://www.hpe.com/us/en/solutions/tco-calculators.html) providing them to customers. In the case of [John Deere](https://tools.deere.com/john-deere-total-cost-of-ownership/en_GB/), for example, offers potential customers of its tractors a set of tools to calculate the total cost of ownership. Customers can quantify and compare the value of John Deere’s tractors to other tractors using criteria such as fuel savings, fluid consumption from hydraulic work, maintenance and repair and depreciation, in addition to the cost of the machine itself.

Let’s say that by using these methods, the company in our example finds out that the economic value to the target customer is \$750 for the larger screen size, \$125 for the HDMI input, \$200 for an extra year of warranty, and \$250 for the Smart TV functionality.

### **Step 6. Calculate the value-based price.**

Once the differences of the product are quantified, the output is the product’s value-based price. For the display monitor, the net premium of the differentiated features is \$1,325 (\$750 + \$125 + \$200 + \$250) over Brand B’s price. Thus, the value-based price of Display A is \$3,199 + \$1,325 = \$4,524.

In most cases, the value-based price calculated in this way is not the final price that is quoted or earned. Instead, it often provides an initial starting point for internal pricing discussions or conversations with the customer. From this value-based price, downward adjustments are made to account for the customer relationship, environment, and competitive factors. Various incentives may also be offered. A simple heuristic is to share the value premium of your offering 50-50 with customers. In a strong economic environment with high customer demand, the company may try to keep more of the premium for itself. When the market is weak, the company may be hard-pressed to keep any of the value premium at all.

There is usually a significant gap between the differentiated economic value and the product’s price. This gap is a function of your pricing power in the marketplace, the significance of your offering’s differentiation to the customer, the ready availability of other alternatives, and so on. According to [venture capitalist Tom Tunguz](https://tomtunguz.com/three-value-propositions/), startups are typically only able to capture 10–15% of the differentiated economic value they create. When the company is larger and more established than its customers are, it may be able to earn as much as 50-60% of the generated differentiated value.
