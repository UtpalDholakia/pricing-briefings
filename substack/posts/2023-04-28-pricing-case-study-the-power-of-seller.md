When I searched for “What should a good bottle of wine cost?” on Google, the first item that popped up was a [Food & Wine article](https://www.foodandwine.com/wine/wine-how-much-spend-wine) written by “someone who buys wine for a living.” The expert’s advice is clear:

> ***“Your real wheelhouse for value \[for wine\] happens at about \$15 to \$25. That's where you can really find great value from people that really want to make a good wine, that are thinking about value.”***

In behavioral pricing, we are always thinking of our customers’ reference prices, particularly how to influence which reference prices customers use in their buying decisions.

[Embedded content: www.youtube-nocookie.com](https://www.youtube-nocookie.com/embed/Ip5jG3djdyk?rel=0&autoplay=0&showinfo=0&enablejsapi=0)

Just a few years ago, only judgment and decision making researchers knew about concepts like [product decoys](https://www.bbc.com/worklife/article/20190801-the-trick-that-makes-you-overspend), [anchoring](http://changingminds.org/disciplines/marketing/pricing/price_anchoring.htm), and [extremeness aversion](https://digest.bps.org.uk/2012/04/30/people-prefer-the-middle-option/). But today, these concepts have turned into widely-known and used influence tactics. A majority, managers and consumers alike, know exactly how they work. And the more customers know about these effects, the [less influential they will be](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0065260108600179?casa_token=GeTpJVheaZ0AAAAA:Cw2gcUyNFbqEi4VkvHWHc4S038sa7XYWJIaX-bsEE-Yi3TGpP2t9VchYZhu_EUAI3PG8mYCwU60).

To stay ahead of the game, we should ask different questions about the role of price and think of new ways of influencing customers. More powerful questions today, then, are *how do customers form price references in the first place,* and *can managers influence the process of forming reference prices of customers?* The shift is from using contextual influence tactics to building customers’ understanding and knowledge of prices in the product category, even if it is just-in-time for the purchase decision. And the concept involved is the ***price threshold*** which isn’t used widely or systematically yet.

### **What is a price threshold?**

Imagine that after more than a decade, my big-screen television is on the blink. I want to replace it with the largest, highest-quality television I can find *for a reasonable price*. The problem is I am not sure what a reasonable price for a really good TV should be. Is it \$200? \$1,000? or \$5,000? If I had to guess, I would say somewhere between \$1,000 and \$5,000, but I simply don’t know. I haven’t kept up with advances in television technology.

Now if I want to make a smart TV buying decision, I need to set up some rules about my spending first. I may say, “I don’t want to spend more than \$3,000 for a new TV, no matter how amazing it is.” This is my *upper-limit threshold*. And even in my current state of TV ignorance, I may believe, “I don’t think I will get a good-quality big-screen TV for under \$500.” This is my *lower-limit threshold*. In the absence of current knowledge, my values may be off, but they serve as relatively hard constraints based on my budget and beliefs.

Customers f[orm and use upper- and lower-limit thresholds](https://www.jstor.org/stable/pdf/3149411.pdf?casa_token=WjFlsMJ8YzgAAAAA:Ekjf7_71SVRqtgPMnPvCiuWyC0IzeP2qwKl9hz7yzX6c_hZvko3-v9crtZLWTVkDOfhzkum2L_2rwg9i4strSjclvWUTh_AWL92zTazG3Z9VzTB3FA6e) all the time. These thresholds establish the range of prices that customers will consider as the boundaries. Any choice that falls outside this range will be summarily rejected. A price below the lower limit will be deemed as too cheap and of questionable quality. A price above the upper limit will be seen as too expensive and unaffordable or not worth it.

After some research, I may conclude that there are a lot of excellent big-screen TV choices available in the price range of \$1,900 to \$2,700, and I am probably going to end up buying a TV within this range. This is my *preferred price range threshold*.

![](https://bucketeer-e05bbc84-baa3-437e-9518-adb32be77984.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/d0849fd5-21a5-4189-b0dc-6132c25da637_1280x1042.jpeg)

None of the three price thresholds, not even the *preferred price range*, answers my original question of what a reasonable price to pay for a big-screen television is. But the three thresholds do lead me to much more tentative knowledge: “*I feel like I should be able to buy a very good big-screen TV for a price between \$1,900 and \$2,700*.” This understanding will guide which TVs I consider further.

### **Seller-Supplied Price Thresholds**

Price thresholds are [powerful influencers of customer decisions](https://www.brides.com/story/american-wedding-study-how-much-average-wedding-2018-cost). Except in situations where customers purchase the product all the time *and* pay attention to prices diligently, they need price thresholds when making buying decisions. Leave out things like common grocery items, gasoline, fast food, and the like, and price thresholds dictate which prices customers consider as reasonable and which ones as out-of-bounds.

For everything from dental services to appliances to subscriptions, customers need help in figuring out their price thresholds. Of course, they can rely on prices they can see in the marketplace. They can also find guidance from intermediaries and experts like the Food & Wine article, or look for opinions about prices in other buyers’ reviews. However, marketers have the unique opportunity to influence and actively create price thresholds for their target customers to use.

> #### **Every time customers ask themselves, “How much should I spend on X?” without having a firm answer, the seller of X has the opportunity to establish price thresholds.**

### **De Beers, the engagement ring, and two months’ salary**

The ingenious marketing approach used by diamond seller De Beers over the past 80 years illustrates the durability and influence of price thresholds. You probably know this story, but it’s worth unpacking it in detail for some object pricing lessons. The De Beers strategy unfolded in two stages and tackled two significant challenges for customers: what to buy and how much to pay for it.

#### **The First Challenge**

A marriage can be [proposed in all kinds of ways](https://www.sbs.com.au/topics/voices/relationships/article/2016/02/11/marriage-proposal-customs-around-world). In Japan, a marriage proposal is marked by an exchange of nine symbolic gifts. In India, the man proposes to the parents of the woman he wants to marry. In Scotland, the man sets about accomplishing a set of tasks set by the bride’s father, and in Ghana, he brings a group of his friends and knocks on the door of the woman’s family to proclaim his intentions. In these and many other cultures, diamond engagement rings are nowhere in the picture. Or at least they didn’t use to be, until recently.

For De Beers, the first challenge, then, was to associate their product, the diamond, with a concrete and broadly applicable use and purchase occasion. They settled on the American marriage proposal, perhaps because there was already a tradition of giving a ring to the woman, itself established by marketing efforts. (There was also a concerted push to [popularize male engagement rings](https://www.jstor.org/stable/pdf/3790353.pdf?casa_token=3G-Yyt1Kq_gAAAAA:WeRhoofXjVG4IhkiQgasrroyj6TvkJT3uylnA9_x2KoLgBN1RuTnMwyGN82LqM6DQZUZi_D235x1Wo0PgwFVt7lAx4AUFxkpbHCw8kK8ukqsodMmppG-) in the 1920s, but that idea never took off). In describing the 1890s, the [retail historian Vicki Howard observes](https://www.jstor.org/stable/pdf/3790353.pdf?casa_token=3G-Yyt1Kq_gAAAAA:WeRhoofXjVG4IhkiQgasrroyj6TvkJT3uylnA9_x2KoLgBN1RuTnMwyGN82LqM6DQZUZi_D235x1Wo0PgwFVt7lAx4AUFxkpbHCw8kK8ukqsodMmppG-):

> “When one considers the variety of ring styles and practices during this period, it becomes clear that jewelry manufacturers and retailers, from an early date, sought to invent traditions that would create new uses for their product. In addition to wedding bands and diamond engagement rings, catalogs for the trade and for the public offered specialty rings for babies and for young girls, signet and graduation rings for men, and dinner rings for women… The industry knew it had a vested interest in wedding customs that involved jewelry purchases and united in national campaigns to promote new traditions that required their expert, specialized services.”

Advertisement by Traub targeting jewelers in *The Jeweler’s Circular*, 1927.

![](https://bucketeer-e05bbc84-baa3-437e-9518-adb32be77984.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/bf9baef4-a669-44a5-a557-2d867851956d_762x844.png)

Some use pitches stuck, others didn’t. By the 1940s, engagement rings for men had fizzled out, engagement rings for women were common but they were as likely to have diamonds as they did rubies, sapphires, or some other precious stone.

Then, De Beers came along with its famous slogan “A Diamond is Forever” in 1947. It is now widely regarded as the best advertising slogan of the twentieth century, and for good reason. By advertising this idea over decades, the company linked the occasion of an engagement with a diamond ring and made the other use pitches fade away. No one buys a diamond ring for a baby today.

[Embedded content: www.youtube-nocookie.com](https://www.youtube-nocookie.com/embed/8pU6WQXkiOU?rel=0&autoplay=0&showinfo=0&enablejsapi=0)

De Beers characterized a man’s purchase and gifting of a diamond ring when proposing marriage to a woman as a timeless and universal tradition (when it was neither). It reinforced this message repeatedly over the years until eventually, this idea took cultural hold in the United States.

From America, the same idea spread to Japan, Germany, India, China, and other countries which had never used diamond rings in this way before. As cultural norms changed, De Beers marketed diamonds in the same way to same-sex couples. The “A Diamond is Forever” association became ubiquitous. relevant to everyone everywhere.

[Embedded content: www.youtube-nocookie.com](https://www.youtube-nocookie.com/embed/n1TSQj9ANkI?rel=0&autoplay=0&showinfo=0&enablejsapi=0)

Just one statistic is enough to illustrate how successful De Beers has been in tackling the first challenge of conquering the use occasion. In the late 1930s, before De Beers created the slogan and associated proposal and diamond ring, only about 10% of engagement rings in the United States contained diamonds. However, by 1990, the number of engagement rings with diamonds had gone up to 80%. As the graph below shows, advertising-driven upward trajectories occurred in Japan and China.

![](https://bucketeer-e05bbc84-baa3-437e-9518-adb32be77984.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/bef173f1-5278-47d0-b249-a90798e3d9e6_1133x711.png)

Source: De Beers research.

The “A Diamond is Forever” message solved the first challenge of when to trigger a diamond purchase. It changed what was an atypical and unusual purchase without any social or religious ramifications into a conventional purchase that is expected of every individual who wants to propose marriage to their partner.

#### The Second Challenge

The “A Diamond is Forever” message, while successful, still leaves a second formidable challenge that customers have to deal with. How much should the engagement ring cost? This is the *classic price threshold challenge*. Without a clear answer like the Food & Wine article, potential buyers will remain doubtful and confused. And if we know one thing about consumer decisions, it is that doubt and confusion are bad for action. They delay purchases.

This is where De Beers did what very few companies or trade associations do. They provided a concrete and substantial price threshold to the eager fiancé, to remove confusion, and prevent them from pinching pennies and buying a ring with a small or poorly-cut diamond that didn’t cost enough. *How to create a universally applicable price threshold that consumers can understand and agree with?* A ridiculously low price for one person would be a completely unaffordable price for another. It wouldn’t make sense to say, “A diamond engagement ring should cost at least \$X” because \$X would be too high for some people and too low for others. The Food & Wine article price threshold, \$15 - \$25 for a good bottle of wine, misses the mark using this criterion.

Also, notice how this is a problem that exists in many categories today. How much should a good car cost? How much should a tooth extraction cost? How much should a summer vacation cost? How much should a birthday party cost? There are no clear guidelines, most consumers are just winging it.

This is where De Beers’ ingenuity comes into play, serving as an object lesson to pricing strategists. The company designed another inspired marketing message devised in the 1980s that *circumvented a specific price point* and provided a *reasonable price threshold* applicable regardless of income level.

> **The seller-supplied price threshold is that a diamond engagement ring should cost about two months’ salary.**

This time the ad showed an attractive just-engaged woman called Jane Smith brandishing her diamond ring. The tagline read, “2 months’ salary showed the future Mrs. Smith what the future will be like.” The subtext was that if Mr. Smith proposed with a diamond ring that cost less than two months of his salary, it should plant seeds of doubt in the lady’s mind about the intensity of his love and commitment to her.

![](https://bucketeer-e05bbc84-baa3-437e-9518-adb32be77984.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/ccf7e600-54ca-4590-9fa1-d1c4a287a559_768x1032.jpeg)

What is particularly clever about this seller-supplied price threshold is that it is *substantial, flexible, and feasible* all at once. It is not one fixed value. Rather, it is a moving target levered to the customer’s wherewithal. If you are George Clooney, you [spend \$750,000 for an engagement ring](https://www.yatesjewelers.com/amal-alamuddin-engagement-ring.html), but if you are a young person in your early twenties working in your first entry-level job, you will spend \$1,000-2,000. For both individuals, it is a substantial amount of money that is dictated by the seller-supplied price threshold. Sales managers would call this price threshold a [stretch budget for the ring buyer](https://hbr.org/2017/01/the-stretch-goal-paradox).

This two-month-salary price threshold for engagement rings remains firmly entrenched more than three decades later. Amid the emotional turmoil associated with the momentous decision of proposing marriage to a romantic partner and choosing an engagement ring, this price threshold serves as a soothing heuristic that signals the normalcy of purchasing an engagement ring.

### **Lessons for Pricing Managers**

1.  Influence tactics like decoy effects, anchoring, and extremeness aversion are losing steam as customers become knowledgeable and desensitized. To stay ahead, managers need to think of new ways of influencing customers.

2.  Customers need help in establishing price thresholds for purchases they don’t make regularly or frequently. They search across different information sources, looking for help in establishing spending thresholds.

3.  Unlike diamond engagement rings, most product categories *do not have* clear price threshold guidelines for customers. There is room for managers to think of creative ways to establish and then publicize thresholds. These efforts could be product category-based or driven by a single leading brand in the category.

4.  It is useful to distinguish between upper- and lower-limit thresholds on the one hand and preferred price range thresholds on the other. The upper- and lower-limit thresholds are based on customers’ personal budgetary constraints, beliefs, and idiosyncratic preferences, and may be difficult to influence collectively.

5.  Preferred price range thresholds are the most useful thresholds that sellers can provide to customers. They help customers to answer the core question, “*What is a reasonable amount to spend on the product?*”

6.  As the De Beers case study showed, the most powerful seller-supplied price thresholds are *substantial, flexible, and feasible* all at once. Instead of a fixed amount, they yoke the price threshold to customers’ wherewithal in a general way. They are compelling, easy to communicate, and credible.

7.  And finally, the proof of the pudding is in the eating. The most effective seller-supplied thresholds influence customer behavior. The two months-salary price threshold for engagement rings continues to drive purchases of millions of people. According to the 2018 American Wedding Study conducted by Brides magazine, the [average price paid for an engagement ring in the United States was \$7,829](https://www.brides.com/story/american-wedding-study-how-much-average-wedding-2018-cost), which works out to just over two months’ after-tax salary for the median wage earner.
