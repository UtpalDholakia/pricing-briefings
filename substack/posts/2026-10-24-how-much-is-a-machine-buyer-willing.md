##### ***Summary.*** In a [previous post](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/are-behavioral-pricing-tactics-dying), I found that when choosing which item to purchase, machine buyers completely ignored the left digit of prices, correctly added up partitioned prices, preferred a single all-inclusive price when the totals were equal, and chose the item at the top of the list between comparable options, essentially neutralizing behavioral pricing tactics. In this post, I report five new experiments involving 29,070 responses from 13 AI models, 4 of which I ran with their reasoning or thinking modes on and off (for a total of 17 model configurations) that put a dollar value on 8 product features and list position. I hypothesized and found that machine buyers mostly overlap in their willingness to pay (WTP) when a feature’s value can be computed, and are very idiosyncratic when it must be judged subjectively. Free shipping was valued exactly at \$6.99 by 10 of the 17 models, but when compared to a 4.4-star rating, a 4.7-star rating was worth \$14 to Google’s Gemini 4 Argon and \$47 to Anthropic’s Claude Sonnet 5.5, and a one-year warranty extension was worth anywhere between \$5 and more than \$40, depending on the model. Six machine buyers, including GPT-6 Astra, Claude Opus 5.5, and Gemini 4 Argon, used badges, scarcity messages, and embellished product copy only to break identical ties between options, while a few smaller models paid significant amounts for them. Finally, when I asked the agents directly what each feature was worth, there were large gaps between stated WTP and expressed WTP of all machine buyers. For sellers, purchasing by machine buyers means that the value of a product’s features is highly contingent on the underlying AI model, with significant variability by feature. Machine buyers are not rational automatons; they are a lot like human buyers.

### **How Much Are Machine Buyers Willing to Pay?**

In a [previous post](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/are-behavioral-pricing-tactics-dying), I reported two experiments in which I tested whether two behavioral pricing tactics, charm pricing and price partitioning, work on machine buyers. Four AI agents from OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, and Meta chose between pairs of nearly identical products that varied in how their prices were presented. A one-cent price difference was enough for the machine buyer to choose the lower-priced product every single time, essentially erasing the effects of charm prices and partitioned prices. One AI agent, OpenAI’s GPT-6 Sol, paid \$2 more for free shipping, but only when the free-shipping product appeared first and the cheaper product second on the page. In this post, I want to build on these findings and ask another significant pricing question about machine buyers: ***how much are they willing to pay for a better product?***

For human buyers, decades of research has examined [how best](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1509/jmkr.48.1.172?casa_token=AqICUkfF4_MAAAAA%3AcxRWHVGbcw480eKBc-M6hSRzYZLNx4Ld2DxGxPsqEcSBv6mzShqLVIoyAE6ixXlQCa53uvCsxehqn6E) to measure WTP, from direct questioning to choice-based conjoint analysis. Research on machine buyers is now beginning to apply these methods to study their WTP. One [February 2026 study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.09802) inferred WTP for hotel-room features from AI models’ choices. The larger AI models produced economically interpretable estimates, but often valued amenities more highly than historical human benchmarks. Furthermore, these valuations shifted when additional user information was given to the AI agent. For instance, business-traveler personas and examples of expensive past purchases generally raised WTP estimates, whereas examples of cheaper past choices brought WTP estimates closer to human benchmarks. Related research on AI as simulated consumers is cautionary. A September 2026 study comparing WTP of machine buyers with human valuations elicited for real purchases found overestimation for two hedonic products and mixed results for two utilitarian products, suggesting that even when AI estimates match average human WTP, it may conceal different demand distributions, leading to mispredictions about how many human buyers will purchase at a given price.

Research on AI agents performing shopping tasks suggests that their purchasing behavior also depends on the information environment. A [recent preprint](https://ideas.repec.org/p/arx/papers/2608.22697.html) found that with complete information, 7 of 8 models weighted cents proportionally to dollars, while 6 treated discount savings as equivalent to reductions in list price. But when fees were incurred during product detail retrieval, and instructions were vague, AI shopping agents often skipped the information needed to compare unit prices and selected lower-value options. Explicit instructions to minimize price per ounce led to better choices. Although this study does not directly estimate WTP, it suggests that a seemingly high WTP reported by a machine buyer may reflect incomplete search or an ambiguous buying objective. This research suggests that WTP of machine buyers may be sensitive to the buying context.

Effective pricing depends on knowing what a feature is worth to the customer, but when the customer is a machine buyer, the feature is worth whatever the underlying AI model has learned to believe it is worth. How can a seller set a price premium for a superior feature when different machine buyers value it differently and cannot (like human buyers) articulate what they would pay? This is the pricing conundrum I explore in this post by investigating the WTP of 17 machine buyers for different product features.

### **The 17 AI Model Configurations Used in the Study**

I tested 13 AI models from five companies, using each company’s developer interface, or, for the three open-weight models, through [Together AI](https://www.together.ai/). They included the four models from my first study (OpenAI’s GPT-6 Sol, Google’s Gemini 3.8 Flash, Anthropic’s Claude Sonnet 5.5, and Meta’s open-weight Muse Glimmer 30B), plus OpenAI’s GPT-6 Astra, GPT-6.1 Sol, and GPT-6 Luna, OpenAI’s open-weight gpt-oss-120b, Anthropic’s Claude Opus 5.5 and Claude Haiku 5.5, Google’s Gemini 4 Argon and Gemini 3.5 Flash-lite, and DeepSeek’s V4.1 Flash. Every model ran at its default settings. Four models allow their reasoning (the “thinking” a model does before it responds) to be switched off or turned up, so I ran them a second time with the reasoning changed from their default: Luna, Sonnet, and Haiku with reasoning off, and Flash-lite, whose default is minimal thinking, with thinking set to high. This resulted in 17 AI model configurations in all, which I refer to as “17 models” or “17 machine buyers” for ease of exposition in the rest of this piece.

### **Common Procedure**

The experimental setup was very similar to the [previous behavioral pricing study](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/are-behavioral-pricing-tactics-dying). Each machine buyer was instructed that it was a shopping assistant authorized to buy one product for the user, then given a need (e.g., “I need wireless earbuds for my commute and the gym”), and then presented with two product listings. Each model provided a one-sentence reason and then indicated its choice. The products were of fictional brands from the same three categories as the previous study: blenders, wireless earbuds, and coffee grinders. Within each condition, I alternated the order in which the products appeared, rotated the feature descriptions between the two options, and rotated the categories. ### **Measuring the WTP of 17 Machine Buyers**

To put a dollar value on each feature, I adapted a procedure from psychophysics called the [method of constant stimuli](https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03194543), which researchers use to measure how large a difference must be before it is noticed (related to the widely known concept of [just noticeable difference](https://psycnet.apa.org/record/1974-10005-001), or j.n.d.; the method of constant stimuli is used to measure the j.n.d.). With this method, the experimenter fixes a set of stimulus levels in advance, presents each level many times in random order, and records the share of trials at each level in which the subject provides a particular response. The point at which the share exceeds 50% is known as the [point of subjective equality](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6927926/), defined as the level at which the study participant is indifferent between the two options. Behavioral scientists use a variation of this procedure, called the [multiple price list](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles/pdf/doi/10.1257/000282802762024700), to [measure risk and time](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/experimental-economics/article/abs/elicitation-using-multiple-price-list-formats/64DC7053BB669012D6201B10B0B455D6) preferences.

In the present case, the stimulus is a price gap. On every trial in the studies reported here, an AI agent chose between two product options that were identical except for one feature, such as a 4.7-star rating instead of 4.4 stars. Furthermore, the comparison product option always cost \$80.00, while the product with the focal feature was priced at \$80.00 + *g*, where *g* was drawn from a fixed list of eight values that were set in advance. For most features, the list was \$0, \$0.5, \$1, \$2, \$4, \$8, \$16, and \$32. For features that the pilot test (described below) revealed agents valued more highly, I expanded the list (e.g., from \$0 to \$64 for the rating). Each price gap was shown 10 times with the featured product listed first and 10 times with it listed second, with the product category rotated across trials. This resulted in 160 choices per feature for each machine buyer.

I then estimated a psychometric function for each model configuration, product feature, and listing order, using a logistic curve giving the probability of choosing the featured product at each price gap:

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/fb2428a9-fcde-446d-b9d7-80eeab5ed93c_1506x305.png)

Here, *g\** is the gap at which the agent chooses the featured product half the time, referred to as the agent’s *switch point*, and considered the dollar value the agent assigns to the feature. The parameter *s* measures how gradual the switch is. A small *s* implies that the machine buyer flips abruptly at one price point (the binary change described in my previous article), whereas a large *s* indicates that the agent trades off price against the feature gradually. I fitted the curves using maximum likelihood estimation and computed 95% confidence intervals by bootstrapping (resampling the trials at each gap 2,000 times), following standard practice in [fitting psychometric functions](https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03194544). Note that the fitted curve can’t be used in the following two cases, and I consider these two cases separately:

1\) *Never switched*. If an agent still chose the featured product in most trials at the largest gap, I report its value only as “more than” that gap in the results tables.

2\) *Switched instantaneously*. If an agent switched completely between two adjacent gaps, I report the value as the range of the gap (e.g., “\$6.98 to \$7.00”) in the results.

An example will help to explain the procedure. The next table provides GPT-6 Astra’s choices in the rating test, combining the two listing orders.

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/3e47d72f-78b6-486b-8124-7cceea8b1a74_1404x194.png)

In this example, Astra chose the higher-rated product each time when it cost up to \$8 more. Its choices then shifted between \$12 and \$16, and it never chose the higher-rated product at \$24 or more. The curve fitted to this data crosses the 50% mark at \$15.02, indicating that GPT-6 Astra’s switch point for the incremental value of the 4.7-star rating (over the 4.4-star rating) is \$15.02. In other words, GPT-6 Astra would pay up to \$15.02 *more* for an \$80 product with a 4.7-star rating (compared to a 4.4-star rating).

Additionally, rotating the listing position allowed me to measure the valuation of the position (1^(st) or 2^(nd)). If being listed first is worth *v* dollars to an agent, its switch point will be higher by *v* when the featured product is listed first, and lower by *v* when it is listed second. I therefore report a feature’s value as the average of the two switch points and the value of the first position as half their difference. For GPT-6 Astra, the two orders produced the same switch point, meaning that it wasn’t willing to pay more for the option’s position on the list.

### **The Five Experiments**

Given that the experiments described here build on the [previous piece](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/are-behavioral-pricing-tactics-dying), which had two experiments, they are numbered Studies 3-7.

#### ***Study 3. Replication of Study 2 idiosyncratic choice***

The most surprising result in my last piece came from a single condition of Study 2 in which Product A cost \$70 + \$8 shipping (\$78 in total) and Product B cost \$80 with free shipping. A machine buyer who adds up the totals should always choose Product A, because it is \$2 cheaper. Three of the four agents did so on every trial. However, OpenAI’s GPT-6 Sol did so whenever Product A was listed first, but when Product A was listed second, it chose the more expensive Product B in 19 of 25 trials. Study 3 was designed to consider three questions related to this result: (1) Is this result reliable, or was something weird going on during that run? I reran the same 50 prompts, using the same products, product codes, categories, and listing order; (2) Is the weird result limited to GPT-6 Sol, or to OpenAI’s models in general? I ran the prompts on all 17 models, including the three other GPT-6 models; and (3) Does the phrase “free shipping” cause the weird result? I added a second condition in which Product B’s price read “\$80.00, shipping included” instead of “\$80.00, free shipping” to try and rule out this possibility.

#### ***Study 4. Free shipping***

The “Free shipping” feature was tested in Study 4. While most product features don’t have a single unambiguous economic value, free shipping does, in the sense that it is worth exactly what the shipping charge would be in the competing option (everything else being the same between them). This makes it a very useful benchmark for valuation by machine buyers. In this study, one option cost \$80 + \$6.99 shipping, totaling \$86.99, while the other option came with free shipping and its price was varied from \$80 to \$100. A buyer who compares total prices should choose the free shipping option whenever the price is below \$86.99 and switch instantaneously to the alternative option at prices above it, thus making the switch point \$6.99. To test the precision of machine buyers’ buying responses, the steps in this study also included \$86.98 and \$87.00, one cent on either side of the expected switch point. Contrarily, an agent that switches choices well above \$6.99 pays a premium for the free shipping benefit, while an agent that switches choices much below this amount penalizes free shipping.

#### ***Study 5. Meaningless differentiation***

The idea that meaningful brands are often created from meaningless differentiation using irrelevant attributes is one of the most compelling ideas (and also the title of one of my [favorite papers](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/002224379403100302)) in marketing. In the present context, my focus is on cues sellers use to make a product appear more appealing without delivering higher objective quality. In this study, I tested whether machine buyers are willing to pay for such cues. The product with the cue had one of three “irrelevant” features: (1) an embellished description, whereby the same feature was described in more embellished language (for e.g., “powerful 1,000-watt motor” instead of 1,000-watt motor” or “large 48 oz. jar” instead of “48 oz. jar”); (2) a scarcity message (e.g., “Only 3 left in stock”); and (3) 23 more reviews: 1,249 instead of 1,226 with the same 4.4-star rating. The logic behind these choices was that, in each case, these three cues provide no additional functional information about the option and therefore the 17 machine buyers should have a WTP=0 for them.

#### ***Study 6. Subjective value features***

The goal of Study 6 was to examine the WTP for a different type of feature, one that actually provides useful information about the product or the seller, and is therefore likely to have a WTP\>0. However, unlike free shipping, the value is subjective and therefore likely to vary among buyers, whether human or machine. Four specific added subjective-value features were tested in this study: (1) a 4.7-star rating instead of 4.4 stars, (2) a 2-year warranty instead of a 1-year warranty, (3) an “Editor’s pick” badge, and (4) a “Sponsored” label. The practical issue here is how the 17 machine buyers differ in their WTP for this type of feature. If they are found to have similar WTPs for a feature, the seller could set a single price premium for all machine buyers, but if they vary a lot, more customized pricing would be warranted.

#### ***Study 7. Direct WTP elicitation***

In the last study of this set, the goal was to test whether asking a machine buyer directly about their valuations would provide useful information. In separate conversations with each of the 17 machine buyers, I asked them the maximum additional amount they would pay for each of the 8 features and for being listed first on the page (rather than second), if the two options were otherwise identical. See below for an example of the stimulus. Each question was asked 10 times. If machine buyers can accurately report their own valuations of individual features, their answers should match the switch points calculated in Studies 4-6. However, if they can’t do so, a seller cannot learn a machine buyer’s valuations by asking it and must resort to indirect WTP elicitation techniques, as we do with humans.

#### ***Some additional bells and whistles***

A couple of additional points across the studies are worth noting. First, two cues, one from Study 5, the scarcity message, and one from Study 6, the “Sponsored” label, were run differently, in a reverse option setup. This is because a pilot test showed that most machine buyers avoided products carrying these cues when prices were equalized. Consequently, in these studies, the option with these two cues was made cheaper instead of more expensive. Thus, in both cases, the switch point reflects the discount a machine buyer needs when encountering this cue, rather than a premium.

Second, I reran three features from Studies 4-6 using a reworded version of the instructions to check whether the results depended on specific wording. These were (1) free shipping, (2) the embellished description, and (3) the 4.7-star rating. The reworded version changed the machine buyer’s instructions and the shopping request without adding anything about price or value, and it repeated each price step 5 times in each listing order. If the results reflect the models and not my phrasing, the ranking of the settings should be similar under both wordings. See figure below for an example of a stimulus with wording change. There were no differences on account of the wording, so this factor is not considered further in this piece.

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/750ad08e-6182-4194-96a8-d4925066a3e9_1986x738.png)

Example of stimulus with wording change (marked in green)

Across all studies, the 17 AI models provided 29,070 responses. The run took approximately 2 hours. I preregistered the hypotheses and analysis plan on [AsPredicted](https://aspredicted.org/mm35mk.pdf) before the main run. Note that after preregistering, I added GPT-6 Astra, which was not in the original plan.

### **Pilot test**

Before the main run, I ran a pilot test of 1,808 responses on 16 of the 17 models (113 per model; GPT-6 Astra was added later). The pilot served three purposes. First, it sought to confirm that every model returned a readable answer. All 1,808 replies could be read on the first attempt. Second, it provided guidance on setting the range of the price gaps. In the pilot, several models still chose the higher-rated product at a \$32 premium, the top of the original range, and some still chose the 2-year warranty or free shipping at the highest gap tested. In response, in the main run, I widened the range to \$64 for the rating, \$48 for the warranty, and \$20 for free shipping. The third purpose of the pilot test was to set the direction of each test. As discussed earlier, the scarcity message and the Sponsored label were found to have negative value and were reversed in the main run.

### **Results**

I discuss the results of the studies sequentially, combining their presentation in a couple of places where appropriate. Throughout this section, when I say that a machine buyer paid or was willing to pay a certain amount for a feature, I mean it was the price premium it accepted, as evidenced by its choices.

#### ***Study 3 Results***

The results from Study 2 in the previous piece were replicated exactly. When the \$78 product (\$70 + \$8 shipping) was listed first, GPT-6 Sol chose it in all 25 trials. When it was listed second, below the \$80 free shipping option, GPT-6 Sol chose it in only 6 of 25 trials, the exact same numbers as Study 2 in the behavioral pricing study. The other three models from Study 2, Gemini 3.8 Flash, Claude Sonnet 5.5, and Muse Glimmer 30B, again chose the cheaper product in all 50 trials. Table 1 shows the results for all 17 models.

[Embedded content: datawrapper.dwcdn.net](https://datawrapper.dwcdn.net/cWaJI/1/)

Thus, the results of Study 3 show that only GPT-6 Sol showed the effect again, but none of the other OpenAI models (GPT-6 Astra, GPT6.1 Sol, or GPT-6 Luna) showed it, and no other machine buyer showed the pattern of paying the premium only when the option with free shipping was listed first. Furthermore, the label “free shipping” did not make a significant difference. When the \$80 option’s price included the label “shipping included,” GPT-6 Sol chose the \$78 option in 11 of 25 trials when it was listed second, up from 6/25 trials. This difference is not statistically significant (p = .23). GPT-6 Sol’s explanations for the choice reflect that it noticed the price difference and chose to pay it, describing the \$80 option as having “free shipping and only a \$2 higher total price.”

As Table 1 also shows, two other machine buyers chose the \$80 option most of the time, regardless of its position: GPT-6 Luna with reasoning off and Gemini 3.5 Flash-lite at its default (minimal thinking) setting. However, their explanations reveal a different issue than GPT-6 Sol. In 63 of the 67 trials in which Luna (with reasoning off) chose the \$80 option, it described the product as cheaper or lower in total (e.g., “the Orlo Mix Pro costs \$2 less overall and includes free shipping”), indicating an arithmetic error rather than a preference for free shipping. Thus, Study 3 reveals some inconsistencies between the machine buyers.

#### ***Study 4 Results***

Study 4 tested the “Free shipping” feature, which can be objectively valued. The results showed that 10 of the 17 machine buyers chose the free shipping option in every trial when it was priced at \$86.98, one cent below the other option’s \$86.99 total, and switched to the other product in most or all trials when it was priced at \$87.00, or one cent above. The listing order did not matter in this case. Put differently, these 10 machine buyers valued free shipping at precisely \$6.99 that it saved them. They included all three Anthropic models at their default settings, Gemini 4 Argon and Gemini 3.8 Flash, GPT-6 Astra, GPT-6.1 Sol, and GPT-6 Luna, Muse Glimmer 30B, and DeepSeek V4.1 Flash. This result is consistent with the [recent research finding](https://ideas.repec.org/p/arx/papers/2608.22697.html) that most models weighted cents in proportion to dollars when they had complete information. Table 2 shows the dollar value of free shipping to each machine buyer.

[Embedded content: datawrapper.dwcdn.net](https://datawrapper.dwcdn.net/5PIW4/1/)

Two machine buyers, GPT-6 Luna with reasoning off and Gemini 3.5 Flash-lite at its default setting, still chose the free shipping option in 14 and 16 of 20 trials, respectively, when its price was \$20 higher, i.e., when it cost \$13.01 more in total than the alternative. These are the same two machine buyers that got the arithmetic wrong in Study 3. Three other machine buyers paid a premium for free shipping: Claude Haiku with thinking off (\$7.97), gpt-oss-120b (\$8.75), and Claude Sonnet with thinking off (\$7.02-\$8.02). GPT-6 Sol was again a special case, with its choice depending on the position of the free shipping option. When the free shipping option was listed first, GPT-6 Sol’s switch point was \$8.77, i.e., it paid a \$1.78 premium for free shipping. But when it was listed second, the switch point dropped to the \$4-\$6.98 range. Its explanations in many of the near-tie trials often got the arithmetic wrong, stating that the chosen option was one cent cheaper when it was actually one cent more expensive.

#### ***Study 5 Results***

Study 5 tested the machine buyers’ WTP for cues that sellers use to make a product appear more appealing without delivering higher objective quality. These cues played a significant role in determining machine buyers’ choices when two options were priced the same. For instance, the option with 23 more reviews was chosen in 321 of 340 trials where price was tied, and the option with the scarcity message was avoided, being chosen in only 77 of 340 trials. The embellished description made no difference at all. The machine buyers chose the first-listed option, as they had in Studies 1 and 2, and in 174 of 340 trials (just over 50%), it happened to have an embellished description. Many machine buyers explained that the two products were identical and that they had chosen the first one listed, thus placing no value on the embellished description.

When prices differed between the two options, most machine buyers ignored these cues. Table 3 reports the percentage of trials with a price difference in which the cue was preferred over the lower price for each of the 17 machine buyers.

[Embedded content: datawrapper.dwcdn.net](https://datawrapper.dwcdn.net/z9GoC/1/)

The table shows that 8 machine buyers never let any of the three cues outweigh a price difference of 50 cents or more, and two more did so in less than 5% of the trials. This is consistent with my preregistered prediction that most machine buyers would pay little, if anything, for cues that create meaningless differentiation. It’s worth noting that the smallest price step was 50 cents, so some machine buyers may have valued these cues at less than 50 cents (and greater than zero).

The table also shows that a few machine buyers did pay for these cues. When the option with the embellished description was listed first, gpt-oss-120b chose it in 6 of 10 trials even when it cost \$8 more, and its switch point in that case was a healthy \$12.48. (Not bad for one flowery word or phrase!) GPT-6 Sol paid for more reviews, revealing a premium of \$1.57 for an option with 23 more reviews listed first, and explaining that it chose this because its listing had “slightly more customer reviews supporting their 4.4-star rating, making them worth the extra \$2.” The scarcity message penalized the seller with several machine buyers. Claude Haiku required a discount of approximately \$0.89 (\$0.69 with thinking off) before selecting the option labeled “Only 3 left in stock.” When the option was listed second, gpt-oss-120b and GPT-6 Luna, with reasoning off, required discounts of \$3.23 and \$4.12, respectively.

#### ***Study 6 Results***

The goal of Study 6 was to examine WTP for features that have real, subjective value for machine buyers. There were four qualitative features considered: 4.7-star vs. 4.4-star ratings, a 2-year warranty vs. a 1-year warranty, a Sponsored label, and a badge saying “Editor’s Pick.” Table 4 shows how much each of the 17 machine buyers valued these features.

[Embedded content: datawrapper.dwcdn.net](https://datawrapper.dwcdn.net/IGbqJ/1/)

As the table shows, there’s quite a bit of variability between the WTPs of the 17 machine buyers for these subjective value attributes. A 0.3-star higher rating was worth \$14 to Gemini 4 Argon and \$15 to GPT-6 Astra, but \$47 to Claude Sonnet 5.5. On an item that costs \$80, that translates to a premium difference of 17.5% to 59% of the total price. Obviously, I don’t have data on how much human buyers would value this 3/10-of-a-star rating, but my guess is not as much. The valuation of the 2-year warranty was more consistent among machine buyers, with 9 of the 17 valuing the extra warranty between \$4 and \$15. But there were outliers here also; gpt-oss-120b valued it at more than \$42, and Gemini 3.5 Flash-lite (default) valued it at more than \$35. Finally, most machine buyers valued the Editor’s pick badge at less than \$1, but here again, gpt-oss-120b seemed very impressed by the badge, valuing it at \$23, while our friend Gemini 3.5 Flash-lite (default) valued it at more than \$21.

When two options cost the same, the machine buyers chose the product with the Editor’s pick badge in 308 of 340 trials, but when the option with the badge cost 50 cents more (and was comparable in all other respects), they chose it in just 70 of 340 trials. Six of the machine buyers (GPT-6 Asta, GPT-6.1 Sol, Claude Opus 5.5, Claude Sonnet 5.5 with and without thinking, and Gemini 4 Argon) never valued any of the cues at more than 50 cents. Their explanations were explicit. For instance, GPT-6 Astra said that at equal prices, “the Editor’s pick label gives the Kestrel Buds Air the tiebreaker,” and Claude Opus 5.5 said that even at equal prices, the badge “adds no concrete information.” This pattern resembles the use of a lexicographic decision rule, in which the decision maker compares options on the most important attribute first, considering a second attribute only if the options are tied on the first one. For these six machine buyers, price was the first attribute, and badges, labels, embellished descriptions, etc., only came into play when prices were equal or very close to equal.

#### ***The value of first position in the list***

In Studies 4-6, every price gap was tested in both listing positions. Consequently, I could also measure the value of being listed first to each machine buyer. Table 5 shows the dollar value of being listed first for each feature across the three studies, for each of the 17 machine buyers.

### **Practical Pricing Strategies for Selling to Machine Buyers**

The studies reported in this piece underscore the importance of shifting our mindset from a default “my buyers are human, and I know a lot about their psychology and can use it for pricing” to one that is humbler, understands, and caters to machine buyers. One key thing for every seller, whether a brand or a retailer, is to find out which AI assistants their customers use and to test what those machine buyers will pay for the features that distinguish their products. In the studies I’ve done so far, I have deliberately stayed away from existing brands, but an obvious, important question is how (if at all) machine buyers are valuing current brands. I said in my previous piece that behavioral pricing tactics may be on life support, and it would be equally important to find out whether brands are also on life support in a machine-buyer-dominated world.

In Study 6 here, moving from a 4.4-star to a 4.7-star rating on an \$80 product was worth approximately \$14 to Gemini 4 Argon and \$47 to Claude Sonnet 5.5. A seller charging a \$25 premium for the higher rating would therefore face very different buying responses from these two machine buyers. The same issue applies to warranties. There is no single premium for a better product that all machine buyers will accept (or reject). Sellers will need to measure these valuations and consider the mix of machine buyers they serve when setting prices. I am being speculative here, but it’s quite likely that not all of these AI models will be equally popular among consumers as machine buyers; eventually, the top 2, 3, or 4 AI agents will dominate, making it more manageable for sellers.

These results also suggest being selective about investments in differentiation. Higher ratings and longer warranties supported substantial premiums in the studies, while things like badges, embellished descriptions, and small differences in review counts often helped only when prices were tied. All of this is to say that in the previous piece and this one, a clear theme privileging the price over all other features seems to be emerging.

Another implication is that sellers should learn from machine buyers’ choices rather than what they say (and this advice is not that different for human buyers). In Study 7, every machine buyer said that list position was worth nothing, even though some had paid considerable amounts for it. Asking a machine buyer what it would pay is therefore a poor substitute for presenting competing offers and observing what it chooses, and when it switches its choice. Sellers can use such tests to evaluate feature premiums, shipping fees, and listing positions with their models and settings that customers actually use, and repeat them after model updates.

The findings here also show that when options are comparable, matching a competitor’s price may allow things like an Editor’s pick badge, or additional reviews to be the deciding factor in the buyer’s choice, while a lower price may override such cues. But note that in the studies reported here, my smallest price difference was 50 cents, so these findings cannot ascertain what happens at smaller price differences. Showing an all-inclusive price also merits consideration (as Study 2 in the previous piece and Studies 3 and 4 here showed), given the arithmetic errors some machine buyers made when shipping was priced separately.

### **Conclusion**

How much will a machine buyer pay for a better product? My results suggest that the answer depends on which machine buyer is doing the buying and on what makes the product better. Most AI models valued free shipping at precisely its quoted price in the alternative option, but their valuations of more subjective-value features like higher ratings and longer warranties varied considerably. Their stated valuations provided little guidance about their actual choices or WTPs. In this respect, machine buyers pose the same challenge as human buyers: WTP measurements should focus on actual choices and purchases rather than stated amounts or purchase intentions. But there is one added complication. The machine buyer’s valuation is supposed to, or ought to, reflect the valuation of the human on whose behalf it is acting. Whether its valuation actually matches human preferences and WTP, and whether it continues to do so after the next AI model update, are important questions that will need to be answered.
