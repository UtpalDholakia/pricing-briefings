##### ***Summary.*** This post examines how customer consent works in subscription auto-renewal settings and how it erodes over time. It considers consent in four key stages in the subscriber’s journey (the initial sign-up, the end of the introductory offer, the recurring charges, and cancellation), considers the limitations of the company’s “we disclosed it” defense, reviews relevant federal and state regulations, and discusses how surveys can establish what subscribers actually understood.

##### ***“After the lapse of ten years, no careful person of ordinary memory, whose business was somewhat extensive, could speak positively of a transaction of small pecuniary moment, which had not been recalled to his mind.”***

##### — *Chief Judge Hiram Denio, New York Court of Appeals (1860s)*

### **Introduction[^1]**

In an [earlier post](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/the-legal-risk-for-retailers-in-a), I explored the gap in the meaning of a strikethrough price to a retailer and a shopper.[^2] The retailer thinks of the strikethrough price label as one thing (e.g., an internal accounting benchmark or a manufacturer’s suggestion), while shoppers interpret it as something else (information about the product’s normal price and that they are getting a deal). Over the past decade, consumer protection law has increasingly sided with the shopper’s interpretation, especially when the retailer’s internal account of the label’s meaning is not explicitly communicated to shoppers.

The customer’s journey through subscription pricing settings also exhibits a similar dissonance. Here, the core issue is about how the customer’s consent works and when it expires. The subscription company’s perspective is that the customer gives consent as soon as they click the “Accept” button, and their consent remains in effect indefinitely, until the customer actively revokes it. For customers, however, their consent was given at a specific moment when subscribing, for a specific decision, based on their understanding at the time (and possibly their heightened enthusiasm). However, as time passes, their memory of this moment fades, and the logic, information, and mindset that led them to sign up may no longer apply. Thus, rather than being indefinite, consent erodes over time.

### **Recurring revenue from customers forgetting**

Many customers sign up for a subscription in the heat of a buying impulse or for a specific, narrow goal and, over time, forget to cancel it, or even that they have it. The phenomenon of forgetting is one of the oldest areas of psychological research and also one of the most robust principles of human memory, with over a century of research findings.[^3] Relevant to us, this research has found that memory decay is particularly significant for prospective memory, which involves remembering to perform actions in the future, such as canceling a free trial before the trial period runs out.[^4]

Thus, a company using an autorenewal subscription pricing model that doesn’t engage them or actively refresh customers’ memory periodically is, in effect, building its recurring revenue stream on a foundation of customer forgetting. In effect, they are turning a single moment of customer consent located in a very specific, unique buying context into many months (or even years) of customer payments. Lawmakers and regulators have started to appreciate the significance of forgetting, and especially how the erosion of consent can undermine consumer welfare.[^5]

Consider the following common CX scenario. A consumer named Maria is really interested in watching a recently released miniseries on a specific streaming platform. In January, she visits the platform’s sign-up webpage and sees a prominent banner: “Start your 7-day free trial.” She enters her email address and credit card details, then clicks *Start Watching.* A small block of gray text below the button, that is less than half the size of the headline, stipulates that her trial will automatically convert to a \$14.99 monthly subscription unless she cancels at least 24 hours before the trial ends, that the regular price after a three-month introductory offer will be \$19.99 per month, and that cancellation must be initiated through the account settings page on the website rather than through the mobile app. She binge-watches the series over a single weekend (during the trial period), uninstalls the app from her iPad, and promptly forgets about the platform and her subscription.

Months later, in October, when going through her credit card statement, Maria notices a recurring \$19.99 charge she does not immediately recognize. Then she remembers she signed up for the service in January and tries to cancel it through the company’s mobile app, only to be told she must cancel on the website instead. On the website, she clicks *Cancel,* is taken through a series of three retention screens with different offers, two confirmation steps, and a final offer of two months at half price, before she can cancel. In a follow-up call, the customer service rep refuses to refund Maria the nine months of recurring charges.

At least three significant changes occurred during Maria’s journey over this period: (1) the price she paid increased from free to \$14.99 after a week and to \$19.99 after three months, (2) Maria’s memory of the enrollment faded quickly, and she essentially stopped thinking about the platform after finishing the series she wanted to see, and (3) Maria quickly transitioned from active enrollee and viewer to passive subscriber and to inactive (we might even say, oblivious) payer over this period. While none of these changes individually was a breach of the company’s terms of service, taken together, they meant that Maria’s original “Start Watching” click in January was not an accurate representation of what she would have agreed to in October. Somewhere in that journey, Maria’s original consent became behaviorally detached from her current understanding, expectations, and willingness to continue paying.

### **Consent as an “on/off” switch vs. consent as a contextualized moment**

At the center of legal subscription disputes is a basic disagreement about how the subscriber’s consent works. Many companies tend to think of it as a switch. The switch goes on when the customer clicks and stays on until they switch it off. In this framework, the length of the customer’s relationship, the extent of use of the service, that is, whether the customer partook of what they were paying for, whether they remembered they had subscribed, or the total cumulative charges accrued, are irrelevant, because the switch was still on.

Not surprisingly, customers have a very different idea. From their perspective, their consent is tied to a specific moment (the click) and to the beliefs they held at that moment. These might include beliefs about the trial, the low price, the features and benefits, their prospective intention to consume these things, the perceived ease of the cancellation process, and expectations about their relationship with this service provider. As these beliefs fade from memory or change (e.g., the price increases after the introductory period) and are replaced by other beliefs that contradict them, the customer’s consent, which was founded on them, weakens. In Maria’s case, the “Click to Watch” in January is far removed from the charge on her credit card bill in October. The click was by a Maria who knew what she was doing. The October reaction of surprise was from Maria, who didn’t remember her January click.

In my piece on the [legal risk of a strikethrough piece](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/the-legal-risk-for-retailers-in-a), there was a similar gap between the retailer’s and shopper’s accounts of a single price label. The retailer saw the strikethrough price label as an internal accounting benchmark, whereas the shopper saw it as a claim about what the product was normally worth. Both accounts were internally consistent, but the gap arose because the two parties used different “price vocabularies.”[^6] The same logic applies here, but the gap arises from different perspectives on time rather than on meaning. Companies see the click as an indefinite authorization by the customer to charge the price, implicitly assuming that the customer’s valuation and intentions remain unchanged. For customers, the consent to charge is attached to a particular moment in time, and as time passes, that consent loses validity and decays like memory. The recurring legal question is how much weight the law should give to the original click when the customer’s later understanding, memory, use, and cancellation experience have materially diverged from the company’s account of the transaction.

### **Consent erosion during the subscription customer journey**

Considering the subscription customer’s journey through a journey-mapping lens helps understand the fading of consent. For our purposes, the customer journey begins with the initial click on the website or app button that starts the subscription and ends when the customer finally cancels their subscription on the company’s website. As this journey unfolds, the customer’s understanding of what they signed on for, and even the fact that they actually signed on, drifts further and further away from the subscription company’s immutable view of consent. This drift is most significant at four points in the journey.

Consent erosion during the subscription customer journey

#### ***1)***  ***The sign-up***

This is the first relevant step of the subscriber’s journey. Their decision to start a subscription may be triggered by any number of reasons, from wanting to see a specific show or series (as in Maria’s case) to a broader interest in having content to watch, to unwind or be entertained, and so on. For some consumers, it might be the call to action of a free trial that pushes them to sign up. What is important here is how the company and the customers see the act of signing on. For the customer, signing up means committing to a free trial to experience the content and the service. For the company, it means the customer has consented to all the stated terms of service on the page. One conflict is that many consumers may not pay attention to the specific terms of the offer listed at the sign-up location (e.g., they will have to start paying a recurring fee after the free trial, the price may increase after a certain period, or the subscription will auto-renew).[^7] Given that these events will occur in the future, the customer may resolve to address them then. Some may cancel the subscription before the free trial expires; others may give the service a chance and decide whether to remain a subscriber; and still others may be committed to a longer-term subscription. In a nutshell, the subscription company treats the sign-up click as indefinite consent, whereas the consumer treats it as a sequential decision to be reserved for the future, and perhaps revisited repeatedly after gauging the value of their ongoing consumption.

#### ***2)***  ***The end of the introductory offer***

The end of the introductory offer is the second significant step in the subscriber’s journey. This is when the service price increases from the introductory price to a higher price. (In Maria’s example, it happened twice, from \$0 to \$14.99 after a week, and from \$14.99 to \$19.99 after three months.) The customer is now being charged a price that was not explicitly shown on the sign-up button but was displayed much less prominently. We might say that the introductory price took center stage, and the subscriber may not even know that this higher price is what they agreed to when they signed on. Even for those who paid attention, their present-biased decision making may have given less weight to the higher future price[^8] or seen it as a contingent feature of a future state that they expected to revisit before the price went up, and forgot to do so.

#### ***3)***  ***Paying the recurring charge***

The subscription journey is open-ended, and customers who don’t review their automated payment charges may remain inactive for months or even years, having forgotten they are subscribed to the service. Research on subscriptions has found that consumers substantially overestimate their own subsequent attention to cancellation decisions and consequently pay for periods over which the service has, in their actual experience, ceased to matter to them.[^9] From the company’s perspective, this is a non-issue. The customer provided their consent, and forgetting it does not revoke that consent. From the customer’s perspective, however, the initial consent was specific, narrowly defined, and deliberate, and has faded away. This is why many state automatic-renewal laws increasingly require periodic renewal notices, so that the subscriber can refresh (or not) their consent to remain subscribed.

#### ***4)***  ***Cancellation***

The final stage of the journey is when the customer leaves their inactive state and realizes they are still subscribed. Any number of trigger points may cause this, from a careful check of their credit or debit card bill to a conversation with a friend, an email from the subscription company, or even signing up for a service that finds inactive subscriptions on an individual’s behalf. For many companies, the cancellation process is onerous, with several hurdles and “friction points” put in place. For instance, a customer may not be able to unsubscribe with an app and may have to visit the company’s website. Even once they reach the cancellation section of the website, they may have to go through several so-called retention screens that present different offers and reasons not to cancel. In some cases, the only way to cancel may be by phone during business hours.[^10] Returning to consent, when signing up, a customer may have expected (reasonably so) that it would be just as convenient and easy to cancel the service. Being subjected to hurdles was not part of the expectation. State and federal laws increasingly treat this easy sign-up–difficult cancellation strategy as a misrepresentation of what customers consented to at sign-up.

Taken together, these four stages of the subscription customer’s journey reveal how the initial moment of consent eventually becomes detached from the customer’s experience (or more accurately, non-experience) with the subscription service. The Maria who clicked *Start Watching* in January is not, in any practical sense, the same Maria trying to cancel after discovering all the monthly charges she’s paid to the company in October.

### **The limitations of the “we disclosed it” defense**

When a subscription dispute reaches litigation, the subscription company’s defense is often that all of the material terms of the subscription were disclosed on the enrollment webpage and that the consumer agreed to them when they clicked the sign-up button. Furthermore, the terms there included automatic renewal (“Your membership will continue and automatically renew until terminated”). Thus, this defense hinges on the consent-as-switch model described earlier, in which the customer turns the consent switch on, and it remains on (i.e., automatic renewal) until the customer turns it off.

However, there are several significant limitations with this defense when the psychology behind the subscriber’s journey is taken into account. First, consumer protection statutes apply a reasonable consumer standard based on what an ordinary consumer would have understood at the moment in question.[^11] What’s more, the moment in question is not always defined as the moment of the initial sign-up click. It may be any of the moments in the journey explored earlier, or even others, such as the point at which a free trial ended and the customer made the first payment to the company. A defense that addresses only the moment of the first click leaves the other moments of the subscriber’s journey uncontested.

Second, the company’s subscription sign-up page discloses details such as the duration of the free trial, the future higher price or prices, and the need to actively cancel the subscription. But it often fails to disclose salient aspects of the customer’s experience, such as that they will not receive a reminder before the transition to the higher price, that their engagement may drop off rapidly, or that the cancellation procedure may have constraints and frictions that will require more effort than the enrollment. Important to note, some of these features may be described elsewhere on the page, some may appear in the linked terms of service, and some may not be provided anywhere. To the extent that the consumer’s consent is based on this missing or hard-to-find information, the disclosure defense is vulnerable.

The third issue with the subscription company’s defense is context-specific, customer-centric, and psychological. Once the inquiry shifts from what the page said to what the consumer understood and when, the company’s website and behavioral records are no longer sufficient. It becomes a question about the customer’s mental state at one or more specific times during the journey, based on evidence of consumer perception, not on testimony about the subscription company’s intent. In the [strikethrough pricing piece](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/the-legal-risk-for-retailers-in-a), I described how the JCPenney litigation illustrated this dynamic in retail pricing. In that case, internal documents revealed that the company had essentially not sold any products at the regular price during the relevant period, and the company’s former chief executive had been recorded telling investors that “nothing was bought at regular price.”[^12]

A similar pattern also occurs in subscription cancellation. In *FTC v. Amazon.com,* filed in 2023, for example, the complaint cited internal documents in which Amazon employees described the Prime enrollment process as designed to obscure the conversion to a paid membership, and the process of cancellation (internally referred to by project names suggesting an elaborate maze) as designed to deter cancellation. The case was later settled in 2025, with Amazon agreeing to monetary relief and changes to its Prime enrollment and cancellation practices.[^13] In *FTC v. Adobe,* filed in 2024, the complaint alleged that Adobe’s Annual Paid Monthly plan concealed the size of the early-termination fee at sign-up and made cancellation difficult.[^14] In both cases, the company’s position that “the terms were disclosed” ran into discovery about what the enrollment was actually designed to communicate, what the cancellation process was intended to deter, and what consumers actually took away from each. The focus shifted from the literal content of the disclosure to the design of the customer experience.

### **The legal response to consent erosion during the subscriber’s journey**

Once the underlying problem is framed in terms of consent erosion along the subscriber’s journey, federal and state law can be understood as a set of responses targeted to specific moments of erosion along that journey. The main federal statute is ROSCA, the Restore Online Shoppers’ Confidence Act. ROSCA prohibits sellers from charging consumers in an internet transaction through a negative-option feature unless the seller clearly and conspicuously discloses all material terms before obtaining billing information, obtains the consumer’s express informed consent before charging the consumer’s financial account, and provides simple mechanisms for the consumer to stop recurring charges.

Until July 2025, the FTC’s amended Negative Option Rule, commonly referred to as the Click-to-Cancel rule, appeared likely to add more granular federal requirements for negative-option marketing, including the principle that cancellation should be at least as easy as enrollment. The FTC finalized the rule in October 2024. On July 8, 2025, however, the Eighth Circuit vacated the rule on procedural grounds under the Magnuson-Moss rulemaking requirements. The FTC has since restarted the process through an advance notice of proposed rulemaking on negative-option marketing. As of this writing, the details of any replacement rule remain uncertain.[^15] The underlying principle of the vacated rule, that the cancellation experience for the subscriber cannot be materially more onerous than the enrollment experience, continues to be applied by the FTC under ROSCA and Section 5 enforcement.

At the state level, California’s Automatic Renewal Law (ARL, most recently amended in 2024) requires clear and conspicuous disclosure of automatic renewal terms, affirmative consent to those terms by the consumer, post-enrollment acknowledgment, advance notice of renewal in many circumstances, specific and separate disclosure of pricing changes, and a one-click or comparable online cancellation mechanism for consumers who enrolled online.[^16] New York, Massachusetts, Oregon, Colorado, Connecticut, and other states have enacted similar statutes that vary in detail but have the same broad requirements.[^17] It is noteworthy that components of these state laws address specific subscriber journey points covered earlier. For instance, the post-enrollment acknowledgment addresses the subscriber’s memory of the original consent, the advance notice of renewal addresses the issue of forgetting and passive payment, the price-change disclosure encourages the consumer to think about consent at the crucial journey point of price change, and the online cancellation requirement addresses the asymmetry in ease of signing up versus difficulty of canceling.

The FTC’s 2022 enforcement policy statement regarding negative option marketing and its application has reinforced the idea that the agency treats the overall enrollment-to-cancellation experience as the relevant unit of analysis rather than specific customer journey steps.[^18] For companies that operate nationally, the practical effect of these laws is that they aim to comply with the strictest applicable state law in their service enrollment and cancellation processes. For instance, at the moment, California’s amended ARL is widely treated as the binding constraint for this purpose.[^19]

### **Establishing what consumers understood during the subscription journey and when**

Just as in strikethrough pricing disputes, the legally relevant question in subscription lawsuits is what reasonable consumers actually understood from the specific subscription context.[^20] Here, the temporal dimension takes center stage, and the survey may need to establish not just what the consumer understood at the time of sign-up but what they understood at the point when price(s) changed and at attempted cancellation, because each moment may be independently consequential. Five best practice principles from the survey research literature are relevant here.

***Choose the right population for the study and identify the right moment.*** In a subscription lawsuit, the relevant universe is consumers who could reasonably have been exposed to the enrollment process at issue. These may be recent enrollees of the company, recent visitors to the enrollment page, or, in some cases, consumers who attempted to cancel their subscription. A survey of recent enrollees may study their consent at the moment of the click by having them attend to the terms of service or test their knowledge of the terms beyond the initial trial, a survey of subscribers who have experienced the price increase may study the consent at the price change, and a survey of attempted cancellers may study the consent at exit. In each case, dependent measures should assess what respondents understood, expected, and believed they had agreed to at each stage of the journey.

***Use realistic stimuli of the enrollment process.*** The survey stimuli should replicate the actual subscriber journey as closely as possible. This would include making sure the sign-up page, the wording and placement of disclosures, the location and styling of the call-to-action, the contextual elements (the brand, the marketing claims, the offer framing) are all matched as closely as possible to the actual design elements.

***Ask about the consumer’s inferences, not a legal conclusion.*** Questions like “What did you understand would happen at the end of the seven-day trial?” or “What did you expect the cancellation process to look like?” are preferred to questions such as “Was the disclosure misleading?” or “Was the enrollment process deceptive?” The former ask for the consumer’s perceptions of the CX, whereas the latter ask the respondent to render a legal opinion that the trier of fact is supposed to reach.[^21]

***Use a control where feasible.*** A well-designed survey randomly assigns respondents to the actual enrollment process and to a control process in which the design feature at issue is altered. For instance, the disclosure may be made more prominent, the auto-renewal feature may be removed, or the cancellation process may be made identical to the enrollment process, while holding other features constant. The difference between conditions can be attributed to the manipulated feature, and an accurate sense of the specific feature’s role in affecting subscription decisions and perceptions of the subscription experience can be derived.

***Augment survey data with marketplace evidence.*** While a well-designed consumer survey can establish what respondents took away from the subscription process, market evidence establishes the representativeness of these inferences for actual subscriber behavior. Depending on the case, useful sources may include the subscription company’s own data on cancellation timing (i.e., how many subscribers cancel before the free trial ends vs. after, or before the price hike to regular price vs. after), customer service complaint patterns (how frequently consumers describe themselves as having been “charged after a free trial” or “unable to cancel”), social media and review-site descriptions of consumer experience, and chargeback and dispute records from payment processors. Where survey and marketplace evidence converge, our confidence in our understanding of subscribers’ understanding is strengthened.

### **Implications for the different constituents**

I will conclude this post by considering the implications of these issues for the different constituents. Starting with the subscription service providers, the major practical implication is that the customer’s consent needs to be thought of more broadly and with greater nuance, encompassing the entire customer journey, from enrollment through price changes and ongoing engagement to ultimately, cancellation. A business strategy that encourages the customer to sign up and then doesn’t do much to actively engage them and support their use of the service, or at least have them periodically acknowledge they know they are still subscribed to the service, will be increasingly difficult to defend in potential lawsuits.

For lawyers and expert witnesses working in this space, the core implication is methodological, echoing the findings of other deceptive pricing cases. Testimony from the subscription company’s side about what the company disclosed is incomplete unless paired with evidence about what reasonable consumers actually understood, and that question can be answered through properly designed consumer surveys, and combined with any available marketplace evidence on measures such as timing of cancellations, complaint patterns, and consumer reviews, and informed by the academic research on attention to disclosed contract terms, present-biased decision-making, and the consumer psychology of default rules. The expert who works on consumer perception in subscription disputes is tasked with empirically establishing what reasonable consumers actually understood and distinguishing that from what the defendant claims was disclosed.

For consumers, the implication is somewhat pessimistic (again, just like in the [strikethrough price label discussion](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/the-legal-risk-for-retailers-in-a)). In subscription pricing models, the CX is designed to favor a longer revenue stream for the company, often at the subscriber’s expense. For example, in many setups, the process of subscribing through a digital channel is optimized to minimize friction at sign-up, and to increase it at cancellation, and recurring billing is set up to fade into the background of the customer’s overall financial life so that it goes unheeded. Customer inaction is often valued over action, encouraging passivity and the non-consumption of benefits to which subscribers are entitled. Consumers must act deliberately and persistently to counter this default psychology by nudging themselves to use subscriptions they value and to get rid of ones they don’t, without hesitation. Adopting practices such as setting calendar reminders when signing up for a new subscription, carefully reviewing bank and credit card statements every month, and using virtual card numbers or merchant-locked cards for free-trial sign-ups may be useful. As a century of cognitive psychology has found, forgetting is hard to overcome by trying harder to remember.

### **A note on legal risks of strikethrough pricing and subscription pricing**

The [strikethrough price label piece](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/the-legal-risk-for-retailers-in-a) and this one about the subscription pricing journey are, in the end, about the same pricing issue in different settings. In both cases, a particular price structure yields internally coherent but externally conflicting accounts from the seller and the customer about what was communicated and what was agreed to. They conflict because the two parties are operating from different vocabularies: price inference for strikethrough prices and temporal subscriber engagement here. For both practices, laws and regulations resolve the conflicts by focusing on what a reasonable consumer actually understood. They both ask how much of the seller’s documented claims regarding the relationship the law should recognize when the consumer’s experienced account differs substantially. With the widespread adoption of subscription pricing models for new AI-based services, this issue is likely to become even more important in the future.
