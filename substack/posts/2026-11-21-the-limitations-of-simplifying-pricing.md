##### ***Summary.*** *In February 2024, the auction house Sotheby’s reduced its buyer’s premium and replaced negotiated sales commissions with a published non-negotiable price schedule. Less than a year later, it reversed course, admitting that the new price structure had “proved less attractive to potential sellers.” In this piece, I argue that the core challenge of Sotheby’s revised price structure was its attempt to replace customized pricing with a simpler, standardized version. On the face of it, the auction house had a compelling argument, since a lower buyer’s premium can attract bidders and support higher final prices. However, when a seller compares expected outcomes across auction houses, a fixed schedule cannot accommodate differences among consignments, sellers, and competing offers that do far better with the opportunity to negotiate. The Sotheby’s case provides an excellent example of my enduring thesis that overly simple pricing structures often hurt buyers and sellers, especially in two-sided markets.*

“Explanations exist; they have existed for all time; there is always a well-known solution to every human problem — neat, plausible, and wrong.” - **H.L. Mencken,** ***The Divine Afflatus*** **(1917).**

In November 2024, two paintings sold in New York at two different auction houses, each for the same staggering price of \$59 million. After accounting for buyers’ premiums, however, the prices paid by the respective buyers were different. The Sotheby’s sale was of Claude Monet’s Nymphéas, at a final price of \$65.5 million, while Christie’s sold Ed Ruscha’s modern artwork Standard Station for \$68.3 million. The buyer benefited quite a bit at Sotheby’s, [paying \$2.8 million less](https://www.theartnewspaper.com/2025/01/23/comment-sothebys-fees-fiasco-displays-terrible-timing-and-a-miscalculation-of-market-dynamics) for the same winning bid amount. One would think Sotheby’s customer-centricity would pay off. But no.

Just a month later, Sotheby’s [CEO Charles Stewart announced](https://www.theartnewspaper.com/2024/12/19/sothebys-u-turn-on-new-fees-structure) that the new seller terms it had implemented less than a year earlier had “proved less attractive to potential sellers,” such as “people used to consigning and those with art advisers.” He added that the market had a strong preference for the status quo price structure, which was notably more complicated than the one Sotheby’s had instituted. By February 2025, Sotheby’s had abandoned its simpler structure in favor of a more conventional price structure based on negotiated seller terms and higher, graduated buyer premiums.

#### **Sotheby’s simpler price structure**

On February 1, 2024, Sotheby’s announced that it was substantially simplifying and reducing the buyer’s premium it charged auction winners. The buyer’s premium is the fee paid by the winning bidder and is typically calculated as a percentage of the winning bid. Before the change, the auction house charged 26% up to \$1 million, 20% between \$1 million and \$4.5 million, and 13.9% above that, plus a 1% so-called “overhead premium” to cover its operating costs. After the change, Sotheby’s [reduced and simplified](https://www.artnews.com/art-news/news/sothebys-overhauls-fee-structure-lowers-buyers-premium-1234694742/) the buyer’s premium to a flat rate of 20%.

Sotheby’s also simplified its price structure for sellers, but it was still more complex than the one for buyers. Its [revised structure](https://www.artsandcollections.com/sothebys-announces-new-fees-structure/), which applied to consignment contracts signed after April 14, 2024, can be characterized as follows (using Sotheby’s own descriptions where available):

- Sellers paid “10% on the first \$500,000 of the hammer price per lot,” which meant they paid a maximum of \$50,000 on each lot.

- The company waived the commission “for consignments valued above \$5 million low estimate.”

- For “consignments with a total low estimate in excess of \$20 million and up to and including \$50 million,” sellers received 40% of the buyer’s premium (which counterbalanced the commission, and could even result in a net gain, see figure below).

- Consignments expected to bring more than \$50 million remained bespoke.

- A 2% success fee applied to lots selling above their high estimate.

- Guaranteed lots were excluded from this price structure. Their terms remained negotiable, with a new fee of 4% of the guarantee amount.

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/f3708872-0a64-4e8e-950b-b7e9b74cf774_1800x920.png)

As the figure shows, using only Sotheby’s published pricing schedule, sellers paid the same or less (and even gained substantially) after the 2024 price change. However, the one thing that the figure doesn’t account for is negotiated pricing terms. A key way Sotheby’s simplified its pricing structure was to eliminate negotiated prices from most of its transactions, leaving them in place only for guaranteed lots and the very largest consignments, such as the Monet painting.

Thus, while the average buyer and seller did better under its new simplified pricing strategy, it seems clear that certain groups, such as collectors with consignments below \$5 million who had negotiated reduced or zero commissions before, and sellers of larger consignments who had received a larger share of the buyer’s premium than stipulated by the new fixed rate, earned less money. This maps onto CEO Charles Stewart’s assertions that experienced sellers resisted this seemingly benign price change.

#### **The logic behind Sotheby’s price change**

The pricing context behind the price change is worth considering briefly. At the time, Sotheby’s was facing financial pressure. Its EBITDA was down 88% year-over-year during the first half of 2024, and the [company was concerned](https://www.theartnewspaper.com/2024/04/03/sothebys-new-fee-structure-a-highwire-act-of-risk-and-reward) that it was “giving too much away to sellers” through the negotiation process. Simplifying and standardizing prices made sense, with [the rationale that](https://www.axios.com/2024/02/01/sothebys-auction-pricing-structure-commission):

“The reduction in the Buyer’s Premium will incentivize more bidding from clients and result in higher hammer prices for sellers. The standardization of selling terms pivots away from private negotiations, prioritizing transparency and simplicity.”

Sotheby’s is a two-sided market in which both buyers and sellers must be considered when setting prices and evaluating pricing strategy. At first blush, research on two-sided markets actually endorses Sotheby’s pricing logic. Suppose every bidder had a total budget in mind that they wanted to spend (essentially a WTP, matched by an ATP, or an ability to pay). And suppose that, with this total budget, the buyer lowered or raised their bids to offset the change in the buyer’s premium they’re paying, and that the set of bidders remained the same. Under such a scenario, moving fees from buyers to sellers [would have little effect](https://publications.ut-capitole.fr/1207/1/2sided_markets.pdf); buyers would simply bid higher under a lower premium, and sellers would earn about the same amount of money. The shift only matters when bidders do not fully offset the fee reduction, or when the fee affects which buyers participate in the auction.

[Research on platform pricing](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/4324/1/competitionintwosidedmarkets.pdf) shows that a platform sets each side’s price based on how much value that side generates for the other. In the Sotheby’s case, buyers create value for sellers because each additional serious bidder in an auction raises the seller’s expected price. Thus, charging bidders less will ultimately benefit sellers. There’s also some empirical evidence supporting this idea. For instance, a [2025 study](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1086/734722) of an online wine auction platform found that some sellers can be better off even when they pay higher fees to sell their wines; [charging sellers more](https://conference.nber.org/conf_papers/f176607/f176607.pdf) and using the proceeds to subsidize bidders left everyone better off because the subsidy attracted more buyers.

The logic behind this research also provides two reasons why Sotheby’s fee cut may have failed to accomplish its goals. First, a subsidy received by every participating bidder is fundamentally different from a discount received by the winning bidder. The former is likely to encourage more bidders to participate, while the latter is not, because only the winning bidder benefits. Second, the pricing structure also influences which sellers participate on the platform, which in turn attracts bidders to it. Sotheby’s new fixed pricing terms were the most attractive to sellers who had previously paid standard rates, but less so to those with the bargaining power to negotiate better terms. What’s more, such power arises from things like a particularly desired artwork or a long track record of selling blockbuster pieces, things that are particularly appealing to buyers.

Finally, [platform pricing research](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/4324/1/competitionintwosidedmarkets.pdf) explains the compelling logic behind Sotheby’s previous pricing structure (which has been used in the auction house industry for decades). Consider a situation in which one group of customers uses only a single platform (“loyalists”) and another group uses several platforms (“switchers”), and platforms compete fiercely for the loyalists. They charge switchers more and pass the resulting revenue on to loyalists by offering them preferential prices. In the case of auction houses, a regular seller can be considered a loyalist, as they offer a particular painting through a single house, while serious collectors can be considered switchers, as they attend every house’s auctions and bid on lots they are interested in. Thus, it makes more sense to compete for regular sellers and reward them with revenue earned from collectors. In fact, the buyer-premium-focused pricing structure has been used by Christie’s and Sotheby’s since at least 1975 and has allowed them to [lower the commissions](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w10795/w10795.pdf) they charge sellers.

#### **Understanding the seller’s perspective on Sotheby’s old and revised pricing structure**

When considering auction houses, the seller’s main focus is on how much money they will receive for their item, net of all fees, including commissions and incentives. Both the revenue and the costs matter for this final amount. On the revenue side, this is driven by the winning bid amount, which in turn depends on the number of serious bidders and how deep their pockets are, the specific sale in which the item is positioned (i.e., the quality of the other adjacent lots), and the estimate of the item developed and advertised by the auction house. On the cost side, the seller will weigh any so-called [enhanced hammer payments](https://www.wealthmanagement.com/high-net-worth/deal-of-the-art-why-auction-houses-are-giving-away-millions) (i.e., specific negotiated incentives for larger or high-profile consignments), any guarantee and its cost, the success fee, fees for insurance, photography, and marketing, and the timing of the sale. The price components on the revenue and cost sides are compensatory, i.e., a lower commission at one house can be offset by a higher expected hammer price at another. However, when two competing auction houses have the same expected final price, the terms of the contract play a more significant role in the seller’s decision about where to sell.

Working through a simple numerical example illustrates how Sotheby’s 2024 change affected different sellers. Consider a seller selling one painting that is not guaranteed, is consigned on its own, and is selling within its estimated price. In a Sotheby’s auction (as in other houses' auctions), bidding stops when the second-highest bidder reaches their limit. Suppose the final bid is for \$1.27 million in total (including the buyer’s premium). Under the old pricing, the final hammer price would have been \$1 million, plus a \$270,000 buyer’s premium. With the new lower pricing for buyers, the \$1.27 million final price equates to a much higher hammer price of about \$1,058,000, if the bidder decides to spend all the money they’ve saved on the premium in bidding higher. However, if the bidder sticks with the original \$1 million bid, they will keep the \$70,000 in savings.

Now consider three sellers. Under the old pricing, the first paid Sotheby’s standard 10% commission. The second seller was savvier and had negotiated a zero commission, and the third was even savvier (or more powerful) and had negotiated an enhanced hammer of 5%, i.e., a payment of the hammer price plus 5%. It is worth reiterating here that enhanced hammer deals are more common for larger consignments and are usually not publicly disclosed. Under the new pricing structure, all three paid the same 10% commission. The table below shows the results for the sellers under Sotheby’s old and new structures.

The table shows that the price change resulted in all three sellers earning the same net amount under the new pricing, where they had earned significantly different amounts before, depending on their negotiated terms. Sellers who didn’t negotiate actually benefited under the new price structure, regardless of whether bidders bid the same amount as before or a higher amount. For sellers who didn’t pay commission, the outcome was dependent on the impact of the lower buyer’s premium on bidders’ propensity to bid higher. But sellers who had negotiated favorable terms, such as a 5% hammer price, invariably did much worse under the new, simpler Sotheby’s pricing strategy.

There’s a second source of variation to consider here also, resulting from the per-lot cap imposed by Sotheby’s. Because Sotheby’s capped the seller’s commission at \$50,000 per lot, its effect depended on how a consignment’s value was distributed across the lots. A single painting with a \$4 million hammer price generated a \$50,000 commission, amounting to 1.25% of the price. In contrast, 10 paintings with \$400,000 hammer prices apiece also yielded \$4 million in revenue for the seller, but generated a commission of \$400,000, or 10% of the total. Collectors consigning expensive works benefited from the price change, but those selling mid-priced works in volume, say, from an estate, were really hurt by this fixed, inflexible pricing schedule.

Third, the \$5 million threshold also had an effect. A collection of mid-priced works with a total low estimate just under \$5 million would incur several hundred thousand dollars in commission, while a similar collection that clocked in just over \$5 million would avoid this fee entirely. Those with advisors, who typically negotiated fees down, would work out these adverse possibilities and be far more reluctant to go to Sotheby’s when other auction houses were readily available.

Finally, there is the issue of perceptions of fairness to consider. One way to think of fairness here is that every buyer and every seller gets equal treatment and pays the same prices (as a percentage of their transaction). Generalizing from the Sotheby’s case, this idea of treating all customers equally is usually a significant reason for adopting or advancing simple pricing structures. However, there is a different, more nuanced way of thinking about fairness. Shouldn’t customers who generate greater revenue and profit for the auction house get more favorable treatment? With this alternative definition, fairness is offering customers economic value that corresponds to the value generated for the seller, and this view favors a more complex pricing structure that privileges higher-value customers.

Not surprisingly, the results were quite discouraging for Sotheby’s. Its 2024 auction sales fell 28%, compared with a 16% drop at Christie’s. The company also reported that its lot volume fell by 14%, and that “we simply had far more demand than supply” (translation: not enough sellers). Of course, the caveat that other unrelated factors in the pricing context could have played a role (e.g., the impending U.S. election) in these outcomes should be acknowledged.

#### **Understanding the buyer’s perspective on Sotheby’s new and revised pricing structure**

The old pricing structure charged buyers a 27% buyer’s premium on hammer prices below \$1 million (26% + 1% overhead premium). It is important to consider how lowering the price to 20% would affect the buyer’s venue choice, participation, and bidding behavior, especially given that many bidders are serious collectors who regularly bid in auctions, and some are likely to be assisted by expert advisers. Considering the venue choice first, price has little effect on bidders, since they are interested in the items being auctioned and will follow the artwork they are interested in to whichever auction house is auctioning them. Thus, while a lower buyer’s premium may attract some new buyers to Sotheby’s, the price cut is unlikely to affect most bidders’ venue choice.

Where bidding participation is concerned, a lower buyer’s premium could get some people who would otherwise not have bid to consider bidding, or keep marginal bidders in an auction for longer. In fact, this was the logic behind Sotheby’s simplification of the price structure. The company reported that artwork costing over \$15 million [attracted more bidders per lot](https://www.theartnewspaper.com/2025/01/23/sothebys-made-6bn-in-2024but-sales-dropped-for-second-consecutive-year) in 2024 than in 2023, implying that the substantially lower buyer’s premium was at least partially responsible.

As discussed earlier in this article, the biggest impact arises from the possibility that the bidder will use some or all of the money saved on the buyer’s premium to bid higher. In the art market, dealers, advisors, and seasoned collectors usually think of their purchases in terms of total cost incurred, so they are likely to adjust their bids upward when they save on the premium. Indeed, some findings from [an interesting natural experiment](https://pubs.aeaweb.org/doi/pdf/10.1257%2Fjep.3.3.23) on wine auctions also support this possibility. Back in 1986, Christie’s in London auctioned wine without a buyer’s premium in the spring but instituted a 10% premium in the fall. A comparison of wine auction prices at Sotheby’s London during this period found prices to be 12% lower than Christie’s (which wasn’t charging a premium at the time) but returning to parity in the fall when Christie started charging the same premium. Thus, bidders carefully accounted for the buyer’s premium in their bids for wine and adjusted them almost fully in line with it. However, as suggested by [research on partitioned pricing](https://www.jstor.org/stable/3152164), at least some bidders will pay insufficient attention to the buyer’s premium and will be primarily influenced by the hammer price.

Finally, there is the issue of substitution to consider. Bidders interested in one artwork can turn to other pieces by the same artist, pieces from the same school by other comparable artists, or seek out artwork to buy through private sales or dealers rather than an auction. While a unique masterpiece may have no substitutes, mid-priced artwork costing \$300,000 or \$400,000 may have many other comparable options that sell regularly. All of this is to say that Sotheby’s was dealing with a heterogeneous population of bidders. While some would be willing to pass on most or even all of their savings from the lower buyer’s premium through to their bids, others would not account for the lower premium, and still others would be drawn to bid because of it. Whether the net effect of all these responses worked out in Sotheby’s favor was not clear.

#### **Transparency and negotiability in pricing structures**

Sotheby’s simplification of its pricing structure combined two decisions that can, and perhaps should be treated separately, while paying attention to their interaction. The first was to publish its terms for sellers, thus making them freely available for every potential seller. The second was to remove negotiation from the price-setting process for sellers. I want to emphasize that the two issues are unrelated. For instance, in many B2B settings, a company publishes a standard schedule online and still negotiates exceptions with individual customers. On the flip side, it can keep its pricing completely private behind “Call us for more information” instructions, yet refuse to negotiate the terms with individual customers. Each decision is associated with costs and benefits that are worth examining.

Making the pricing freely available has obvious benefits, but also some not-so-obvious disadvantages. Sellers who are unfamiliar with auctions will be able to see exactly how much they will have to pay Sotheby’s without any need for individual conversations or briefings. However, research suggests that such transparency affects the behavior of both customers and competitors. For example, a [study of hospital purchasing](https://www.nber.org/papers/w22039) (an industry notorious for its lack of transparency) found that when hospitals gained access to information on what other hospitals paid for the same supplies, they were motivated to negotiate lower prices. The price reductions were most enjoyed by hospitals that had previously paid the highest prices. Extrapolating to the Sotheby’s case, sellers now had an easily available reference point that might not be helpful for transacting with Sotheby’s, but was very useful for going to Christie’s or another one of Sotheby’s competitors and negotiating effectively with them. Christie’s [maintained its prices](https://theartnewspaper.com/2024/12/17/christies-2024-results-down-6-percent-from-2023) and pricing structure, continuing to negotiate privately with sellers while fully aware of its main competitor’s prices.

The price negotiability decision also has its pros and cons. Research on [haggling and fixed prices](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormksc/v23y2004i2p219-233.html) has found that negotiating can be profitable for a seller when many of its customers are unwilling to negotiate. In this case, the seller can charge them the full price and offer more accommodations to those who are willing and motivated to ask. In other words, the propensity to negotiate serves as a cue to the customer’s price sensitivity, guiding the seller on what price to quote, but only when most customers lack that propensity. On the flip side, when most customers choose to negotiate, it is better (and more profitable) to have a fixed price. And finally, the same study found that if one company offers fixed prices while a competitor is willing to negotiate, the fixed-price seller will lose business to the competitor, just like the aftermath Sotheby faced after simplifying its pricing structure and doing away with negotiability. The two decisions about price transparency and price negotiability can and do reinforce each other. Transparent prices inherently disfavor negotiability, and vice versa.

Making things even more complicated for Sotheby’s, its relationship with Christie’s over the past decades has been checkered, to say the least. After the art market crashed in 1990, for example, both auction houses cut their commissions, often all the way down to zero, to win business from sellers. Then, in 1995, as shown in the table below, they adopted matching, non-negotiable commission schedules that they had secretly agreed on (in breakfast meetings between CEOs), which led to a criminal price-fixing case. Within two years, as the art market recovered, the competition for valuable artwork intensified. Sotheby’s offered to waive its commission on a prominent estate, while Christie’s responded by resuming charitable donations to sellers.

Once the aftermath of the price fixing indictment played out (with [prison time for Sotheby’s chairman](https://money.cnn.com/2002/04/22/news/taubman/index.htm), and hefty \$256 million payments by each auction house to settle a class-action lawsuit), the two companies changed their pricing structures in February 2000, never returned to a fixed selling price (until 2024). All of this is to say that in this industry, fixed prices are really difficult to pull off, and there is a lot of pressure that supports negotiability of prices on the seller side.

#### **Phillips’s conditional discount-based pricing structure**

The pricing approach used by Phillips, the third and smaller cousin of Sotheby’s and Christie’s, provides another useful data point for understanding pricing structures in this industry. Phillips is a more focused auction house than either Sotheby’s or Christie’s, concentrating on 20^(th)-century and contemporary art, photographs, and watches, but competing with the two majors for the same buyers and sellers.

When Phillips decided to cut its prices, it adopted a different approach from Sotheby’s. In Fall 2025, it [stipulated that buyers](https://www.phillips.com/article/160527128/priority-bidding-for-live-auctions) who place a written, binding bid at least 48 hours before a live auction, at or above the low estimate, will pay a lower premium if they win. In New York, for example, this [price change resulted](https://www.artnews.com/art-news/news/phillips-auction-house-new-priority-bidding-structure-1234747999/) in a 4% reduction on the first \$1 million of the hammer price, 2% between \$1 million and \$6 million, and 1% above that. Phillips did not change its pricing structure for sellers.

Where Sotheby’s discount was unconditional in the sense that every bidder received it, Phillips made it available only to bidders who committed early and could not withdraw. Essentially, it was paying buyers for valuable information because early bids indicate the extent of demand for a particular lot before the sale, reduce the risk that the lot will go unsold, and may encourage competitive bidding once the auction begins. Phillips’s CEO, Martin Wilson, [described the goal](https://www.theartnewspaper.com/2026/07/10/phillips-leans-on-luxury-watches-in-first-half-of-2026) as encouraging “early engagement in order to generate spirited bidding,” reported that early bids have almost tripled since the program was put in place, and claimed that some sales were “40% to 50% sold before the auctioneer even takes his place in the rostrum.”

#### **Sotheby’s retraction of its simplified pricing and its current price structure**

Sotheby’s [raised its buyer premiums](https://www.theartnewspaper.com/2024/12/19/sothebys-u-turn-on-new-fees-structure) in February 2025, just 10 months later, using a graduated pricing structure like before, and [again increased prices](https://www.antiquestradegazette.com/news/2026/sotheby-s-raises-buyer-s-premium-to-28-at-the-lower-tier/) in February 2026 (see Table below), without publicly giving a reason for the increase. It had returned to negotiating terms with sellers in February 2025. The idea of providing customers with greater value and simple pricing was shelved entirely. In comparison, Christie’s pricing remained unchanged (carrying over from all the way back in 2015).

#### **Lessons for pricing consultants, managers, and entrepreneurs**

The Sotheby’s case illustrates why simplicity should be treated as one criterion for evaluating a pricing structure, alongside other criteria such as its effects on customer participation and engagement, competitive positioning, revenue, and profitability. A published price schedule may be easier to explain and administer, yet still make a company’s offering less attractive to its most valuable customers. Before recommending simplification, pricing practitioners should identify the purpose of the existing complexity. Things like negotiated terms, commission schedules, and conditional incentives may reflect meaningful differences in customers’ preferences and in the economic value they generate.

In two-sided markets, this evaluation must account for how a pricing change affects both parties and their interactions. Sotheby’s, not unreasonably, expected lower buyer premiums to benefit sellers through higher bids, but the benefit depended on bidders’ behavior, which was uncertain. In contrast, the loss of flexibility from being able to negotiate was immediately apparent to affected sellers. This issue is especially important for entrepreneurs building two-sided platforms: attracting more customers on one side will accomplish little if the pricing structure used to attract them discourages desirable customers on the other side. The relevant question is how the overall pricing structure influences participation and value creation on both sides of the market.

Averages are particularly misleading in this analysis. A pricing change that appears beneficial to the average customer can be significantly harmful to customers with alternatives or who contribute disproportionately to the business. Before introducing a standardized pricing schedule, managers should compare outcomes for customers with different transaction sizes, purchase histories, and negotiated arrangements. Sotheby’s per-lot cap and consignment thresholds also demonstrate the importance of examining how apparently straightforward rules can interact and lead to unintended negative consequences for a subgroup. Customers with similar total consignment values faced having to pay starkly different commissions depending on how many lots were in their collections. Working through representative customer scenarios can reveal these cases before implementation.

Transparency and negotiability also deserve to be considered separately while accounting for their interaction. Publishing standard terms, such as list prices, can help customers understand an offer while leaving room for justified exceptions. Conversely, a company can maintain fixed prices without publishing them. Consultants should help clients evaluate each choice on its own merits and consider how competitors might respond. A transparent, inflexible price schedule gives a competitor that is open to negotiating a clear reference point for winning business. Where negotiation remains useful, managers can establish rules for exceptions based on criteria that make sense for them, such as the value of the relationship or the competitor’s interest in the customer. Such an approach will provide discipline while preserving useful flexibility.

Phillips’s approach provides another useful lesson for pricing practitioners. A discount can be designed to elicit a specific, valuable customer behavior that is geared to a meaningful financial outcome. Its lower premium specifically rewards an early, binding commitment from buyers that provides valuable information about demand and reduces the auction house’s uncertainty. Managers considering a price cut should specify the customer behavior they wish to encourage and structure the incentive in a way that does so. Furthermore, the incentive’s cost should be evaluated against the value of the customer behavior, including how much of the discount is given to customers who would have behaved in the same way anyway.

Finally, substantial changes to the pricing structure should be viewed as hypotheses to be tested. By itself, a lower fee does not demonstrate that a new structure will be effective. Managers need evidence about concrete KPIs, such as customer retention, quality of new customer accounts, engagement, realized prices, and net revenue. In a two-sided market, these measures should be attuned to both parties. Where feasible, a test or a limited introduction can help distinguish the effects of pricing from broader market changes and reveal which customers benefit and which ones are turned off. The practical lesson offered by Sotheby’s experience is that pricing simplicity should be pursued with an understanding of what might go wrong or what might be lost. An effective pricing structure is one that is understandable enough for customers to evaluate and flexible enough to accommodate relevant differences between them.
