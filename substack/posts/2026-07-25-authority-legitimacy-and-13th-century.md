##### ***Summary:*** In the [kick-off post of this series](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/from-diocletians-edict-to-netflixs?), I proposed a working hypothesis that the history of price-change communication can be traced through five accumulating vocabularies of price legitimacy: authority, virtue, necessity, mechanism, and future value. This post examines the earliest period, from approximately 1750 BCE to 1500 CE, to probe how authorities and sellers legitimized price changes to their audiences. I draw upon six cases from three continents, including Hammurabi’s Babylon, Diocletian’s Rome, Kautilya’s Mauryan India, the medieval Islamic Mediterranean, Tang and Song China, and medieval England. The coverage finds that counterintuitively, mechanism, the basis of modern algorithmic and dynamic price legitimacy, was a fully developed premodern vocabulary of price legitimacy over a thousand years before the first computer-based algorithm, and the medieval English version of mechanism-based shrinkflation was widespread, long-lived, and far more transparent than today’s CPG versions.

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/d10b0e89-c08e-46e4-bd94-b6186a80eb7d_1672x941.png)

##### ***Author’s note:*** The phrase “price-change communication” carries a modern connotation that doesn’t quite fit how things operated in antiquity and the medieval world. Then, most prices were announced through proclamations, edicts, decrees, statutes, inspections, rituals, and enforcement measures. These communications were institutionalized by being embedded in the law, the formula, the inspection routine, the punishment, and other forms rather than conveyed by written or spoken messages addressed to a particular target. Consequently, when I use the word “communication” in this post, I apply it broadly to cover these channels and the entire constituency for whom they were meant. These would have included the sellers, buyers, inspectors, rulers, guilds, artisans, farmers, laborers, merchants, religious authorities, weights-and-measures officials, pirates, smugglers, and the citizenry.

### **Stable prices, shrinking bread loaves, and full price transparency**

Imagine yourself in a market town in late thirteenth-century England, say Norwich or Bristol, on an early afternoon of a beautiful spring day. Grain prices have risen over the past few weeks due to poor harvests. You walk into the local bakery to buy your usual ha’penny loaf of wheat bread. The bread looks distinctly smaller than last time, but you are not surprised. You know exactly why it’s smaller.

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/6936dd94-7d88-479d-8d56-c2f076e8f314_1969x847.png)

Text of the Assize of Bread, Source: Internet Archive

This is because the published rule, the Assize of Bread, governed the weight of a bread loaf according to the current price of grain[^1]. When grain prices rose, the loaf shrank, and vice versa. The rule was enforced by local borough authorities. The market’s clerk was empowered to weigh bakers’ loaves on any given day, and bakers who were caught cheating faced punishment, as described in the Judicium Pillorie[^2]:

“If a Baker or a brewer be convicted, because he has not observed the Assize of Bread and Beer, the first, second, and third times, he shall be amerced according to his offence; if it be not over-grievous; but if the offence be grievous and often, and will not be corrected, then he shall suffer punishment of the body, that is to wit, a baker to the pillory, and a brewer to the tumbrel, or some other correction….First, they shall inquire the price of wheat, that is to wit, how a quarter of the best wheat was sold the last market day, and how the second wheat, and how the third; and how a quarter of barley and oats. After, how the baker’s bread does answer in his court, that is to wit, wastel and other bread after wheat of the best, or of the second, or of the third price. Also upon how much increase or decrease in the price of wheat a baker ought to change the assize and weight of his bread. Also how much the wastel of a farthing ought to weigh, and all other manner of bread, after the price of a quarter of wheat that they present. And for default in the weight of the bread, a baker ought to be amerced, or to be judged unto the pillory, according to the law and custom of the court.”

This is medieval shrinkflation at its most transparent from seven hundred years ago. The mechanism, in this case, the rule behind the price change, was public knowledge, and the price change was strictly, we might even say, ruthlessly, enforced.

The administration and transparency of the Assize of Bread (which we will examine in greater detail later in this post) illustrate a much bigger point I want to highlight throughout this post. In antiquity and the medieval world, the dominant approaches to legitimizing price changes were [authority, virtue, and mechanism](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/from-diocletians-edict-to-netflixs?). Necessity and future value, the two vocabularies of price legitimacy I associated with the industrial age and the platform era in the [kick-off post](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/from-diocletians-edict-to-netflixs?), are largely (though not entirely) absent in this era. Interestingly, the mechanism approach, which we tend to think of as a modern, technologically enabled characteristic that works today for algorithms, indexes, yield management systems, and dynamic pricing, was a fully developed premodern price legitimacy vocabulary and was often more transparent than current applications.

### **Reliance of price communication on public infrastructure**

Contrasting the infrastructure that supported price communication in this era with modern channels is instructive. Most prices in antiquity and the medieval world were fluid[^3]. The price tag, the workhorse of modern retailing, did not become common until well into the nineteenth century with the rise of department stores. In virtually every transaction, buyers and sellers settled on prices through haggling, custom, or intricate, mostly subtle responses to relationships, class, social status, and other factors (such practices can be seen even today by visiting a small city or village in [India](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-319-19428-8_14) or [Nigeria](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/1461445609105215?casa_token=3HqtJDLZD1EAAAAA:azz9E2ktep0ZrQJsQZmCfuUYbMP2BrIsjKEVSAb3xAA4hf-RVUXKnHhWOkzyZgjmuVQflDyjGu9b98g)). In this world, a common shopper would have developed price knowledge through experience, by cultivating relationships with vendors, and by listening to gossip and market news; prices paid would have varied dramatically across situations and over time.

When prices were formally fixed by an authority, the communication was directive. Instructions about how prices should be set, or the prices themselves, were publicized, whether inscribed in stone, carved on a public column or tablet, and placed in a market square or a city gate, or posted on parchment in a public location. At different times and in different places, there were other methods, such as a proclamation by a town crier who walked the streets calling out the day’s prices or the morning’s new ordinance, or enforcement through inspection, where an official walked the stalls with weights or a measuring stick, checking what was being sold matched what was allowed.

The medium itself signaled the price’s legitimacy. Inscriptions, carvings, and highly visible enforcements backed by authorities all reinforced the idea that these prices should be taken seriously. For instance, when Diocletian’s Edict was inscribed on stone tablets, it sent multiple messages to multiple constituents. It was simultaneously a communication to merchants (“do not exceed these prices”), to buyers (“do not pay more than these prices”), to municipal officials (“enforce these prices”), to soldiers (“here is what your salary can buy”), and to the broader community (“your emperor is acting on your behalf”).

### **Authority was the oldest method of legitimizing prices**

It’s not surprising that the earliest surviving price communications in the historical record come from authoritative sources, who had the resources to inscribe and publicize them in durable forms, such as stone, capable of surviving for thousands of years. One of the oldest, the Code of Hammurabi, inscribed on a stele in approximately 1754 BCE and now in the Louvre in Paris, contains provisions setting wages and fees for a range of services. For instance, a boatman who built a sixty-gur boat was to receive two shekels of silver, a surgeon who performed a successful operation on a free man was to receive ten shekels, a field laborer hired by the year was to receive eight gur of grain, and so on[^4]. These prices were intended to establish benchmarks by attaching the king’s authority to specific values. We can surmise that these prices were used as references and the basis for negotiation by the public. Even older Mesopotamian price provisions, including the laws of Eshnunna dating to approximately 1770 BCE, are comparable in form, suggesting that the practice of authority-based price setting was already mature by the time Hammurabi’s stele was carved[^5].

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/ea84e7de-4fb9-413f-9a84-9b70bfc13687_3477x2299.jpeg)

Section of the code of Hammurabi, iStockPhoto

This pattern recurs consistently across thousands of years and many cultures. A central authority sets a price, whether it is a reference price or a price ceiling, attaches its name and authority to give it heft, threatens punishment for violations, and announces the rule publicly. Diocletian’s Edict on Maximum Prices, issued in 301 CE, is the longest and most detailed surviving example of this pattern. Roman aedile market regulation offers a more routine, administrative example. Rather than issuing a sweeping empire-wide price decree, the aediles supervised markets, weights and measures, food provisions, and rules of buying and selling as part of Rome’s ordinary civic governance. Their office continued in different forms for several centuries[^6].

The public response to these price directives was decidedly mixed. They were often widely known and coercive, but they were not always obeyed. For example, Diocletian’s Edict appears to have lost its authority within a few years. Lactantius, a hostile contemporary critic of Diocletian, claimed that merchants withheld goods from the market rather than sell at the mandated ceiling prices, and the empire’s monetary crisis continued to push prices upward. The edict set price ceilings, but the underlying economic and social conditions made them unsustainable[^7].

This brings us to a subtle but important point. History suggests that a common reason for the failure of prices legitimized by authority is that the justification ceases to make sense for those affected. Diocletian’s preamble blamed merchant greed for the inflation, but the people experienced a combination of monetary debasement, imperial fiscal pressure, and ongoing military expenditure, none of which had to do with avaricious merchants. This pattern of authority failing through a “narrative mismatch,” as I will call it for the rest of the series, occurs when the audience sees the authority’s statements about prices as incomplete or false (and often self-serving). The ongoing debate about greedflation in the United States has the same narrative-mismatch dynamic, with corporations playing the role of authority and consumers the role of the skeptical audience.

### **Price legitimacy around the ancient world**

Given the prominence given to Hammurabi, Rome, and medieval Europe in most pricing histories, it is easy to assume that the vocabularies of price legitimacy emerged primarily in the Western world. In reality, some of the most sophisticated premodern systems for cultivating price legitimacy developed in parallel in India, the Islamic world, and China. Importantly, their approach of using institutions differed from the European tradition. I will examine three cases here.

#### ***India: Price legitimacy as an administrative calculus***

The Arthashastra is a Sanskrit treatise on statecraft attributed to the ancient Indian statesman, philosopher, and political advisor, Kautilya, dating to approximately the second or third century CE (although parts of it are likely significantly older). It contains one of the most impressive and comprehensive premodern accounts of price administration anywhere in the surviving record[^8]. In the treatise, Kautilya envisions a state with a dedicated officer, the Panyadhyaksha, conventionally translated as the Superintendent of Commerce, whose duties included monitoring demand, supply, and price movements for every important commodity in the kingdom and declaring prices accordingly.

The following extended text from the Arthashastra should give the reader an idea of the pricing rules set forth by Kautilya[^9]:

“The superintendent of commerce shall ascertain demand or absence of demand for, and rise or fall in the price of various kinds of merchandise which may be the products either of land or of water, and which may have been brought in either by land or by water path. That merchandise which is widely distributed shall be centralised and its price enhanced. When the enhanced rate becomes popular, another rate shall be declared. That merchandise of the king which is of local manufacture shall be centralised; imported merchandise shall be distributed in several markets for sale. Both kinds of merchandise shall be favorably sold to the people. He shall avoid such large profits as will harm the people. There shall be no restriction to the time of sale of those commodities for which there is frequent demand; nor shall they be subject to the evils of centralisation (saṅkuladoṣa). Or pedlars may sell the merchandise of the king at a fixed price in many markets and pay necessary compensation (vaidharaṇa) proportional to the loss entailed upon it (chedānurūpa).”

And it goes on. Kautilya’s instructions for setting prices are formulaic. The just price equals the average cost of production, plus tolls and taxes, plus transport and associated costs, plus a reasonable profit margin. For locally produced goods, the prescribed profit margin was about 5 percent. For imported goods, the margin was about 10 percent[^10]. When the actual price exceeded the amount recommended by the formula, the Superintendent was instructed to intervene, including by releasing state-held stocks of goods to lower prices.

Arthashastra’s price communication blends three vocabularies of price legitimacy from [our framework](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/from-diocletians-edict-to-netflixs?) into a single institutional grammar. First, there is authority, supplied by the king and his Superintendent; second, there is virtue, encoded in the moral injunction against profits that harm the people, and third, mechanism, supplied by the formula that links the legitimate price to its component costs. This Indian system of pricing, developed by Kautilya, anticipated today’s cost-plus pricing logic by nearly two millennia.

#### ***The medieval Islamic world: Price legitimacy as moral market supervision***

From the eighth century onward, cities across the Islamic world developed an institution called hisba, and the office of the muhtasib, the market inspector charged with enforcing it. The legal historian Kristen Stilt’s study of Mamluk Egypt (1250 to 1517 CE) describes the muhtasib as a regulator of markets and public spaces whose responsibilities included monitoring weights and measures, the quality and authenticity of coins, the quality of goods sold, fraud, hoarding, and price discipline[^11]. The duty was framed in religious terms as “commanding right and forbidding wrong” (al-amru bi’l-maʿrūf wa-n-nahyu ʿan al-munkar), and the muhtasib operated at the intersection of jurists’ law (fiqh) and the sultan’s command (siyasa).

Like Kautilya, what makes the muhtasib particularly interesting for our purposes is the institutional fusion of multiple price-legitimacy vocabularies within a single office. The muhtasib simultaneously embodied authority (his office was appointed by the state and backed by coercive power), virtue (his religious mandate was to command right and forbid wrong, considered the fundamental duties to God), and mechanism (his inspection of weights, measures, and coin authenticity using physical tools, in public view, by a known procedure).

There is also a subtle and recurring point about the institution’s history worth mentioning. The “market inspector” function predates Islam, appearing in Greek and Byzantine cities as the agoranomos and in the early Arab caliphates as the sahib al-suq. These roles began as practical necessities for performing mundane but essential administrative tasks for whoever was in charge, such as checking scales, weights, and currency. The religious framing of the office under the rubric of hisba was added later, grafted onto the existing functional apparatus[^12]. Using our terminology, the vocabulary of Virtue was added to a system that began as a mechanism. This kind of layering of price legitimacy vocabularies, where one justification attaches itself to another functioning one, is a pattern we will encounter throughout this series.

#### ***China: price legitimacy as stabilization***

A third premodern price grammar is found in the Chinese institution of the “ever-normal granary,” or changpingcang (常平倉). This institution originated under Han Emperor Wu through the so-called Balance Law in the second century BCE, when a civil servant named Sang Hongyang, who had previously been a merchant, designed a system in which the state would buy grain in seasons of abundance, store it, and release it in seasons of scarcity, with the explicit goal of stabilizing prices and preventing profiteering during shortages[^13]. By the Tang dynasty in the seventh and eighth centuries CE, the ever-normal granary system had been institutionalized at the prefectural scale.

The historian Denis Twitchett documents how Emperor Xuanzong’s Kaiyuan-era reforms (early eighth century) expanded the system substantially, and by 745 CE, ten regional networks of changping warehouses spanning Guannei, Hebei, Hedong, and other regions together held more than 4.6 million shi of grain[^14]. In the eleventh century, the Song reformer Wang Anshi revived and expanded the system as part of his New Policies, adding a state-backed lending facility tied to the granaries[^15]. Interestingly, throughout this period, the ever-normal granary communicated the rulers’ price policy to the public without any verbal messaging, simply through its visible, large-scale administrative activities of stocking and selling, and its clearly expressed intent to stabilize prices.

This is fundamentally different from Hammurabi’s and Diocletian’s operations, and it depended on a fundamentally different kind of state. The Roman emperor at the end of the third century had the power to threaten merchants with execution (and carry out that threat), but lacked the administrative capacity, infrastructure, or financial resources to buy and hold grain stocks in every province and time their release. The Chinese system, on the other hand, made no threats and twisted no arms: it worked because the state had built that capacity over centuries to execute the price stabilization process.

### **Virtue as reciprocal discipline**

We now return to Western Europe and look more closely at the second major price-legitimacy vocabulary of the medieval period: virtue. The medieval European discussion of the “just price” is one of the most extensively studied topics in the history of economic thought, and I cannot do justice to the concept here[^16]. Here, I want to emphasize one feature of the doctrine that is most surprising from a modern pricing perspective.

As developed by Thomas Aquinas, Duns Scotus, and the wider scholastic tradition in the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, the just price treated exchange as a matter of justice between the parties. Aquinas defines the just price as “the one, which at a given time, can be gotten from the buyers, assuming common knowledge and in the absence of all fraud and coercion[^17].” This idea feels familiar today because customer value is so central to how we think about legitimate pricing strategies. For the medieval period, however, the claim was striking. A seller who charged above the just price acted wrongly, as did a buyer who paid below it. Even a buyer who paid more in order to secure a scarce supply could be seen as violating the moral order of the exchange[^18].

The economic historian Raymond de Roover warned against a common modern misreading of this material in his definitive article on the just price[^19], observing that the scholastics understood prices to move with supply and demand. They were most concerned with how exchange was conducted between buyers and sellers within the range of prices produced by markets. The just price was usually close to the prevailing market price, though both parties still bore moral duties in negotiation and trade. In de Roover’s words[^20]:

“The scholastics were more favorable to freedom or competition than is generally assumed. Their hostility toward monopoly was especially marked. Contrary to a widespread belief they certainly did not rely on the price system to maintain the social hierarchy.”

The just price doctrine established moral expectations for buyers and sellers and introduced the notion of reciprocity when considering prices in an exchange. In previous eras, for instance, in Hammurabi’s code and Diocletian’s edict, the price was established and administered authoritatively, so that both parties had to transact (in theory, at least) at that level. In contrast, the medieval just price placed the onus on both parties, requiring them to act virtuously while negotiating within the range of market prices. The center of moral attention shifted from the price itself to the conduct of the parties involved in the transaction. Importantly, the explicit acknowledgment of reciprocity changed the buyer’s role, empowering them and increasing their personal responsibility by establishing obligations: they were expected to bargain fairly, refrain from taking advantage of scarcity or distress, and deal honestly in a particular exchange context. In this way, virtue became the central vocabulary through which price legitimacy was established.

For another interesting case study utilizing the virtue vocabulary in the medieval period, read [my earlier post on the medieval Catholic Church’s sale of indulgences](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/when-the-coin-in-the-coffer-rings?). One point worth highlighting from the indulgence case, relevant to the current discussion, is the income-graduated price scale (an early form of tiered pricing) the Church used to sell indulgences. The Church justified the use of this price structure entirely through virtue language. The variation in price by buyer wealth was framed as fairness rather than as price discrimination.

In medieval Europe, the institutional infrastructure that promoted and enforced the virtue vocabulary for pricing was multi-layered and substantial. Guild ordinances, regulated by the towns and cities, set quality standards and price norms for the trades of their members. As an example, the Bakers’ Company and city regulations in London, recorded in the Liber Albus, governed matters such as bread selling, grain buying, permitted types of bread, and the use of bakers’ seals[^21]. Bishops and church courts could weigh in on matters of usury, just price, and fraud, especially clever, complex schemes. For instance, to circumvent usury laws, savvy moneylenders would sell everyday items like hats to customers at inflated prices, essentially “cloaking” usury[^22]. It took a well-developed and well-functioning apparatus to detect, prosecute, and punish such acts. Furthermore, town magistrates inspected weights and measures while local juries set the going prices of staples. The just-price doctrine was operationalized through everyday institutional practices across hundreds of European towns. Thus, when prices were established or changed, they were supported by this infrastructure of inspection, ordinance, and moral expectation.

### **Lessons from the Assize of Bread about the transparency of mechanism**

Let’s return to the Assize of Bread and consider how it employed the mechanism vocabulary for legitimizing prices. As briefly described earlier, the Assize used a relatively straightforward price structure. The standard wholesale price of wheat was set locally, usually by a borough jury, at regular intervals. Once this price was established, the weight of bread that a baker was required to sell for each denomination of coin (a ha’penny loaf, a penny loaf, a farthing loaf, etc.) was determined by a published formula. When wheat prices rose, the ha’penny loaf became smaller (as our protagonist discovered), and vice versa. The mechanism, in this case, the loaf weight, was responsible for the price’s movement. In this form, the Assize remained as a price structure for over five centuries (from 1266 until it was repealed in the 1820s)[^23].

Several aspects warrant further exploration. First, the Assize was a public rule well known to everyone involved. In principle, anyone could check whether a baker’s ha’penny loaf was the stipulated weight, given the current grain price. This was done by the market’s clerk during regular inspections. Second, the mechanism operated directly through the tangible product the buyer could see, touch, weigh, and respond to, just as consumers do today with shrinkflation. Third, the moral framing of the Assize was reciprocal, as I described in the previous section. The baker who cheated by selling underweight loaves was committing an offense against the moral order, just as the customer who hoarded bread during shortages, or tried to bid the price down. Moral expectations were imposed on both sides of the transaction and carried consequences when breached.

The key difference between the shrinkflation in the Assize of Bread and today’s practices is transparency. In many cases, when CPG brands want to reduce the quantity or amount of a product today, they take steps to [hide the fact](https://qz.com/2129426/inflation-and-supply-chain-snags-are-shrinking-your-products) from consumers to the extent possible. Usually, there are no public announcements or press releases, and quite often the package design is changed (see below), or a new SKU variant is introduced with a reduced quantity to make it harder for consumers to identify that quantities have decreased.

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/075e45e1-0339-4195-98bc-a5ca3fe09767_4080x2296.png)

Example of shrinkflation (295 g to 275 g) concurrent with a significant package change. Source: r/shrinkflation, post by Jess_7478, May 20, 2025.

In recent years, the hunger for content creation among social media influencers has led to the exposure of many instances of shrinkflation. Where the Assize of Bread embraced agency and laid bare the price-legitimizing mechanism, today’s shrinkflation practitioners try their best to conceal the mechanism in a black box, leaving even the savviest consumers to wonder to what extent input cost increases (as opposed to something else) led to the shrinkflation.

As the cases throughout this piece have found, the medieval and early modern world possessed a recurring institutional reflex to make the underlying mechanism governing prices visible to customers (and everyone else) as part of their communication processes. In contrast, the modern world has, by and large, lost this reflex. The implications of this loss offer an important insight for today’s pricing decision-makers, drawing on the premodern era.

To legitimize price changes, the vocabulary of mechanism remains powerful in modern price-change communication. It is plausibly the single most effective of the five vocabularies for the company that uses it, because it allows a seller to deflect blame from itself onto an ambiguous “other.” However, the medieval evidence suggests that the same vocabulary can also be deployed in a transparency-enhancing way that strengthens rather than erodes the customer’s trust and understanding of prices. The medieval baker was operating in a system that was more accountable to its customers than the average consumer brand is today.

### **Scarcity as a countervailing force against medieval price-legitimizing vocabularies**

The bubonic plague pandemic, also known as the Black Death, swept through Europe from 1347 to 1351, killing between 30 and 50 percent of England’s population. A similar percentage of the population died across Western Europe[^24]. (For context, COVID killed .2-.3% percent of the European population)[^25]. Obviously, the economic consequences were severe and immediate. With labor suddenly in short supply, the surviving workers found themselves in a much stronger bargaining position. Wages rose sharply, and skilled workers in some regions doubled their pre-plague pay within a few years, and worker mobility increased.

The authority’s reaction was swift and severe. While the plague was still going on, King Edward III of England issued the Ordinance of Labourers in June 1349. His parliament reinforced it with the Statute of Labourers in 1351 once it could safely convene. The text of the Statute is worth quoting because it explains the authorities’ motivations clearly[^26]:

“Because a great part of the people and especially of the, workmen and servants has now died in that pestilence, some, seeing the straights of the masters and the scarcity of servants, are not willing to serve unless they receive excessive wages, and others, rather than through labour to gain their living, prefer to beg in idleness…that every man and woman of our kingdom of England, of whatever condition, whether bond or free, who is able bodied and below the age of sixty years, not living from trade nor carrying on a fixed craft, nor having of his own the means of living, or land of his own with regard to the cultivation of which he might occupy himself, and not serving another, if he, considering his station, be sought after to serve in a suitable service, he shall be bound to serve him who has seen fit so to seek after him; and he shall take only the wages liveries, meed or salary which, in the places where he sought to serve, were accustomed to be paid in the twentieth year of our reign of England, or the five or six common years next preceding.”

Wages were fixed at pre-plague levels, with a combination of authority and virtue used to legitimize this action. Additionally, through this statute, the Parliament simultaneously suppressed the competing necessity vocabulary (which we haven’t encountered in this era, but is common in other eras). Specifically, the plague had created a genuine scarcity of resources of all types, and scarcity-driven wage increases (and price increases) were the rational economic response. By labeling these increases as “excessive” and the workers as “malicious,” the Statute denied the legitimacy of the necessity vocabulary.

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/e7cd5ad6-0365-4ada-aade-a07f887f3d79_2126x1517.jpeg)

The Triumph of Death (1562-63) by Pieter Brueghel the Elder, oil on panel, Prado National Museum, Madrid, Spain. Source: Wikimedia Commons.

Despite this Statute, wages continued to rise in England throughout the second half of the fourteenth century. The additional payment was offered to workers in a number of ways, through in-kind payments or cash bonuses described as gifts to evade the law. The English Peasants’ Revolt of 1381, while caused by many factors, drew some of its energy from the long-running resentment of authorities who tried to hold wages down through fiat[^27]. Across France, Flanders, and Italy, similar wage-control attempts met similar resistance.

Prices followed a similar pattern, with greater volatility than wages. The 1351 Statute required victuallers, such as bakers, butchers, fishmongers, and brewers, to sell at “reasonable” prices, extending the older Assize of Bread and Ale that had governed English staple-food prices since the thirteenth century. Prices proved even less controllable than wages for the authorities. In the decade and a half after the plague, consumer price inflation surged on supply shocks and monetary disruption, swamping the nominal wage gains workers had won and keeping real wages depressed even as money wages rose. Here, again, there was a narrative mismatch. In the final quarter of the fourteenth century, deflation set in, consumer prices fell substantially, and real wages finally increased. In both phases, the Statute did little to control wages or prices[^28].

An interesting, durable insight emerges from this case study. The Statute of Labourers invoked the dual vocabularies of authority and virtue, using them together to suppress the necessity vocabulary that the environment had made so significant. The suppression failed repeatedly through a period of fifty years, not surprisingly, because market conditions overwhelmed authoritative attempts to construct a different narrative, highlighting the narrative mismatch. From here on out, as we shall see in future posts, necessity would become a more influential and commonly used vocabulary for legitimizing prices.

### **Lessons from antiquity and the medieval world for today’s pricing decision makers**

In every case studied in this post, the price legitimizing apparatus operated in public and was broadly visible. The stone tablet with Diocletian’s edict stood in the market square; the muhtasib walked the streets; the ever-normal granary existed as a visible civic institution with heft; the Assize of Bread was both a published rule and a routine of public inspection. Even the medieval baker’s loaf could be weighed by anyone curious enough to ask. Modern price communication, especially in its personalized, A/B-tested, algorithmically approved form, has acquired precision and adaptability while losing much of this common visibility. A customer who receives a price-increase email does not know whether their neighbor or friend who patronizes the brand received the same message, the same price, or the same explanation. Premodern audiences took the common knowledge for granted as a condition of legitimate pricing. Modern audiences have learned to live without it, but they still notice when it is missing and are always discomfited, to a lesser or greater degree. This begs the question of why we consider low transparency as the default setting, and whether certain brands would benefit from adopting a “common visibility” approach to communicating prices and price changes.

Modern price-change communications routinely deploy virtue language, asserting that the seller is acting fairly, transparently, responsibly, and with the customer’s interests at heart. However, upon closer inspection, this virtue is one-sided, involving claims about the seller’s conduct. In stark contrast, medieval virtue language relied on two-sided virtue, where buyers were bound by moral obligations to the same extent as sellers. It’s interesting that in most normal market transactions today, buyers either need not or are explicitly not expected to behave morally, and in some narratives, they may indeed be discouraged from doing so (see, for instance, the recent debate over whether [microlooting](https://www.city-journal.org/article/progressives-microlooting-hasan-piker-jia-tolentino-podcast) is ok). But when market conditions depart from normality, the two-sided moral imperative quickly emerges. For example, in the early stages of the COVID-19 pandemic, many buyers who stockpiled hand sanitizer or N95 masks were publicly reviled and shamed as much as sellers who price-gouged customers. Similarly, an adult who [snatches up popular Pokémon cards](https://news4sanantonio.com/news/nation-world/parents-frustrated-over-pokemon-card-collectors) from kids or throws a fit and [turns violent in a restaurant](https://www.nbcchicago.com/news/local/pilsen-restaurant-owners-searching-for-answers-after-waitress-attacked-over-50-check/3717496/) receives the same sort of disdain.

In the ancient and medieval world, we encountered three vocabularies of price legitimacy, authority, virtue, and mechanism, all of which relied on retaining the audience’s trust for their continued functioning. Diocletian’s edict failed when the emperor’s economic policies lost their credibility and inflation ran rampant. During and after the Black Plague, the Statute of Labourers failed when the labor shortage dictated that wages had to rise, statute or no statute. The Assize of Bread worked for over five centuries because the local borough institution maintained the credibility of the entire process, from price setting to price enforcement, throughout this period. The key lesson here is that, far more important than the specific vocabulary used to legitimize the price, is the set of processes used to build and maintain trust among those affected by the price.

I began this post by asking you to imagine yourself as a medieval English consumer buying bread. If you noticed your ha’penny loaf was smaller one week, and suspected wrongdoing, you could complain to the borough authorities, ask for your bread to be weighed, and get satisfaction. You knew what the price was, why it changed, and were confident everyone else was paying the same price. In a real sense, as a consumer, you were more empowered in medieval England in the late thirteenth century than many of us are today, having to deal with a flurry of dynamic pricing, shrinkflation, surge pricing, and algorithmically personalized offers without logic or explanation.

The next post in this series will pick up the story of price communication in the early modern period, from 1500 to 1750, when commodity price currents, long-distance merchant correspondence, and the early applications of the necessity vocabulary of price legitimacy begin to take center stage.
