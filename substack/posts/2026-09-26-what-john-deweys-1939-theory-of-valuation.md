##### ***Summary.*** If you want a deeper understanding of willingness-to-pay, you have to look beyond the popular idea that WTP is a well-formed estimate in the customer’s head that can be extracted as a numerical value or range. In his 1939 monograph, *[Theory of Valuation](https://archive.org/details/theoryofvaluatio032168mbp/page/n11/mode/2up)*, the philosopher John Dewey provided a more nuanced perspective, distinguishing between two forms of valuing something: (1) *prizing*, i.e., holding something dear, and (2) *appraising*, i.e., working out what something is worth. He argued that appraisal only happens when the status quo is disturbed, and the resulting judgment is more like a forecast that is confirmed or revised by later experience than like an established value. Dewey’s ideas from 1939 provide us with a deeper, richer understanding of the customer’s WTP and insights into a range of contemporary pricing issues, such as WTP research for new products, enterprise AI renewals, value-based pricing, and subscription price increases.

### **Introduction**

Suppose there’s a consumer named Felicity who has subscribed to a popular video streaming service for the past several years (perhaps even a decade or more; she couldn’t tell you how long). She watches anything on the service rarely, perhaps a buzzy documentary or a new movie from time to time. If asked, she would tell you that she likes the service and enjoys being a customer. She has never calculated what it is worth to her, and doesn’t even know exactly how much she’s paying for it every month, or whether that’s a good price. Then, one day, she receives an email from the streaming service telling her that prices are going up by \$3 per month, starting with the next billing cycle.

That evening, for the first time in many years, Felicity sits down and evaluates the service. First, she finds out exactly how much she’s paying right now, and then she does some investigation on the streaming service’s app to figure out which shows she watched over the past month. She works out the number of hours viewed on a monthly basis, explores two other competitors, including the content available on them and their price, and learns about the different tiers available from these services. How does Felicity’s evaluation of the streaming service before the email differ from her more systematic evaluation afterward?

In the pricing world, these questions are often answered by invoking Felicity’s willingness-to-pay (WTP), and then trying to measure it and use it as an input of customer value in the pricing decision. Our understanding of how WTP works and should be used has a lot of baggage accumulated over the decades of pricing surveys, choice-based conjoint analysis, Van Westendorp, etc. But if we drop some of this baggage and go back about 90 years, we can gain a fresh perspective on WTP specifically and on the concepts of value and valuation more generally. These insights, which are well worth visiting (or revisiting), can be traced to a marvelous monograph from 1939 by the philosopher John Dewey (1859-1952), titled the *[Theory of Valuation](https://archive.org/details/theoryofvaluatio032168mbp/page/n11/mode/2up)*. Dewey’s work clarifies customer valuation concepts in very useful ways and has the potential to inform many contemporary pricing issues with fresh insights, as explored in this post.

### **Dewey’s distinction between prizing (also known as valuing) and appraising (also known as value judgment or evaluation).**

In the *Theory of Valuation*, Dewey examines the basis of a person’s valuation, distinguishing between *prizing*, which he defines as “holding something precious, dear (and various other nearly equivalent activities like honoring, regarding highly,” and *appraising*, which he defines as “*putting* a value upon, *assigning* value to. This is an activity of rating, an act that involves comparison, as is explicit, for example, in appraisals in money terms of goods and services” (emphases in original). The distinction between prizing and appraising will be carried through this post.

While prizing reflects an individual’s attitude towards an object, appraising involves the volitional activities of gathering information, estimating, comparing, and evaluating. The two forms of valuation are obviously connected; appraising can lead to something new being prized, and something that was prized before can stop being prized after an appraisal. Furthermore, the two need not co-occur. A person can prize something that they have never appraised, like Felicity, prior to receiving the price increase email. On the flip side, something can be appraised and not be prized.

The philosopher Elizabeth Anderson, whose [pluralist theory of value](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/what-elizabeth-andersons-pluralist) I discussed in an earlier piece, has provided a useful interpretation of Dewey’s theorizing that I will refer extensively here. In her [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on Dewey’s moral philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/dewey-moral/), she summarizes the relationship between prizing and appraising (which she calls “valuing” and “value judgment” or “evaluation,” respectively) this way: “Value judgments arise when valuings are subjected to evaluation or appraisal, when one asks the question whether (one ought) to value (seek, cherish, consume, etc.) something.” Anderson’s elaboration of Dewey’s two concepts is helpful for our understanding, starting with prizing (valuing)[^1]:

“At the most primitive level, valuings are tendencies to move toward, acquire, or ingest certain things, or, on the negative side, to avoid, reject, spew out other things. One need not have any idea of what one is valuing in order to value it. Hence, they are less sophisticated than desires, which have propositional content (an end in view) and arise from practical reflection. In the first instance, valuings denote impulses toward or away from objects, as when an infant turns toward human voices, or swats away a fly. Valuings of objects as useful can also be immediate — that is, not mediated by cognition or awareness of what one is doing. One uses a fork to pick up food, without thinking about it. Habits, then, are also a species of valuing.”

This means that prizing or valuing does not require any process of assessment or calculation. A consumer may simply like something for its sake or because it supports a habit and that will be reflected in a higher valuation. Furthermore, to the extent that prizing something is a visceral response, the emotion or the sensory experience occurring from an experience will have a significant impact on the consumer’s prizing of the object. Now on to the concept of appraising, which Anderson calls “value judgment.” Here’s how [she describes the concept](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/dewey-moral/#ValuVsEval):

“Value judgments arise when valuings are subjected to *evaluation* or appraisal, when one asks the question whether (one ought) to value (seek, cherish, consume, etc.) something. Thus, value judgments are practical judgments. Although they may have a descriptive form (“*x* is good,” “*x* is right”), the constitutive point of making them is to alter or guide our valuings. The need to question our valuings arises when immediate action on them is not possible or yields unsatisfactory consequences. There is no more ice cream in the refrigerator; is it worthwhile to go to the store to buy some more? Or, the lactose-intolerant person may observe that she gets a stomach ache after eating ice cream, and discover that ice cream is the cause. Should she just give up ice cream, can she take something that avoids the symptoms, or are there lactose-free substitutes? Having sketched out some alternative solutions to her predicament, she imaginatively fills out the details of acting on them, including their projected consequences (Do the pills have side effects? Does the lactose-free ice cream taste good?). The consequences are the objects of valuings, which guide the formation of a new end-in-view, a new valuing — say, to go for lactose-free ice cream, because taking pills would be bothersome, and the lactose-free ice cream tastes just as good. The comparative value judgment (“eating lactose-free ice cream is better than taking pills with regular ice cream, or eating regular ice cream alone”) is practical because its function is to guide conduct toward the best solution to the person’s problem.”

### **When does the customer move from prizing to appraising?**

As should be clear from Anderson’s explanation of appraisal or value judgment, appraising is effortful and requires considerable cognitive work. An important contribution of Dewey’s account was to examine when an individual moves from prizing to appraising. He concluded that people do so only when their situation forces them, that is, when “there is something the matter.” In Dewey’s words:

“When analyzed, this “something the matter” is found to spring from the fact that there is something lacking, wanting, in the existing situation as it stands, an absence which produces conflict in the elements that do exist.” (p. 33).

Thus, appraisal occurs when there is a problem in the normal course of things, and the individual needs to think more carefully about the object’s worth. In Dewey’s framework, then, most consumers, for most products and services, for most of the time, do not have an appraisal on which to base their valuation. Instead, their valuation is rooted in prizing (or not prizing), that is, in visceral responses of liking or disliking. Appraisals are produced at particular moments by particular events, whether it is a new need, a problem with the product, a competitor’s offer, a price change, and so on. For pricing research, this means that asking a consumer to provide a WTP estimate is itself an intervention. Where no appraisal was needed or existed, the question led the consumer to form an appraisal then and there. How accurate that appraisal is and whether it will carry through to an actual purchase decision in the future are open questions at this point.

Going back to Felicity’s case, before she received the price increase message, her evaluation of the streaming service was a prizing of the service brand, devoid of specific evaluative content and driven by a reflexive, perhaps emotional response. But upon receiving the message, it changed to an appraisal and a value judgment. From a mindset of “the streaming service is worth it to me,” Felicity moved to the question of ‘‘how much is this streaming service worth to me?”

### **The end-in-view as the institution of desire and a forecast of the future**

The outcome of an appraisal results in a change in the valuation of the object under consideration and leaning towards a particular outcome or object, called “end-in-view” by Dewey, and what Anderson refers to as “the institution of a desire….denoting a tendency toward action that the agent has adopted, rather than simply a motive clamoring for our attention or moving us behind our backs…a reflective, conscious valuing, not a mere “affective-motor” attitude.” She goes on to note that “It is a cognitive state. As the individual engages this new valuation, she experiences the consequences of acting on it. Reflection upon these consequences is then incorporated into more intelligent valuations, by way of further appraisals.” Thus, in some sense, the end-in-view is a forecast of action, a prediction of the individual’s preferred outcome.

Returning to Felicity’s case, her appraisal may lead her to prefer a new service, which would mean that her end-in-view is to cancel the subscription to her current service, which she has been loyal to for many years, and join the new service. The economic valuation of both services, the current one and the new, preferred one, is part and parcel of the appraisal process and the end-in-view. Significantly for us, the appraisal and the election of the end-in-view lead to the formulation of Felicity’s WTP.

It’s worth noting here that Dewey’s formulation of the shift from prizing to appraisal and the adoption of the end-in-view anticipated by several decades the common conceptualization in such marketing concepts as [Marketing Myopia](https://hbr.org/2004/07/marketing-myopia) and the [Jobs To Be Done framework](https://hbr.org/2016/09/know-your-customers-jobs-to-be-done) that when customers purchase a product, what they are really buying is the benefits delivered by the product, and therefore any other product, even if seemingly dissimilar, that delivers the same benefits is a potential substitute for the given product. What’s more, Dewey’s formulation adds a layer of nuance that is missing in these later ideas. Because the end-in-view is a forecast, its quality is variable, and the seller influences the assessment of quality through approaches such as demonstrations, trials, case studies, referrals, and so on, bolstering the buyer’s end-in-view. Thus, a significant aspect of what we call value communication, according to Dewey, is helping to form an end-in-view.

### **The inextricability of price from the object of value**

Another important aspect of valuation, according to Dewey, is the dependence of the end upon the means required to reach it. In contrast to the conventional view that means are selected once an end is clearly established, Dewey argues that an individual appraises the whole course of action, including the effort, the money, and the other things that must be done and given up along the way to reach the end. Thus, an outcome that looks attractive on its own may not be as attractive once the means required to reach it are considered, and a person who ignores the outcome hasn’t really valued it at all. In a nutshell, it’s the whole means-ends package that is relevant to valuation, not the ends and means separately. He underscores this point with a terrific story from Charles Lamb about the origin of roast pork. As Dewey puts it:

“The story, it will be remembered, is that roast pork was first enjoyed when a house in which pigs were confined was accidentally burned down. While searching in the ruins, the owners touched the pigs that had been roasted in the fire and scorched their fingers. Impulsively bringing their fingers to their mouths to cool them, they experienced a new taste. Enjoying the taste, they henceforth set themselves to building houses, inclosing pigs in them, and then burning the houses down. Now, if ends-in-view are what they are entirely apart from means, and have their value independently of valuation of means, there is nothing absurd, nothing ridiculous, in this procedure, for the end attained, the de facto termination, was eating and enjoying roast pork, and that was just the end desired. Only when the end attained is estimated in terms of the means employed the building and burning-down of houses in comparison with other available means by which the desired result in view might be attained is there anything absurd or unreasonable about the method employed.”

Anderson calls this the “reciprocal determination of means and ends,” and explains, “The value of the end depends on the costs and benefits of the means, and the costs and benefits of the further consequences to which the end is judged as a means or cause…. A full inquiry into the means needed to achieve the end may lead to a re-evaluation of the end itself…. Furthermore, reaching the end has anticipated consequences of its own.… that may modify the valuation of the end…. It is irrational to take one’s end as fixed before investigating the costs of the means and the consequences of achieving the end.”

This idea is counterintuitive but makes a solid contribution to how we think about value and price. Typically, we treat value and price as two separate things, and commonly (like in my [Value Pricing Framework](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/the-value-pricing-framework-updated)), value is an input into price. We assume that the customer has some sort of value in their mind for the product, observes the price, and purchases the product if the price is below that value; otherwise, they walk away. In contrast, Dewey’s account argues that price is one of the means to the end and therefore enters the customer’s appraisal from the outset. Thus, value is determined by the price, not the other way around, and changing the price changes the product’s value. (Note that this logic is consistent with the [quality-signaling potential of price](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/002224378502200210?casa_token=6QWriFuSFYAAAAAA%3An5THfvg-GDRTtUL6OLbIkWdBRUjfuJFm3tQmoW3q0w32kgQo_2nFrowV8aDpuwxGW3jcO3e-v9O7P4w) that is often invoked when discussing concepts like premium or tiered pricing).

### **Valuation as a hypothesis revised with experience**

The final concept from Dewey’s theory that I want to focus on here is the revision of valuation. Rather than treating valuation as a static quantity that resides in the individual’s mind, Dewey treats it as a variable that is revised with experience. The individual makes a forecast by adopting the end(s)-in-view, acts in accordance with the means to reach it, and, on the basis of the experience, revises the end(s)-in-view. For instance, he may learn that some things he desired were disappointing once attained, whereas others were better than expected, and thus adjust what he desires accordingly.

Dewey articulates the distinction between what is *desired* and what is *desirable*. The desired is whatever the individual happens to want at the moment. The desirable is what a person wants after considering the conditions that produced the desire and the consequences of acting on it. Thus, what is desirable relies on experience rather than being a terminal value, or something prized for its own sake. In Anderson’s words:

“We test scientific hypotheses by bringing about their antecedents and seeing if the results are as they predicted. Similarly, we test value judgments by acting on them and seeing if we value the consequences in the way the judgment predicted. Acting on our value judgments — putting them into practice — supplies the data for confirming or disconfirming them. Roughly speaking, a value judgment hypothesizes “try it, you’ll like it” — a statement easily subject to empirical verification and refutation. Intelligent value judgments proceed not by random trial-and-error, but from skilled projection of prior confirmed “try-like” regularities to analogous novel situations, which are continuously modified in light of experiences of the wider consequences of trying in these new situations.”

Here again, this seemingly abstract concept has significant implications for pricing strategy, particularly for how we think about WTP. The most obvious implication is that a customer’s valuation at the time of purchase and their valuation at a later point in time, when they have used the product, are entirely different judgments based on different evidence. While the first is a forecast, the second is based on direct experience and may deviate significantly from the initial valuation. Much of pricing research and managerial attention focuses on the first pre-purchase valuation; in my experience, little, if any attention is given to the second, and it can be argued, far more important valuation.

Furthermore, to understand how the second valuation may deviate from the first, we can distinguish among three effects that arise as customers gain experience. First, the customer learns about the product’s performance, updating their understanding of the product based on direct experience. Second, the customer reassesses how much a benefit or sacrifice matters when using the product, and third, the customer revises the purpose of the purchase. Each of these effects merits a different managerial response. Updating performance expectations signals a need for better, more accurate information, the reconsideration of priorities calls for more flexibility and different configurations, and a revised purpose can change which options are considered as competitive alternatives by the customer.

Taken together, these distinctions provide a useful framework for examining four contemporary pricing problems among many others: (1) WTP research for new products, (2) enterprise AI renewals, (3) value-based pricing, and (4) subscription price increases. I have chosen these four problems here because each one shines a light on a different aspect of Dewey’s theory of valuation, because the idea that WTP can be elicited is central to each of them, and because the four issues span the customer’s relationship with the seller. Let’s consider each issue in greater detail.

### **WTP research for new products**

Consider a well-funded startup that wants to understand the WTP of target customers for a new household appliance that automates meal preparation and requires minimal customer participation. Using conventional concept testing methods, the target customer will see a digital or physical mock-up of the product, watch a demonstration explaining how it will work, and then be asked how much they would pay for it, along with other questions. The participant will provide a response, perhaps after considering the opportunity costs recovered from not having to cook, the price of forgoing a task seen as aversive, the price of alternatives like meal delivery or restaurant food, and so on. In this scenario, the researcher doesn’t know which thinking was used but records the participant’s WTP as a dollar amount or a range.

When using the perspective of WTP as a value to be extracted discussed earlier, the core question is how accurately (in the sense of circumventing the respondents’ strategic intentions to game the process) the research method obtained the target customer’s WTP for the appliance. However, Dewey’s formulation of valuation directs our attention towards the issue of the consumer’s appraisal. Specifically, what appraisal did the respondent engage in because of the questions asked in the pricing research study? Before participating in the concept test, the respondent would have had no reason to evaluate this particular method of preparing a meal and would not even have known that such a thing was possible. The information provided in the study and the WTP question generated the appraisal and influenced which specific aspects were emphasized and which ones were ignored.

For instance, a digital concept video that focuses on the benefits of coming home to a prepared meal will lead to a different appraisal than one demonstrating the specific steps involved in the appliance’s use, such as buying ingredients, preparing them, cleaning the machine afterward, and accommodating the preferences of different family members. Even in the subsequent survey, a question about prepared meal delivery would orient the respondent more towards such services, whereas asking about how the respondent typically prepares food may tilt their thinking towards their time, money, and effort. Thus, the appraisal and selection of the ends-in-view may differ depending on how the study is designed, implying that the resulting WTP estimate will also be based on different antecedents and may take different values.

The variability in the ends-in-view that drives the estimate is particularly significant. One respondent may envision using the appliance to reduce spending money on DoorDash, while a second consumer may look to free up time spent cooking meals for the family every day to do something more agreeable instead, and a third may be most interested in the health benefits derived from the meals by being able to control the quality of ingredients. Each of these ends leads to a different set of means being prioritized in Dewey’s framework, and therefore to a different economic calculus. Averaging responses from these very diverse respondents, even if they are all in a conventionally defined target segment, is likely to produce a misleading WTP estimate. There’s some available empirical evidence supporting this conjecture. A [pricing research meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11747-019-00666-6) covering 115 effect sizes from 77 studies found that hypothetical WTP was 21% higher than real WTP on average, and that this gap was larger for specialty goods than for other product types, which seems consistent with the idea that specialty goods are associated with a more diverse set of ends-in-view.

One obvious implication is that pricing researchers need to examine the content and basis of respondents’ forecasts (in Dewey’s parlance), not just the WTP estimate. This could be done systematically by asking customers specific, open-ended questions, such as “What do you expect to accomplish by using this appliance?” to understand the basis for the valuation. Another useful insight is that consumers need to be revisited after they have had the opportunity to use the appliance, as they are likely to substantially update their appraisal. This would mean that a significant repricing (in either direction) may be necessary as the new product is adopted across the market, and that follow-up pricing research will be needed with experienced appliance users.

### **Enterprise AI renewals**

Enterprise AI renewals provide a setting to consider what happens when the customer has acted on their initial appraisal and had the opportunity to revise it. Consider a company that purchases an AI tool for its customer service group. During the sales process, the vendor demonstrates that the tool can draft replies and send them out quickly after a customer message or complaint is received. The company envisions AI agents handling more inquiries more efficiently and eliminating the need for additional staffing. This initial assessment is based on the expected improvement in the customer service group's productivity.

After adopting and using the tool, the criteria will differ when the time comes to renew the contract. The company now has evidence about how the AI agents were used and what they delivered. It has found that although agents generated replies more quickly, supervisors still had to spend time checking them, and some responses required far more effort on the supervisor’s part, such as reconstructing the customer’s purchase history or correcting a plausible but inappropriate response. Furthermore, the integration of the AI agents with current processes, as well as the training and monitoring of required resources, has had to be done. Thus, when compared to what the company expected (i.e., its forecast), there was a significant gap in what actually occurred.

The three effects discussed earlier help distinguish what the company might have learned. First, performance expectations may have changed. AI agents work well for routine customer inquiries, but don’t suit boundary cases. Second, the company may have reconsidered the significance of things being sacrificed with the adoption of AI. A degree of checking deemed manageable during the product demonstration proved untenable at scale, completely swamping supervisors. And third, the purpose may have changed. Instead of seeking to increase the volume of inquiries responded to, the company may now want to increase the quality and consistency of responses.

The main point is that the company’s experience with the AI product will lead to an appraisal during renewal that may be drastically different than the one at the time of adoption. Depending on the ends chosen, the means will be different and, as discussed throughout, will have a different suitable price or price range. A vendor that is tone-deaf and ignores the revised evaluation criteria will have an unproductive renewal negotiation.

This is also a setting in which the distinction between prizing and appraising is relevant. Individual employees within the company may prize the tool because it automates a task they normally find to be disagreeable and difficult. In contrast, the buying group will appraise the tool using a concrete cost-benefit analysis. Even if this analysis results in a positive evaluation (i.e., economic benefits outweigh the costs), the actual users of the tool may still not value it from a prizing sense. Specifically, there is no reason for overlap in the judgments of users and buyers within the organization. In fact, they may have very different WTPs based on their specific valuation approaches, which are completely missed when producing a WTP measure for the organization as a whole.

For the vendor, this discussion implies that customer value is likely to be different at the time of first sale than at the time of renewal, and some acknowledgment of this difference is needed in the pricing strategy used. Certain pricing models with greater flexibility, such as those that incorporate outcome-based pricing plus some sort of fixed price component, are likely to do better than rigid ones when there’s a big gap between the two. In practice, this may mean that the vendor and buyer should determine which outcomes will be tracked at the outset and then measure them regularly over the course of the product’s use. When the time comes for renewal negotiations, they can be reviewed and used for making price adjustments. (Note that this is easy to propose in a blog post, but very difficult to do in practice in the current manic AI environment.) Thus, from the perspective of Dewey’s theory of valuation, the vendor’s main task from the initial purchase to the point of renewal is to deliver a user experience that converts the buyer’s initial forecast into a matching validated judgment.

### **Value-based pricing**

Value-based pricing presents a more fundamental conceptual challenge. As I’ve [written at length](https://hbr.org/2016/08/a-quick-guide-to-value-based-pricing) before, [the method](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/pricing-concept-the-value-based-pricing) involves setting a price by which the company calculates and tries to earn the differentiated economic value of its product for a particular customer segment when compared to its competitor. The utility of this method lies in its ability to shift the focus externally to the customer’s situation. But from the perspective of Dewey’s theory of valuation, its weakness arises from the fact that estimated benefits are treated as a static valuation separate from the customer’s changing appraisal.

Consider an industrial supplier that sells equipment expected to reduce a particular manufacturer’s operating expenses by \$100,000 annually. Conventionally, the supplier will use these expected savings as the foundation for quoting a value-based price to the manufacturer. But the manufacturer will need to consider various operational aspects beyond the supplier’s attention such as how much time will be required for installing the equipment (and how much this will cost), the changes in work processes that will need to be made when using the new equipment, the expense of training workers to use it, financing costs, and so on, all of which may make the actual value of accrued benefits significantly lower than \$100,000. Thus, the supplier’s estimate of economic value may not force fit onto the customer’s specific situation, and is only part of the customer’s appraisal of the purchase.

Furthermore, the way the total estimate is structured will also impact the buyer’s appraisal and the end-in-view. For instance, the requirement of a large up-front payment may use up the capital required for another project. A recurring charge may require a commitment that the buyer feels reluctant to make, and an outcome-based pricing component may shift the allocation of risk to the vendor without giving it control over how the equipment is used and raising questions about how the outcome will be measured. Each of these pricing schemes generates different appraisals and, in Dewey’s framework, a different valuation and WTP of the equipment. Here, again, we come to the circular relationship between price and customer value. The price, through the structure employed by the seller, influences the buyer’s appraisal and, along with other factors, valuation.

Thus, a seller who focuses exclusively on the outcome will overlook the resources consumed in achieving it and be misled into viewing the customer as more positive about the offer than the customer really is. An appraising customer, on the other hand, may reject the proposal, while acknowledging that the end is attractive in and of itself. Thus, Dewey’s work encourages us to consider both value as an input into pricing decisions and price as an input into customer valuation.

In practical terms, the value-based price calculation needs to distinguish between the differentiated benefits of the offering, the non-price requirements for the customer to realize these benefits, and the details of the proposed price structure. Considering all three, rather than only differentiated benefits, provides a more complete and accurate picture of the customer’s valuation. This requires cooperation and active participation by the buyer in specifying the conditions and assumptions, particularly those dealing with the use of the offering.

Second, Dewey’s framework pushes the seller to go a step beyond calculating a value-based price to consider alternative offers in addition to the strict value-based price. This may involve anything from alternative pricing structures to support for deploying the offering, a different sharing of risk with the seller, and so on. Obviously, each of these options will incur costs that should be taken into consideration, but will lead to a more attractive offer (which we may call a “value-based-plus” offer) that accounts for the customer’s valuation, driven by the interdependency between their means and ends. With Dewey’s framework, value-based pricing becomes a conditional pricing method that involves discussion rather than an absolute method that provides a price to quote to the customer.

### **Subscription price increases**

We can now return to the case of our friend Felicity and the video streaming service’s \$3 monthly price increase. The increase changes the price Felicity must pay, but, as discussed earlier, it also generates a specific condition that favors appraisal. Before the email, Felicity prized the service, now she appraises it. The nature of the relationship between the customer and the seller has changed fundamentally, and, in some sense, a WTP has emerged in Felicity’s mind.

Dewey’s conceptualization helps explain why seemingly modest price increases routinely provoke an outsized response from consumers. The issue is not whether \$3 per month is too much more to pay for the service, but a complete assessment of whether the service is worth the total price being paid. A \$2 per month increase would have elicited the same appraisal and response. The scope of appraisal is much more than a simple calculation of whether the price increase is worth it.

Furthermore, imagine that Felicity’s research on her prior viewing behavior revealed that she watched only a single documentary during the prior month. Such a finding does not necessarily mean that subscribing to the service is poor value. She may value having access to a well-regarded documentary as soon as it is released, or the comfort of knowing she has a library of amazing content at her disposal whenever she wants it. The point is that these things were not considered before when she prized the service; now that she is appraising it, these things become fodder for thought and evaluation. Thus, her continuing liking for the brand can well co-exist with an intention to cancel the service.

Felicity’s end-in-view will also affect her appraisal process. For instance, she may decide to rotate among different streaming services, subscribing to each to watch a specific movie or show. Alternatively, she may choose a cheaper tier or reduce the total number of subscriptions in her household using a non-compensatory decision rule (e.g., “I will not sign on for more than one service at a time). In these cases, the streaming service is competing on different ends, with different WTPs as means; the competition for Felicity’s patronage is far more serious than one service at one price competing against another at another price.

For sellers using subscriptions, the core implication is that a price increase, even if it seems small, is potentially an event that prompts customers to move from prizing to appraisal, with the attendant overall evaluation. Framing the research issue as the acceptability of an incremental price increase is the wrong thing to do if we are to take Dewey’s framework into account, and the risks associated with even minor price increases are higher than conventionally assumed. This issue becomes particularly significant for companies whose customers are automatically charged over long periods and settle into an inertial relationship with the company.

This discussion also suggests a reframing of price-change communication. The normal approach is to downplay the price increase by using euphemisms and being pithy. However, this may be exactly the wrong tack to take if it prompts the customer to take a broader appraisal of the service’s value. Even small price increases may need to be presented within an information-rich missive that describes the core benefits delivered by the service, provides detailed comparisons to alternative services, and offers options to upgrade or downgrade one’s subscription plan. Obviously, this does not ensure that the appraising customer will continue their relationship, but it does support the customer’s appraisal process.

For some customers at least, the appraisal may lead to a confirmation of the value they are receiving, converting the passive customer into someone who becomes an advocate. The powerful insight derived from applying Dewey’s valuation framework to subscription pricing is that the provider should not think of the customer’s decision to cancel versus retain the subscription as dictated by a static WTP, but rather by a larger, multidimensional assessment of the ends they are seeking and the means by which the service delivers them.

### **Conclusion**

Nearly ninety years after it was developed, Dewey’s distinction between prizing and appraising and the integration of means with ends in his theory of valuation provide an enriched understanding of customer value and WTP. As we saw with the application of Dewey’s framework to four contemporary pricing issues spanning the customer-seller relationship, prizing is associated with loyalty and price tolerance but lacks a well-formed WTP and is susceptible to change as soon as an appraisal occurs. When appraisal does occur, the buyer shifts to a holistic assessment of the offering and to formulating a WTP tied to the specific end(s) chosen. In light of this shift, each of the issues I considered here, from WTP research for a new product to a contract renewal, from the calculation of a value-based price to an increase in a subscription price, engenders an occasion for appraisal to kick in, with different evidence, motivations, and consequences in each case. The WTP estimate derives meaning only in relation to the buyer’s specific circumstances. In the end, Dewey’s theory of valuation tells us that not only does WTP not exist for most buyers most of the time as a concrete number that can be articulated and explained, but even when it is formed and expressed, it is constructed within the appraisal context, is contingent on the goals, motivations, and circumstances of the buyer, and is susceptible to revision with experience.
