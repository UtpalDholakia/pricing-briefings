##### ***Summary.*** Outcome-based pricing is premised on a straightforward promise to customers: “pay only for performance.” But for vendors, that promise is tantamount to an insurance policy. They charge a fixed premium for results while absorbing uncertain and sometimes correlated costs. To make this price structure sustainable, AI vendors must screen customers, customize the price, verify outcomes, control costs, and move toward hybrid pricing contracts that limit exposure.

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/bcbe7d6b-1c00-4b9c-b265-cbaeb89d79a8_1672x941.png)

A growing number of AI vendors have recently adopted, or are seriously considering, [outcome-based pricing](https://a16z.com/newsletter/december-2024-enterprise-newsletter-ai-is-driving-a-shift-towards-outcome-based-pricing/), mainly because [traditional SaaS price structures don’t fit AI well](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/ai-pricing-has-a-serious-customer). The benefits of outcome-based pricing for customers are relatively clear-cut: they pay only when the AI agent delivers its claimed benefit, reducing risk from adoption. However, the vendor’s side of the equation is murkier. With outcome-based pricing, the AI vendor promises a fixed price for a result whose production costs it cannot control or fully predict. For instance, when priced per completed resolution, every resolved ticket is billed at the same price, but the computation cost for one resolution may be a small fraction of that for another.

When setting outcome-based prices, the vendor is, in effect, *writing an insurance policy* for its customer. The price charged per outcome is the insurance premium, the computational resources consumed to reach that outcome are analogous to the insurance claim, and like any insurer that prices its policies carelessly, without properly understanding the risk it is taking on, an AI vendor can design an outcome-based price structure that appears to be profitable in theory, but may lose money (and a lot of it) in practice. In this piece, I want to explore outcome-based pricing for AI products from the vendor’s perspective, focusing on the risks for vendors, the natural tensions between customers and vendors under this pricing structure, and lessons AI vendors using outcome-based pricing can learn from the insurance industry.

### Outcome-based pricing in context

It is tempting to call this a brand-new AI-centric pricing structure, but the fact is, businesses have paid for results for a long time. For instance, lawyers work on contingency, salespeople on commission, advertisers on cost per acquisition, and outsourcing firms on shared savings. Paying for outcomes is a well-established and widely used price structure in many industries.

There are a number of similarities between how outcome-based pricing is applied in these settings versus how it is applied for AI products. In these arrangements, the seller assumes financial risk in pursuit of an outcome it does not fully control. For instance, a contingency lawyer who accepts a case may spend years and hundreds of thousands of dollars on discovery, experts, and trial in pursuit of an uncertain payoff. Similarly, an AI vendor that charges per resolution carries the cost of every resolution attempt while collecting only on the successes. In both cases, outcome uncertainty creates financial risk for the seller.

***Customer selection.*** There are at least two significant risks that make outcome-based pricing challenging for AI products. The first is the risk from customer selection. Contingency law firms protect themselves by using a comprehensive process to screen cases before taking them on, accepting only those they expect to win. Similarly, performance-based outsourcing firms scope, qualify, and price the engagement before taking it on, declining deals where their services won’t generate sufficient savings. In contrast, an AI vendor today will typically publish a flat rate (e.g., \$0.99 per resolution at Intercom, \$2 per conversation at Salesforce), making it available to any customer. There is no screening or vetting process; consequently, any number of unsuitable customers may find themselves affiliated with the vendor (to the vendor’s detriment).

***Correlated behavior in the customer portfolio.*** The second is the risk of correlated behavior in the customer portfolio. A law firm may take on many cases at once, with little connection to one another, allowing it to spread financial risk across a portfolio of revenue-generating customers. In contrast, an AI vendor’s customers often show significant correlations in factors that impact costs. For instance, when a customer’s website goes down or it changes a billing policy, thousands of users may contact the AI agent concurrently about the same difficult problem, leading to a spike in the cost to serve each user. This type of risk can also propagate across customers, such as when a tax software vendor’s clients spike usage before a filing deadline, or when a vendor serving retailers experiences peaks at seasonal times, akin to an insurer’s nightmare of [large swaths of houses burning down in the same fire](https://www.latimes.com/environment/story/2025-12-16/los-angeles-fires-push-insurers-disaster-losses-to-107-billion-for-2025). The vendor’s own infrastructure or product strategy can amplify this risk, for example, when an underlying model is upgraded and begins reasoning more expensively, leading to a rise in the cost per outcome across the entire customer portfolio at the same time. Complicating this fact is that many of the AI vendors’ costs are difficult to predict given the evolving technology and significant lumpy fixed costs such as those associated with data centers coming online.

### The counterintuitive consequences of superior performance

Considering customer selection risk, in any outcome-based pricing application, the seller can survive a high average cost per delivered outcome, as long as the average cost is stable and built into the price. For example, at the moment, an AI support agent earns the same fee (\$0.99) for resetting a password in 30 seconds (\$0.05 in compute costs) as it does for solving a complicated billing dispute with an obstinate user requiring the AI model to take dozens of steps to work through (\$1.20 in compute costs), even though the cost associated with the second resolution is many times the first. As long as the vendor has a good sense of the proportions of easy (i.e., cheap) versus difficult (i.e., expensive) resolutions likely to occur for a customer, its outcome-based price will be effective.

What can undermine the pricing model and make it unsustainable is a realized mix of outcomes that is worse (i.e., more expensive to deliver) than the planned proportions. Both issues discussed in the previous section, customer selection and correlated behavior in the customer portfolio, can exacerbate this issue. For example, the AI vendor may have sold to customers whose work is harder than it assumed (the selection problem), or the hard cases may arrive together rather than spread out (the correlation problem). There is a further cost risk peculiar to AI that neither selection nor correlation captures. It puts the outcome-based price structure in tension with the AI product marketing.

Specifically, the features that AI vendors advertise the most, such as more thorough reasoning, more careful checking, and more use of external tools, are the ones that tend to consume more computing resources and generate higher costs. Thus, a vendor that offers a smarter agent may also have a more expensive agent, and when marketplace prices are bound by competition and customer reference (e.g., \$0.99 to \$2 per resolution), the added cost becomes a burden rather than a differentiator. Consequently, and counterintuitively, using an outcome-based price structure can lead to lower margins for the AI vendor with the superior product in the marketplace.

### The impacts of adverse customer selection and moral hazard

As noted, the lack of customer screening by AI vendors can work to the vendor’s disadvantage, attracting the least desirable customers who are high-cost to serve. A well-functioning company with a comprehensive, well-populated database, up-to-date documentation, and an efficient customer service team is well-placed to effectively adopt AI agents into its workflow. Such companies have little incentive to prefer pay-for-performance and may instead prefer a flat pricing model, confident they can extract value. In contrast, a company struggling with its service and facing other issues, such as tangled workflows, outdated knowledge, heavy compliance demands, or a disproportionate number of unhappy customers, will have a greater incentive to prefer “pay only when it works” offers. This allows them to transfer some of the burden of serving their customers to the AI vendor.

The result is that the outcome-based pricing model attracts high-cost-to-serve customers to a greater extent, because it appeals more to customers who are having difficulties. This is the underwriter’s oldest hazard. The eager applicant is eager for a reason, and the reason is often detrimental to the insurer (if not carefully recognized and managed). That said, outcome pricing also attracts sophisticated buyers who value certainty over variability, as customer service AI agent provider Sierra’s (founded by former Salesforce co-CEO Bret Taylor) early enterprise customers, such as ADT, Cigna, and Rivian, illustrate. Selection, therefore, reflects risk appetite as much as operational maturity, which is why underwriting matters.

A second behavioral shift occurs after the customer signs the contract. When a buyer pays a fixed amount per outcome, it no longer bears any cost from sending the AI agent messier, harder work. This means that the expense of keeping its knowledge base current, cleaning up its database, and so on, no longer makes as much sense and may take a back seat with some customers. In a nutshell, when the customer is insured against the cost of complexity, complexity becomes easier to ignore. This is the same reason an insured driver parks a little less carefully or speeds a little too much. Thus, under a flat-rate price structure, the vendor’s and customer’s incentives are perfectly aligned with respect to effort and costs; under outcome-based pricing, they are no longer aligned with respect to the effort and costs required to produce the outcome.

### Who decides the outcome’s validity?

Continuing with the insurance analogy, outcome-based pricing turns the definition of the outcome into a claims process. An insurer considers an adverse event in detail, asking such questions as whether the event is covered by the policy, whether an exclusion applies, and whether the evidence of loss is sufficient to justify paying the claim. In the same way, the resolution of a conversation or the generation of a qualified lead still needs verification, because of the subjectivity involved in what counts as a resolution or a qualified lead. Some entity, AI or (in rare cases) human, must verify every claimed outcome, or at least audit a sample of outcomes.

The best practice on this front is evolving, but the details in this respect are interesting. Consider the example of the customer service provider Intercom[^1], which charges \$0.99 per outcome and carefully defines each outcome. A resolution is said to have occurred when the customer confirms the issue is solved, or if they simply stop asking for help after the agent answers. This second condition, which Intercom calls an “assumed resolution,” occurs when the satisfied customer and the customer who gave up both fall silent and [look identical in the data](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/200963005). Intercom has also [included a clawback clause](https://www.intercom.com/help/en/articles/8205718-fin-ai-agent-outcomes) in its outcome-based price structure. If a customer’s user returns to a conversation that was already marked as resolved (and paid for by the customer), the original charge is reversed. This is very much like an insurer reopening a settled claim when new evidence becomes available. Finally, the company’s Fin for Sales AI agent costs \$10 per qualified lead, with the explicit understanding that the customer, not the AI agent, defines what qualified means.

This brief case study illustrates an important aspect of an effective outcome-based price structure. While defining the outcome at the outset, the vendor needs to engineer some degree of flexibility into the definition to accommodate variability across customers, and the common need to verify for the whole base. At the same time, they need to be ready to adjust the outcome definition or the price to respond to cost constraints. For example, a simple fix for the \$0.99-per-resolution price tag would be to limit the number of steps the AI agent can take, thereby imposing a ceiling on costs. As compute costs change, so can this constraint. This is the tension inherent in any outcome-based price structure: the need to be responsive to customer needs and provide flexibility versus the need to limit financial risk by imposing constraints that will reduce customer value.

### Three lessons from insurers for AI outcome-based pricing success

The discussion so far has focused on the various reasons AI vendors are cautious about outcome-based pricing. However, that is not to say that this cannot be a successful, highly effective price structure for AI products. Even today, some companies rely on outcome-based pricing as their core price structure and are doing extraordinarily well. As one example, Sierra, mentioned earlier, has used outcome-based pricing from the outset and reached \$100 million in annual recurring revenue within about two years. Bret Taylor, its founder, argues that paying for a job well done is simply the right way to sell software, the way a salesperson earns a commission rather than a fee for showing up[^2].

To succeed with outcome-based pricing, vendors need to learn three lessons from insurers that are competent. First, scale, in the form of a large, diverse customer base, is likely to greatly increase the likelihood of success with this price structure. Having a large, diverse customer portfolio will let the AI vendor spread its risk, so that a few costly-to-serve accounts, an external shock, or a bad period do not ruin it completely. Thus, Sierra’s success notwithstanding, outcome-based pricing may be better suited to mature, established vendors selling AI products than to startups using it as a launch price structure.

Second, vendors will need to move from simple, uniform, outcome-based price offers to more complex, customized price offers. It would make little sense for State Farm Insurance to charge the same price for auto insurance to an 18-year-old teenage boy driving a custom-built Honda Civic as a 57-year-old woman driving a Volvo XC90. Insurance companies routinely implement highly personalized, risk-based pricing models that take hundreds of customer variables into account. In the same way, it makes little sense to charge the same \$0.99 per resolution to a customer whose users encounter a lot of difficult challenges and one whose users mostly call in for password resets. A practical starting point is a light underwriting checklist including factors such as historical ticket complexity mix, knowledge base freshness, peak-to-average volume ratio, and expected tool calls per resolution, rather than a single published rate. Where a one-price-fits-all outcome-based price structure falls apart easily, a customized outcome-based pricing approach will be resilient and sustainable for AI vendors.

Third, and perhaps most demanding, the vendor has to treat the cost of producing each outcome as something to be actively monitored and driven down. When using other price structures, such as per-seat or per-token pricing, a rise in compute cost can be passed on to the customer. Under outcome-based pricing, the price is fixed, so the vendor owns the entire cost curve, which can cut both ways: every dollar shaved off the cost of a resolution goes straight to the bottom line, and conversely, every dollar added comes straight out of it. This is the AI vendor’s version of an insurer’s loss ratio, defined as the cost of a claim relative to the premium charged. Here, the AI vendor has an advantage over an insurer. An insurer can rarely reduce the size of a claim once the event has occurred; it can only price the risk and try to influence the policyholder’s behavior. In contrast, the AI vendor has multiple avenues to bring down costs. It can route easy queries to smaller, cheaper models and reserve the expensive frontier model for genuinely hard cases. It can cache and reuse computation rather than paying for it again. It can redesign the agent’s workflow to reach the same outcome in fewer steps.

### Why hybrid price structures might be more effective than pure outcome-based structures to price AI

As outcome-based pricing is adopted more widely, it begins to lose its simplicity. A vendor that pools risk across a large, diverse portfolio of customers, moves towards personalized pricing that accounts for differences in cost-to-serve, and consistently drives down costs starts to look more and more like a well-managed insurance company. A prudent insurer never writes an uncapped policy, but pure outcome-based pricing, charging a flat fee per outcome without a floor or ceiling, is exactly that.

This is why many outcome-based pricing applications settle into hybrid pricing structures, defined here as contracts that combine a fixed component with a variable one, rather than pricing on outcomes alone. In AI agreements, this usually takes the form of a recurring platform fee, often paired with an allowance of included outcomes and a minimum commitment, on top of which the customer pays a variable charge for outcomes, actions, or credits beyond the allowance.

A hybrid pricing structure benefits customers and vendors. Customers get more predictable bills, which supports their budgeting. Vendors can limit their exposure to the financial risks discussed earlier. Specifically, a platform fee provides the vendor with a base premium that it retains regardless of the number of outcomes. An included allowance absorbs ordinary usage before incremental charges begin, much like a deductible. A minimum commitment creates a floor for revenue. Usage-based charges can be employed judiciously to handle the cases where the customer’s activity or complexity exceeds what the vendor is willing to absorb inside the base fee.

Salesforce’s evolution with Agentforce, which I discussed in detail in [another piece](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/outcome-based-pricing-in-practice), shows the drift toward a hybrid pricing structure. It began with a flat price of \$2 per conversation, which made an individual conversation the unit of pricing. It had the benefit of simplicity, but it burdened Salesforce with the risks of customer selection and correlated behavior described earlier. The company then introduced Flex Credits, charging per action, explicitly distinguishing between light tasks that cost little and heavy ones that cost more. This shifted the cost of the hardest cases back toward the customer who generated them. Furthermore, Salesforce also offers conventional per-user licensing for those comfortable with a traditional access-based price structure. Together, offering these different options concurrently gives the vendor greater control over how much risk it is willing to carry, where it wants to meter usage, and where it wants a fixed revenue base.

This is the central tension in using pure outcome-based pricing as it stands now. On the one hand, the power of this price structure for AI products lies in its conceptual simplicity: pay only when it works. But on the other hand, such simplicity can be very risky, and the steps that the vendor takes to protect itself, from minimum outcome volumes, tiers, and allowances, to audits, clawbacks, and decomposed usage units, erode the simplicity, until the contract no longer resembles the simple proposition that was most appealing to the customer. For now and the foreseeable future, the core challenge of using outcome-based pricing for AI products will be accurately accounting for and pricing all the risks involved in charging only for results.
