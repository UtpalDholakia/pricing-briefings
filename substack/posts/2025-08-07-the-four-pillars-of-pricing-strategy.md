When making pricing decisions using a structured process laid out in the [Value Pricing Framework](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/the-value-pricing-framework-updated), you should consider four factors carefully. We will call these factors the “four pillars of pricing” because each one provides an essential input for effective pricing decisions. In this piece, I will briefly introduce the four pillars for someone starting on their pricing journey. Each one is considered at length in the numerous pieces written on this Substack. The four pillars of pricing are shown in the figure below. As shown, the four pillars arise from the organization’s marketing strategy and provide the foundation for it to have an effective pricing strategy. Many managers and entrepreneurs pay attention to one or two of them (costs are usually considered), but ignore the others, leading to a suboptimal pricing decision.

*The four pillars of pricing strategy*

### Costs

Costs are the most commonly recognized factor in a pricing strategy and they usually set the floor on prices. In organizations large and small, a majority of prices are set based *only*, or mainly, using costs. When looking at costs from an accounting mindset, a distinction is made between fixed costs and variable costs, such that variable costs change with the level of the activity (e.g., material costs) while fixed costs remain unchanged irrespective of activity level (e.g., rent). When considering costs from a pricing strategy, however, a different demarcation is needed. In our world, costs are separated into *incremental costs* that are relevant to the pricing decision and need to be considered carefully, and *non-incremental or irrelevant costs* that should be ignored. Understanding each of these costs and how they will change with the pricing decision is *the* critical task for the pricing decision maker. In addition to setting the floor on average prices over the longer term, in many cases, costs can also serve as the centerpiece of the company’s marketing strategy.

### Customer Value

The second pillar of pricing is customer value. It is the total amount of money that the customer is willing to pay for functional and hedonic benefits received from the product. Each of the product’s many features potentially provides customer benefits. Customer value quantifies them by mapping the product’s features to benefits, and benefits to the willingness to pay. Note that the willingness to pay concept is complex, and other posts on this Substack cover this complexity. Here, we keep things simple: Customer Value is simply a strict correspondence of the customer benefits into dollars and cents.

For a pricing decision, customer value is a significant pillar because it is unrelated to costs and therefore offers a unique data point about viable prices and helps to generate a higher bound on prices that customers consider worth paying. It also provides guidance on how to increase customer value in economically beneficial ways. Strictly speaking, the customer value sets the ceiling or the highest possible price that can be charged for the product because obviously, a price higher than the customer’s willingness to pay will be untenable.

### Reference Prices

Customers rarely make purchase decisions or evaluate prices in isolation or in absolute terms. When judging a price and considering the product’s purchase, they compare it to other reference prices. Commonly used reference prices include competitors’ prices, the company’s historical prices, and other prices that the customer may encounter before purchase. Reference prices play dual roles of creating a frame of reference and helping the customer to form a range of prices that are deemed reasonable for the product. Staying within this range of reasonable prices is an important consideration because customers tend to focus less on price and more on non-price attributes when prices are maintained within this range. The second role of reference prices is to persuade customers.

Considering this pillar provides the range of prices customers find reasonable as a third data point for pricing decisions. Perceptive marketers can also influence which reference prices customers use and how they interpret them.

### **The Value Proposition**

As shown in the figure, every pricing decision must be grounded in the company’s marketing strategy and serve to advance and support its strategic objectives. The company’s pricing must be consistent with the rest of its marketing activities. A value proposition is the formal expression of the firm’s marketing strategy, describing the significance of the product’s unique characteristics relative to competitors. By articulating what the company considers its most important points of differentiation, a value proposition provides guidelines and sets constraints for pricing activities. It determines how costs, customer value, and reference prices are weighted in the pricing decision.

To summarize, in the **[Value Pricing Framework](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/the-value-pricing-framework-updated)**, costs set the floor (or the minimum limit) on prices. Customer value sets the ceiling (or the upper bound) on prices, and reference prices provide the range of reasonable prices as well as a means of influencing customer decisions. The product’s price cannot be too far away from prices for comparable products, and from its previous prices. The fourth factor, the value proposition, determines the relative weights given to costs, customer value, and reference prices in the pricing decision.

A core idea of the writings on this Substack is that instead of relying on just one or two factors in a casual or tradition-bound way, the pricing decision maker must consider all four factors carefully and thoroughly when making a pricing decision. When the four pillars are considered carefully and used appropriately, they increase the chances of making informed pricing decisions and enjoying positive outcomes. Together, the four pillars provide the parameters within which to make pricing decisions. Once the roles played by each factor are well understood, the pricing task becomes a clearer, more manageable, and repeatable process.
