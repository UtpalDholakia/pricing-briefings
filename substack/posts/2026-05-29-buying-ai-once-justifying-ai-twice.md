##### 

##### *Summary.* As 2026 renewal deadlines approach, many enterprise AI contracts will be judged less by hype and more by the performance metrics and the politics within the organization. In this post, I argue that renewal decisions will not unambiguously reveal whether AI tools created customer value. Instead, the factors that determine which AI contracts will be renewed include loss aversion, sunk-cost reasoning, subscription structural features that reduce friction, extreme usage (or non-usage) metrics, and the current composition of the buying center.

#### *“CEOs to Keep Spending on AI, Despite Spotty Returns” – [WSJ article headline](https://www.wsj.com/tech/ai/ceos-to-keep-spending-on-ai-despite-spotty-returns-2eaeb6b9) by Ben Glickman.*

##### *“CIOs face challenges in proving the value from AI investments and demonstrate tangible business outcomes…Aligning AI initiatives with strategic business objectives is the essential step for success.” – John-David Lovelock of Gartner, quoted in a [Financial Times article](https://markets.ft.com/data/announce/detail?dockey=600-202605190330BIZWIRE_USPRX____20260519_BW405832-1).*

### Three (Divergent?) Perspectives on the Approaching AI Renewal Cliff

Riding the AI wave, companies spent an [extraordinary amount of money](https://menlovc.com/perspective/2025-the-state-of-generative-ai-in-the-enterprise/) on AI tools and services in 2025. Many CIOs and newly minted [CAIOs](https://www.ibm.com/think/topics/chief-ai-officer) yielded to pressure to “do something about AI,” and wrote large checks to Anthropic, OpenAI, Microsoft, and Google, not to mention numerous specialized vendors. Not only that, a significant number of these contracts were [fast-tracked, bypassing the due diligence](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2025-11-19-gartner-identifies-critical-genai-blind-spots-that-cios-must-urgently-address0?utm_source=chatgpt.com) normally conducted by procurement and finance teams. Many will come up for renewal in 2026 and beyond.

Whether to renew, and on what terms, will be decided based on a year or two of usage data and other performance indicators. This time around, many companies will make a more careful, less hype-driven financial and operational assessment of AI tools and services. This raises an important question about the customer value of AI for adopters:

***Will the 2025 AI spending boom translate into durable perceptions of customer value, or will it slow among early adopters as the consensus shifts toward questioning the value delivered by AI tools and services?***

Recent discussions about how renewals will unfold have been influenced by several conflicting reports, of which I will consider three here. First, Menlo Ventures, whose annual [State of Generative AI in the Enterprise](https://www.globenewswire.com/news-release/2025/12/09/3202258/0/en/Menlo-Ventures-2025-State-of-Generative-AI-Report-Enterprise-Investment-Hit-37B-in-2025-Tripling-in-One-Year.html) report is among the most followed benchmarks of corporate AI adoption, reported that enterprise generative AI spending increased from \$11.5 billion in 2024 to \$37 billion in 2025. It also noted that 47% of AI deals reached production in 2025, nearly twice the conversion rate of traditional SaaS pilots. The second report provided a more pessimistic perspective.

In [The GenAI Divide: State of AI in Business 2025](https://aimagazine.com/news/mit-why-95-of-enterprise-ai-investments-fail-to-deliver), MIT’s NANDA initiative argued that the vast majority of enterprise generative AI initiatives were failing to deliver measurable business impact. The headline finding was that only about 5% of custom enterprise AI tools reached production or produced a rapid revenue impact for the organization. The third report, from Bessemer Venture Partners, was the [AI Pricing and Monetization Playbook](https://www.bvp.com/atlas/the-ai-pricing-and-monetization-playbook). It was bullish over the longer term, but warned that 2025’s “adoption at all costs” buying environment would lead to a 2026 “renewal cliff” as contracts come up for review and adopters are asked internally to justify their spending relative to the value delivered by AI services.

These assessments only seem to contradict each other if we assume that an organization’s contract renewal decision relies solely or primarily on whether the adopted service has delivered measurable financial value. In practice, renewal decisions are made by decision makers who must juggle many considerations beyond ROI. They include the cost and disruption of switching services or vendors (especially when the recent initial adoption was so enthusiastic), the learning curve associated with changing tracks and going with an alternative, the difficulty of justifying a change when competitors have visibly committed to the same category (the so-called benchmarking costs), and the tendency of approved budget lines to persist into the next cycle.

Additionally, there are intra-organizational political and cultural factors at play, including the potential reputational damage to internal champions who pushed for the original purchase and the many PR downsides of admitting the failure of a high-profile investment. When these non-ROI pressures are strong, the organization will renew its AI services contracts, regardless of whether these services have contributed to its financial performance. If they have, great. If not, the conclusion will be that they will start to do so in a year or two.

This logic is consistent with psychological research on consumer contracts, which, although conducted outside the domain of enterprise software or AI, can be easily extrapolated to consider its implications for the renewal cliff. This research finds that customers remain in contracts they no longer use because exiting requires actions they would rather avoid. Canceling requires navigating a process that vendors have often designed to be friction-laden, and it also often involves acknowledging to oneself that the original purchase no longer makes sense.

This pressure is amplified in enterprise contexts, where many buyers may reasonably argue that complex software adoption takes longer than a year to bear fruit and use this to rationalize their continued commitment to underperforming investments. Individually, the resistance from each source is small, but when aggregated across the adopter base, it can lead to renewal rates much higher than a narrower ROI-focused evaluation of the adopted product would predict.

### The Psychology of Renewal Decisions

In applications of pricing strategy to renewal decisions, such as working out unit economics for price negotiations in B2B or for designing promotional renewal offers in consumer services, the customer’s renewal process is implicitly viewed as a continuation of the original purchase. (Take, for instance, McKinsey’s Customer Decision Journey, where the customer circles around in a loyalty loop, bypassing “consider” and “evaluate” steps[^1]). The customer is seen as making the same evaluation (or, more accurately, re-evaluation) of the AI service relative to current competitor offerings, factoring in performance to date, realistic future expectations, sunk costs, and so on, and then making the renewal decision. In pricing models, this decision making process is captured by variables like the churn rate and the (changed) cost to serve. But once we take customer psychology into account, renewal turns out to be different from customer acquisition in several significant ways worth understanding.

***B2B loss aversion.*** The first mechanism is loss aversion, although the concept is often invoked too loosely. In theory, loss aversion can be pithily explained as “losses loom larger than gains,” but in a renewal setting, an important question is what losses are we talking about? At first blush, it could be fears about losing access to the AI product. However, on deeper consideration, two different losses are competing for the customer’s attention. One is the loss of access to the product that is already starting to get embedded in the organization’s workflows, reports, routines, integrations, and KPIs. The other is the loss associated with continuing to pay for the benefits that are not currently being fully or efficiently utilized. (We can pinpoint other emotional losses as well, but for the sake of tractability, we will ignore them in this discussion.) Which loss dominates the renewal decision depends on which is more relevant and available at the time of the decision, and this, in turn, may depend on a number of organization-specific contextual factors.

For those within the organization who actually work with the AI product, the more salient loss will be disruption to their work. Canceling the contract essentially means retraining employees, rebuilding workflows, explaining the reversal, and potentially admitting that the original adoption narrative, whatever it was, did not follow through. For those looking at the adoption decision through the [lens of corporate finance](https://www.cfodive.com/news/rising-ai-software-costs-cfos-middle-tropic/812089/) or procurement, the salient loss will be financial, framed in terms of standard ROI, with the organization’s specific calculus used to calculate costs and returns. It is worth noting that these losses are uncorrelated and, in many cases, may even compete. Users may fear giving up something they use every day that helps them do their work, while overseers may feel discomfort continuing with something that is not delivering financially as expected.

***Sunk-cost reasoning.*** The second relevant psychological mechanism when considering renewal decisions is sunk-cost reasoning, which reinforces the first. The 2025 executive who championed the original AI purchase invested not only their budget in its adoption, but also their political capital, reputation, and organizational attention. Canceling the contract will be read as an admission that the original judgment was wrong, as being knocked out, as striking out, as fumbling the ball, as getting clean bowled, or as taking a punch on the chin. (The metaphor will vary by managerial sport of choice.)

The academic literature on sunk-cost reasoning is unambiguous about what happens next. Classic research on the escalation of commitment has demonstrated that decision makers systematically commit more resources to a chosen course of action when they were personally responsible for the original decision than when someone else had made the initial choice[^2]. This escalation is based on self-justification rather than economic reasoning. A subsequent meta-analysis that included 105 escalation-of-commitment studies identified four conditions under which the effect is strongest: (1) when the original decision was made publicly, (2) when the decision maker was personally responsible for the choice, (3) when the project felt close to producing the expected outcomes, and (4) when performance feedback was ambiguous enough to allow multiple interpretations[^3].

All four conditions apply to the typical 2025 enterprise AI purchase. The deals were typically [announced at all-hands meetings](https://www.tomshardware.com/tech-industry/artificial-intelligence/shopify-ceo-teams-must-prove-ai-cant-do-a-task-before-asking-for-new-hires-resources) and in board materials. They were [championed by named executives](https://completeaitraining.com/news/jpmorgans-agentic-ai-rollout-with-openai-and-anthropic/) whose roles were directly identified with the decision. They were positioned as [transformations that would produce results over time](https://consumergoods.com/coca-cola-sparks-ai-transformation-11b-microsoft-cloud-expansion) rather than immediately. And the performance data through 2026 is ambiguous and “in progress,” with usage scattered among self-selected enthusiasts and outcomes that are difficult to attribute specifically to the AI tool. Even when things aren’t going well, it’s far too early to call the initiative a failure.

A small number of organizations will arrive at renewal with the kind of evidence that resolves this ambiguity in the AI tool’s (and the champion’s) favor. Recently, the casual dining chain [Red Robin credited ChatGPT on its first-quarter earnings call](https://www.restaurantbusinessonline.com/financing/red-robin-reports-better-profits-thanks-chatgpt) with helping managers improve scheduling and food-cost decisions, and for contributing to a 50-basis-point expansion in restaurant-level margins to a 5-year high. The stock rose more than 21% in the days that followed. Earnings-call moments like this one represent the renewal champion’s ideal scenario, since the financial outcome has been externally validated and the narrative has already been delivered by someone more senior than the champion.

Most champions of 2025 AI purchases will not have a story like Red Robin’s to tell. They will instead have to face the sunk-cost predicament directly. Renewing absorbs the cost of an investment whose results are ambiguous. Canceling makes the ambiguity itself a public referendum on the original judgment. The behavioral literature is clear about which choice most decision makers will make under these conditions, and it explains a substantial share of the renewals that will go through in 2026, regardless of underlying customer value.

***Behavioral persistence.*** The third mechanism is the one almost no one in enterprise AI is discussing directly. Many organizations made the original 2025 purchase with a sense of urgency (so as not to get left behind) and projected how they would use the AI services with demos, pilots, benchmarks, and modeling adoption results. The 2026 renewal decision will be made for them with less than a year of observed usage data. In theory, having insufficient evidence should make buyers more disciplined and cautious. But in practice, the presence of usage data introduces bias, and when the evidence is ambiguous or even negative, that bias is even greater.

To understand this point, consider well-cited research on how health club members use (and renew) gym memberships[^4]. In this study of nearly 8,000 members of three US clubs conducted over three years, the researchers found that members on month-to-month contracts were more likely to remain enrolled for more than 1 year than those on annual contracts, even though month-to-month contracts cost more and were theoretically easier to exit. The reason for this perplexing finding was the default structure. The authors concluded that the annual contract created an explicit decision point at expiration, whereas the month-to-month contract remained open-ended unless the customer actively canceled their membership. The contract that required less action persisted longer, even when usage did not justify the expense.

Note that this was in a category with far fewer switching costs than enterprise AI software. After all, you can walk into a new gym and quickly find the different machines and showers; this is not the case for a corporation switching from Anthropic to OpenAI (or vice versa). Many companies signed AI services contracts in 2025 that included auto-renewal clauses, notice windows, bundled commitments, and internal approval routines, all of which make it harder to cancel and easier to renew. So we can expect that the question companies will be asking at the time of renewal will not just be “Should we renew?” but “Is anyone in our company motivated, informed, and senior enough to stop the renewal from happening?” Once the various non-ROI factors I discussed earlier come into play, continuing with the current vendor becomes far more preferable, the path of least resistance, regardless of the financial impact so far.

### How Misprediction of Future Use Affects Renewal Decisions

The health club study also found that members systematically overpredicted their future use. They chose flat-fee contracts, assuming they would attend the gym frequently, when their actual attendance rates would have made it cheaper to pay per visit. This misprediction of future behavior is a common theme in consumer research. We tend to overpredict our future engagement with activities we believe are good for us and underpredict our engagement with activities we would be better off avoiding[^5].

Organizations are susceptible to a structurally similar bias. The most direct empirical evidence comes from research on technology adoption, where a study of 1,471 IT projects found an average cost overrun of 27%, with a long-tailed distribution in which one in six projects became what the researchers called “black swans,” with average cost overruns of 200% and schedule overruns of nearly 70%[^6]. This happened because organizational decision makers anchored on best-case scenarios based on the limited evidence at their disposal and failed to incorporate base rates from comparable projects. The same misprediction bias that drives cost and schedule overruns in IT project planning is also likely to affect assumptions about how broadly a deployed product will actually be used.

This logic is likely to play out in many organizations as they consider renewing AI services they’ve contracted with in 2025. A pilot of fifty employees generates positive feedback from a self-selected group of enthusiastic early adopters who volunteered to participate. The company’s decision makers extrapolate these results into seat licenses for 5,000 employees, implicitly assuming that success will diffuse throughout the workforce. In the larger pool, however, usage often runs significantly lower, and a meaningful number of seats may not be used every week.

This pattern is now starting to appear in the aggregate data and in specific cases in this space. SaaS management platforms have long observed that roughly 30% of paid software licenses in the average enterprise go unused, with [some industry data suggesting that figure can be significantly higher in large organizations](https://cafetosoftware.com/blog/why-30-of-saas-licenses-go-unused/). Early AI-specific examples appear to track at the high end of this baseline. A recent piece in [CIO](https://www.cio.com/article/4171887/the-real-ai-bottleneck-isnt-what-you-think.html) magazine described an organization that discovered 23 AI tools running across six departments, with three of the enterprise licenses having utilization rates below 8% before procurement stepped in and consolidated the subscriptions. The renewal moment is often the first time anyone in the buying organization is forced to confront these usage gaps, and the confrontation typically occurs in procurement rather than in the original buyer’s office. Bessemer’s own [State of AI 2025](https://www.bvp.com/atlas/the-state-of-ai-2025) benchmarking describes the fastest-growing AI vendor cohort (labeled “Supernovas”) as exhibiting “fragile retention metrics and incredibly thin gross margins.” Vendor-side fragility of this kind is consistent with a buyer base that is becoming more cautious as renewal approaches.

Another distinctive feature of B2B renewals is that the composition of the buying decision team often shifts between the initial purchase and the renewal[^7]. The CIO or CAIO who bypassed procurement under the 2025 “adoption at all costs” mandate will likely be joined by other decision makers at renewal, often including the company’s CFO. For instance, [Forrester predicts that](https://www.forrester.com/blogs/predictions-2026-ai-moves-from-hype-to-hard-hat-work/) CEOs will increasingly pull CFOs into AI decisions in 2026, in part because fewer than one-third of current decision makers can tie AI value to financial growth, and CEOs need a more rigorous assessment of the investment. The original champion may still favor renewal for the reasons described earlier in this post, but the newly arrived CFO may join the renewal assessment team with different evaluation criteria and none of the sunk-cost baggage of the champion, more able to focus on the core issue of whether the spending on AI is generating the expected outcomes. In this group decision making context, the AI renewal decision will become an internal negotiation between executives with different reputational stakes and evaluation criteria.

### Low-Friction Renewal Cliffs vs. Steep Renewal Cliffs

As the renewal cliff approaches for organizations, many renewals will be relatively easy decisions because the psychological and organizational factors discussed in the previous sections support continuation. Others will be harder because those same interactions between factors break down, indicating a more nuanced decision.

Among the factors supporting renewal, the primary one is the contract structure. Auto-renewal clauses with notice windows that require active cancellation push the decision toward continuation by inaction. This effect is bolstered as the AI tool or service becomes embedded in daily workflows and reporting routines, and B2B loss-aversion supports renewal. Support is further strengthened when the original champion remains in their role and is identified with the decision; in such circumstances, the reputational hit alone may be sufficient to sustain renewal through to the next period. And then obviously, as with Red Robin, if AI tool adoption already signals positive returns, the renewal decision is a no-brainer. Relatedly, when the workforce, e.g., developers, actively uses these tools daily, renewal is easy.

Renewal decisions are harder when the paid-for AI tools and services are underutilized, or when the buying center has shifted, for example, if the original champion has moved on from the role or no longer needs to defend the original decision. Without the burden of sunk costs, more in-depth and broader decisions will be made at the time of renewal, focusing on outcomes achieved so far and forthcoming in the next cycle. Another challenging situation is when the ROI of adoption is harder to isolate. (Those of us who work in marketing are very familiar with this challenge.) This is the case, for example, when AI tools operate in an advisory capacity and execution is left to the human partner. An example of this is general-purpose copilots sold into non-technical teams. Supporting these faultlines, [Gartner predicts](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2025-06-25-gartner-predicts-over-40-percent-of-agentic-ai-projects-will-be-canceled-by-end-of-2027) that over 40% of agentic AI projects will be canceled by the end of 2027 due to escalating costs, unclear business value, and inadequate risk controls.

The renewal cliff is likely to be steepest where the original purchase was framed as a multi-year transformation, but there is little evidence of results after a year or two of use. Coca-Cola’s [\$1.1 billion five-year partnership](https://www.consumergoods.com/coca-cola-sparks-ai-transformation-11b-microsoft-cloud-expansion) with Microsoft is a prototypical example of this type of framing, with the deal presented at signing as a strategic initiative whose value would compound over time. Two years in, it’s hard to determine whether the AI investment has delivered measurable value to Coca-Cola or its bottling network, based on publicly available information. But we can surmise that, unless the positive results have already begun to flow, the justification for compounding will become less and less tenable.

### **What the Renewal Data Will and Will Not Reveal**

Some of the contracts renewed in 2026 will reflect the actual value delivered to customers. The AI product will be used, embedded, and deemed as worth keeping by the organization. However, other renewals will be based on non-performance-related factors, such as internal champions who continue to defend the original adoption decision without losing face, CFOs and their teams that renegotiate the contract into a structure having greater accountability, procurement teams that miss or choose not to activate cancellation windows, and managers who decide that unwinding the AI tool would be more disruptive and demoralizing than continuing to pay for it. In many cases, there may be pricing adjustments, both in the amounts paid and [the price structure used](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/ai-pricing-has-a-serious-customer), that will be worth paying attention to.

In a few years, we will find out which contracts persisted, and in some cases, we will even learn about the forces behind the persistence. But on the whole, a high renewal rate could indicate either performance or other things such as workflow dependence, inertia, preference of certain constituents, contractual default, a successful reframing of a disappointing deployment, and the list goes on. Stated differently, repeated renewal does not provide automatic evidence of customer value.

Consequently, for analysts and investors trying to identify which AI products are actually being used in 2027 and delivering significant customer value, the renewal rate itself is likely to give incomplete and even misleading information. Looking at the signals behind the renewal, such as whether the contract was expanded, reduced, converted to usage-based pricing, tied to more objective or narrower outcomes, bundled onto a broader platform, or allowed to roll over by default, will be more insightful. Viewed through the lens of contract renewal, the test is less about what the AI product delivered than whether the organization can still defend its decision to keep paying for it.
