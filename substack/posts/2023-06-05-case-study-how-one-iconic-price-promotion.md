Many brand managers dissociate pricing strategy from the intellectual activity of building a brand. They see the price, and particularly price promotions, as a lever to manage unit sales, keep a handle on margin targets, maintain relations with the trade, and control inventory. The success of dollar stores notwithstanding, rarely do brand managers use price strategically for brand-building purposes. And even rarer is one price that becomes so well-known and associated with the brand, so iconic so to speak, that *the price is the brand’s centerpiece*, standing for and representing the brand’s core associations.

### **What is an iconic price?**

Back in the 1990s, in discussions of ***iconic prices***, one brand always came to mind. For many marketers, it was synonymous. The brand was *[Swatch](https://www.swatch.com/en-us/home)*, the Swiss mass-market watch brand.

Swatch was introduced by Swiss watch manufacturers in 1983 as a way to stem market share losses to more efficient, cheaper, and accessible Japanese and American watch brands. Over the next two decades, Swatch was credited for single-handedly turning the Swiss watch industry around, and for [changing the perception](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2017-11-21/how-swatch-started-a-revolution-history-of-fashion-watches?sref=d3S20v77) of a watch as a utilitarian tool or an heirloom to a fashion accessory.

Many [factors were responsible](https://www.mentalfloss.com/article/581875/swatch-watch-history) for Swatch’s success, but one, in particular, stood out: the ***[\$40 (50 CHF) price tag](https://hbr.org/1993/03/message-and-muscle-an-interview-with-swatch-titan-nicolas-hayek)*** that remained unchanged over a decade of growth. (Professor Youngme Moon has written an [excellent case](https://store.hbr.org/product/birth-of-the-swatch/504096) about Swatch’s marketing strategy that I’ve used in my graduate-level marketing classes in the past)

Swatch’s designer [Franco Bosisio called the \$40 price](https://store.hbr.org/product/birth-of-the-swatch/504096) “a simple price, a clean price” and “a mirror for other attributes we try to communicate” like [fun, approachability, fashion, impulsivity](https://www.mentalfloss.com/article/581875/swatch-watch-history), and provocativeness. In essence, the product itself along with the iconic price helped build a globally popular brand that lifted a sagging industry back to vibrancy.

Today, Swatch prices range [from \$55 to \$255](https://www.swatch.com/en-us/sw/), with many choices under \$100. The single iconic price has turned into a peculiar price range, but the pricing is still simple, approachable, and it syncs with the brand’s ethos.

### **An iconic price promotion**

> “There was no big television commercial, no big splash in the newspaper saying we were a cool place to be. There was only the big, blue coupon. The big, blue coupon was our brand.” - [Beth Grossfeld, Bed, Bath & Beyond Senior Marketing Manager](https://www.nytimes.com/2020/12/19/business/bed-bath-and-beyond-coupon.html).

If brand marketers rarely see price as a cornerstone of brand-building, they look at price promotions with downright suspicion. A common assertion that I’ll hear from brand managers is something along the lines of, “*The quickest way to erode my brand’s equity is to offer a discount or a sale without thinking of the collateral damage.*”

There’s a lot of truth in this assertion. Price promotions should be handled with care. But on the flip side, what makes pricing strategy so interesting is the exceptions to the rule, and the lessons that we can learn from the exceptional cases.

A glorious counterexample of the positive branding power of a price promotion is the iconic “20% off any one item” Bed Bath and Beyond coupon, also known as the [Big Blue coupon](https://www.retaildive.com/news/study-72-of-coupons-used-in-2016-affected-shopping-behavior/448387/). The promotion is no less powerful than the \$40 iconic Swatch price. The personal finance columnist Ron Lieber describes the [\$20 off coupon’s role in BB&B’s success as](https://www.nytimes.com/2020/12/19/business/bed-bath-and-beyond-coupon.html) “an oddball marketing achievement where the promotion became a stand-in for the brand itself.”

[Embedded post: https://twitter.com/joeDmarti/status/1025112211260235776](https://twitter.com/joeDmarti/status/1025112211260235776)

Price promotion has been a staple of the retailer, [almost from the moment of its inception in 1971](https://www.washingtonpost.com/business/2019/04/26/your-love-bed-bath-beyond-coupons-could-be-killing-retailer/). The company’s founders made a strategic decision to shun advertising and catalogs, and build consumer interest by offering infrequent but attractive coupons with different offers, which over time, transitioned to the Big Blue coupon.

Sending the coupon to a large number of consumers was the first step of a two-step strategy:

- **Step 1:** Give consumers a reason, any reason, to go to the store. (Because of its versatility, the Big Blue coupon accomplished this beautifully).

- **Step 2:** Once the consumer’s in the store, use clever merchandising to get them to buy impulsively across different categories and shop big baskets.

The strategy was hugely successful. In a [2015 story for Racked](https://www.racked.com/2015/2/26/8110031/bed-bath-beyond-home-goods-market), reporter Chavie Lieber described the experience of David Klar who went to BB&B with the intention to buy a shower curtain:

> “Weaving through Bed Bath’s aisles to get to the shower curtain, he was forced to walk past pretty much everything else in the store. Suddenly lost in a sea of fluffy towels, immersion blenders, and chip bag clips, Klar found everything he needed and plenty he didn't, walking out with a new frying pan, some wine glasses, a corkscrew, K-cups, steak knives, a Brita, and hangers. Oh, and that shower curtain.”

One retailing expert doesn’t think this will work. According to [Joe Keenan of Total Retail](https://www.mytotalretail.com/article/bed-bath-beyond-eliminating-coupons/):

> “Bed Bath & Beyond is trying to reverse years of eroding margins driven by its aggressive discounting policy with a move to a pay-for-play loyalty program, a la Amazon Prime. If my one-person survey here at home is any indication, it's not going to work. The discounting train, so to speak, has already left the station. There's no turning back now.”

I agree.

So what can we learn from BB&B’s iconic coupon? Here are four lessons.

### **Four Lessons for Brand Managers**

1\. [Price has symbolic value](https://www.amazon.com/Priced-Influence-Sell-Satisfy-Behavioral/dp/0999186736). A price or a price promotion can be iconic and become the brand’s core association if the manager chooses it carefully and persists with it. The “20% off one item” promotion is a significant contributor to the Bed Bath & Beyond brand, and the sole reason why many of its loyal customers shop there.

2\. A consistently executed promotional strategy can be very attractive to customers and create sustained differentiation in a way few other marketing actions can. For the brand manager, the key is to match the iconic price to the sensibilities of the target customer.

3\. Like B2B settings, the success of an iconic promotion hinges on ***[discount discipline.](https://hbr.org/2006/11/a-case-for-discount-discipline)*** Throughout its existence, the Achilles’ heel of the Big Blue coupon has been its lax implementation. Frontline employees routinely gave the discount when customers brought in expired coupons, or even when they didn’t have any coupons. Other practices like allowing customers to stack coupons also eroded the promotion’s integrity, and diluted its iconic status. We only have to visit the meme universe to discover how widely consumers know and mock the lax enforcement. Iconic price promotions may forge retail brands, but they make it difficult to consistently earn healthy margins.
