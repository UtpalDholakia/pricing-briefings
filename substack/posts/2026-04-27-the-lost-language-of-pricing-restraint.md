##### ***Summary.*** Large firms once treated pricing power as something to be exercised with restraint. In Robert Lanzillotti’s 1958 study of major American corporations, executives spoke about modest target returns, market share limits, and public responsibility. That language has largely disappeared. Today’s pricing vocabulary centers on value capture, monetization, and margin maximization. But customers still expect powerful firms to justify price increases, especially when inflation, scarcity, or unnatural dependence make them vulnerable. When these justifications fail, pricing power can quickly become a reputational, political, and regulatory problem.

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/cbcd0274-6255-4d5f-8caf-81b7161cc1eb_1672x941.png)

Whether you [agree](https://www.thebignewsletter.com/p/inflation-is-heating-up-and-its-due) with the existence of rampant greedflation, which is the idea that many companies have been taking advantage of the “[fog of global volatility](https://www.yesigiveafig.com/p/greedflation-yes-it-exists)” to boost their prices (and profits) [or not](https://ryanbourne.substack.com/p/excessive-spending-not-corporate), there is little doubt that most companies have a serious ongoing greedflation image problem. For example, polls over the past three years consistently find that large majorities of consumers believe price increases are attributable to corporate greed[^1].

As readers of [this blog](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/) well know, I have an enduring interest in searching out useful lessons for today’s pricing practitioners in historical pricing strategy writing (see my previous posts on the classic [Hall & Hitch (1939) paper](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/how-managers-made-pricing-decisions?) and [Ginzberg’s (1936) paper on Customary pricing](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/tracing-the-history-of-left-digit?)). Recently, I revisited Robert Lanzillotti’s (1958) paper “[Pricing Objectives in Large Companies](https://www.jstor.org/stable/1808156).” It’s a fantastic read. The paper reported a careful, multi-year investigation by Professor Lanzillotti into the pricing objectives adopted by the largest US companies, such as US Steel, General Motors, and Sears Roebuck, in the 1940s and 1950s, and the processes they used to set prices. I will focus on one finding from the paper, which has significant implications for the current greedflation debate.

Professor Lanzillotti found that, although attainable, many of the firms in his study deliberately refrained from pursuing the maximum margins or market shares their respective markets would allow them to earn over the study period. What’s more, they justified this volitional restraint as a kind of duty they owed to others. As Lanzillotti wrote:

> ##### “Because it is big the large firm envisages itself as a part of a socially integrated group, with responsibilities for the whole pipeline and production (including full-line offerings) and associated distribution. They see themselves in a continuing relationship not only with their own distributors, but even with dealers and ultimate customers, and with their suppliers—even when the latter lacked, or especially when they lacked, the bargaining power of a larger firm. The market, in effect, is regarded as a creature of the firm, and the firm has the responsibility for preserving these relationships and perpetuating its own position. The size of these firms also makes them an obvious target for antitrust suits, legislation, Congressional investigation, and similar restraining forces. To a certain extent, size thus entails a vulnerability and generates a sense of noblesse oblige.” (p. 936).

This perceived duty manifested in interesting ways. One U.S. Steel executive told Professor Lanzillotti that he was “unable to understand or properly describe the Corporation’s pricing policy except as something like the approach of the public utilities.” It is remarkable that a leader of the largest American steel company, at the height of American industrial power, was comparing his pricing policy to that of a regulated utility (in this case, entirely self-regulated). In today’s parlance, he was seeking out commodification when he didn’t need to. It seems virtually impossible to imagine, say, the CFO of Amazon or Google (or even Chevron) saying such a thing today, and even harder to imagine that they would stay employed for long if they did.

In this post, I want to explore how the selection of pricing objectives has evolved from the 1950s widely-held perspective of exercising restraint in pricing power because of a sense of duty, to today’s almost universal view among pricing decision makers, regardless of industry, to “maximize margins” and squeeze every last dollar of revenue and profit out of the customer’s wallet.

### **The Key Findings in Lanzillotti (1958) on Pricing Restraint**

Among the 20 firms included in the study, the average targeted after-tax return on investment was a modest 14 percent, with most companies setting ROI targets between 10 and 20 percent. Two things about these 1958 target ROIs are particularly interesting. First, these targets were actually used by the companies to set prices rather than being lowball values appropriated for PR purposes. The chosen targets directly influenced the pricing approaches used by these companies' managers, including the markup factors they applied to cost-plus-priced products or services. Second, these targets were relatively modest. Over the 1947–1955 period, the firms in the study generally achieved them and, in some cases, exceeded them. Even more interesting (and alien to today’s thinking) is the self-restraint these firms imposed on themselves when choosing targets and pricing their products.

For instance, in explaining their market share target, a General Electric executive told Lanzillotti his employer would “rather be pushing to expand a 25 percent share than defending a 50 percent share,” while Johns Manville executives told him that 20 percent was the maximum share in which they were interested. They explained that Johns Manville chose to keep prices somewhat above its competitors while emphasizing service and quality, rather than trying to drive rivals out through price cuts. Furthermore, several firms used target-return pricing because, as Lanzillotti put it, it provided “a benchmark in an area where prices otherwise might be subject to wide and dangerous variations.” (p. 928).

Behind the choice of these pricing objectives was a broader worldview than revenue or profit maximization, marked by the motivations to maintain good relationships with suppliers, channel members, and customers, and even take care of them, as a form of duty to regard their welfare as a necessary part of maintaining their own leadership position.

American politicians, presumably with more clout and a different mindset than today, also reinforced this principle in their interactions with business leaders. As an example, the Economic Report of the President in January 1957, cited by Lanzillotti, reminded business leaders that “business must recognize the broad public interest in price set on their products and services.” (p. 937). And it seems these firms mostly complied with this directive.

Although it has only been about 70 years, today’s world seems a galaxy far, far away. How did things change so dramatically?

### **Why Managerial Restraint Declined in Setting Pricing Objectives**

I will focus here on three overlapping reasons that can be attributed to this dramatic shift in pricing philosophy from the 1958 “we have a duty towards our customers, let’s be restrained” stance to today’s “let’s grab every last dollar out of our customers’ pockets and even take their wallet while we’re at it” mindset. ***Putting shareholders on a pedestal.*** The first reason was academic thought leadership, which gradually led to a radical shift in how the firm thought about its objectives and responsibilities. In the decades after Lanzillotti’s work, American managers were exposed to a very different account of a firm’s purpose by influential scholars in wave after wave of theoretical writing. A reliance on the concepts of balance, stewardship, and responsibility that prevailed in the 50s shifted to the view that managers were primarily agents of shareholders and their main obligation was to increase shareholder wealth.

In September 1970, Professor Friedman published his highly influential New York Times Magazine essay, in which he argued that the (only) social responsibility of business was to increase its profits, and that managers who diverted corporate resources toward broader social goals were spending other people’s money without their authorization[^2]. The formulation of this shareholder-first reorientation grew sharper and more strident throughout the 1980s and 1990s, as agency theory, shareholder value creation, and other theoretical frameworks gained widespread acceptance among business scholars[^3]. The restraint that was so widespread in Lanzilloti’s time became harder to justify, say, in an earnings call or a board meeting, because the logic sounded less like stewardship or moral duty and more like shirking a core managerial responsibility.

***Diminishing potency of antitrust actions.*** The second reason for the loss of restraint in setting pricing objectives was the diminishing potency of antitrust regulation. When Professor Lanzillotti conducted his interviews, the dominant firms operated with the heightened awareness that flexing their pricing power could attract unwanted attention from regulators, legislators, customers, and the public, and lead to significant negative financial consequences. It paid not to act like Godzilla, even if you were Godzilla. Size attracted scrutiny, which, in turn, constrained how aggressively firms used their market leadership positions.

This constraint weakened gradually year after year, decade after decade. Beginning in the late 1970s and 1980s, antitrust thinking in the United States became more focused on whether consumers were being harmed in a narrow economic sense, through higher prices, a reduction in the availability of goods and services, or a measurable reduction in consumer welfare. Robert Bork’s [The Antitrust Paradox](https://www.amazon.com/Antitrust-Paradox-Policy-War-Itself/dp/1736089714/) became the most well-known encapsulation of this revised thinking.

Essentially, Bork argued that antitrust law had become internally contradictory, punishing efficient business conduct (by the largest corporations) in the name of protecting competition. He posited that, instead of protecting small businesses or preserving fragmented markets, antitrust should primarily promote consumer welfare by ensuring the most efficient production of goods and services and their sale at the lowest possible prices. Such a view favored more permissiveness and tolerance of scale, concentration, and efficiency claims in antitrust law, especially when the firm’s conduct at issue could be framed as producing lower prices or operational efficiencies[^4].

This shift was significant for pricing strategy in large corporations, drastically changing the managerial calculus around key pricing objectives. The boundaries of what constituted an acceptable pricing strategy expanded considerably. Practices that might have been seen as politically risky or socially provocative in the 1950s became easier to justify as reasonable, analytical, and shareholder-oriented. Pricing decision makers had more room to leverage their scale and market leadership and to use techniques such as price segmentation, yield management, and, eventually, dynamic pricing to earn higher margins.

Interestingly, the recent revival of concern about the dominance of platforms like Amazon and Facebook, driven by their market power and concentration, suggests that this constraint on pricing power has once again become salient. In fact, legal scholars have recently argued that these last few decades of permissive antitrust regulation have left policymakers impaired to deal with powerful corporations. Thus, it seems that when pricing power becomes salient enough to provoke concern, especially in categories that consumers regard as essential, impactful on their lives, or unfairly favored in some way, the old scrutiny can return quickly[^5].

***Acceptability of higher markups.*** The third development is perhaps the most proximal to pricing practice because it is empirical and concerns what firms have done with their pricing power since 1958. In a highly-cited paper, De Loecker, Eeckhout, and Unger (2020) traced cost-plus price markups across the U.S. economy from 1955 to 2016[^6]. They found that for approximately two decades after Lanzillotti’s 1958 article, markups remained mostly unchanged, hovering at around 21 percent above marginal cost. Then, beginning in the late 1970s and early 1980s, they began to rise, such that by 2016 the average markup had reached 61 percent above marginal cost. Over the same period, average profit rates rose from about 1 percent to about 8 percent.

Furthermore, the authors found that, rather than being evenly distributed, the markup increase was concentrated among firms at the high end of the markup distribution. These were the firms with the most pricing power, having greater ability to turn their scale, differentiation, databases, brand strength, network effects, market position, and other pricing power inputs into higher margins. Finally, the authors attributed the rise in markups to the fact that leading firms in many industries beyond high-margin sectors like technology and pharmaceuticals had become more profitable relative to their costs. This suggests that the kind of restraint Lanzillotti observed in the 1950s declined, leading to a situation where the dominant firms were more able, and apparently more willing, to convert their pricing power into realized margins.

### **The Countervailing Force of Consumer Response**

These structural changes gave dominant firms greater latitude to wield their pricing power and raise prices more freely, embracing “maximize margin” or “maximize revenue” as valid, or indeed the most appropriate pricing objectives. But the expectations of consumers about how dominant firms that many of them transacted with, had emotional connections to, and had invested in, should behave remained relatively stable over this period.

Consumer psychology research provides considerable, consistent evidence about these entrenched consumer beliefs and their consequences. Research on the so-called *dual entitlement principle* found that customers regard it as fair for a firm to raise prices to protect its baseline profits when costs rise, but unfair for it to raise prices simply because demand has increased and it has the leverage to charge a higher price[^7]. Put differently, consumers see cost-justified price increases as legitimate, but demand-justified price increases are seen as unfair and provoke indignation. Figure 2 provides a nice graphical summary of the dual entitlement principle.

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/9a338fa5-3d98-4c9a-ac90-57849540ec30_2816x1319.png)

Figure 2. The Dual Entitlement Principle

The main thesis of the dual entitlement principle has held up well to scrutiny over the years. In their original paper, Kahneman, Knetsch, and Thaler (1986) studied rental housing. Their participants viewed a landlord raising rent on an existing tenant just because the market had tightened as unfair, but saw nothing wrong with charging a higher market rent to a new tenant after a previous tenant had moved out.

This finding has been replicated in other buying contexts. For instance, a study on consumer reactions to ATM fees found that consumers judged fee increases much more favorably when they were tied to higher costs. Without a cost justification, the same increase was seen (unfavorably) as opportunistic behavior[^8]. A similar pattern was also found in research on the German club concert industry. Here, while artists and promoters had no qualms about charging different prices in line with venue costs, they refrained from charging more for weekend shows than for weekday shows, even though weekend demand was presumably stronger[^9].

The recent fiasco with Wendy’s dynamic pricing provides a good example of how consumer response can quickly and decisively rein in a firm’s pricing ambitions. In February 2024, when the fast food company’s CEO, Kirk Tanner brought up the possibility of adopting surge pricing during an earnings call, the public outrage (mostly on [social media](https://www.reddit.com/r/marketing/comments/1b2862a/wendys_new_surge_pricing_how_does_out_of_touch/)) was so strong that Wendy’s issued a statement within days, clarifying that it had no plans to raise prices during peak demand periods and would instead use digital menu boards to lower prices during slower periods[^10].

As I’ve [written before](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/would-you-pay-30-for-dominos-pizza?utm_source=publication-search), the basic pricing mechanism of quickly adjusting prices in response to demand shifts that outraged Wendy’s consumers is common among airlines, hotels, ride-sharing services, and concert promoters, among other industries. In fact, there are dozens of [books](https://www.amazon.com/Hotel-Revenue-Management-Post-Pandemic-Evolution/dp/1637421915) and [courses](https://ecornell.cornell.edu/certificates/hospitality-and-foodservice-management/revenue-management-360/) on revenue management that detail its implementation. However, fairness perceptions are contextual and not necessarily rational. Consumers will readily tolerate dynamic pricing in categories where they have come to expect it and where some legitimating story is present. But they will react with anger when the same logic appears in categories that have historically maintained posted, stable prices.

This contradiction also helps explain why the greedflation debate became so top-of-mind and politically charged, even when the empirical evidence for its occurrence was at best mixed[^11]. Even as inflation increased, consumers continued to apply dual-entitlement reasoning to firms they perceived as powerful, and they were likely to pay special attention to prices, especially as they rose quickly. Inflation became a secondary consideration, leaving only the motives of profit-maximizing companies. Thus, even though we may no longer use the language of *noblesse oblige*, consumers still expect firms like Meta, Google, and Amazon to practice some version of it and will look for every sign that they aren’t.

### **Implications of Lanzillottis (1958) for Today’s Pricing Decision Makers**

For pricing decision makers, there is a huge practical lesson to be learned here. The constraint of duty that imposed restraint, as described by Lanzillotti’s executives, remains very much in play today. In the fog of a dense, volatile context in which these decisions are made, it has receded into the background and is often not considered at all. But a critical moment occurs when the firm’s pricing power becomes visible to customers, and it becomes hard to justify price increases on cost grounds. Then it reasserts itself with full force.

As I’ve discussed here, since about 1980, the language of restraint has largely vanished from the vocabulary of pricing strategy. Today’s managers talk about things like value capture, margin maximization, full-court monetization, and WTP extraction. No doubt these are useful concepts. But they carry a quiet, hidden risk: they can make pricing appear far too simplistic and mechanistic, as if the only goal worth pursuing is the brute-force extraction of economic value from customers.

Pricing power is contextual, subjective, and subject to scrutiny. Customers pay attention to it when it makes sense for them to do so, and they interpret its state and efficacy with abandon. Competitors, journalists, regulators, and political activists do the same thing. This issue is significant, particularly in B2B markets, where pricing decisions are made ensconced in continuing relationships in which both sides may often have years of shared history about how prices behave, what justifies a price change (especially an increase), and what counts as a fair process. Customers in these relationships have long memories, and they will retaliate when the unwritten contract of restraint is violated and reward restraint when they see it, often through things like a delayed renewal, a competitor quietly invited into the next RFP, a share of wallet that shrinks gradually, and so on. The possibilities are endless.

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/647ed40e-0651-4a34-8b60-75e80af04d4e_1254x1254.png)

Figure 3. The Pricing Restraint Triangle

A useful way to summarize the idea is with a pricing restraint triangle, shown in Figure 3. One corner of the triangle represents pricing power, which is the firm’s ability to raise prices because of scale, brand strength, customer databases, switching costs, and other power-enhancing factors discussed earlier. A second corner represents managerial inclination, derived from the trade-offs made by pricing decision makers to capture economic value, maximize margins, and satisfy investors. The third corner represents social constraint, encompassing customers’ fairness judgments, competitors’ reactions, and the scrutiny of journalists, politicians, activists, and regulators. Pricing strategy becomes risky when managers focus on pricing power and managerial inclinations while allowing social constraints to fade into the background.

The managers of firms that preserved their pricing power in the original *noblesse oblige* era understood this challenge intuitively and tackled it with grace. The managers who thrive in the current era will need to rediscover it, perhaps using an entirely different pricing vocabulary, or by toning down today’s aggressive language of maximization, while recognizing that pricing has never been about printing the highest possible dollar amount on an invoice.
