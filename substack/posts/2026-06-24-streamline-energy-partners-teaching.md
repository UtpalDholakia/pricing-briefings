This case focuses on pricing strategy within the capital-intensive midstream energy sector, which transports natural gas and other hydrocarbons from production areas to processing plants and markets. After analyzing this case, participants will develop skills in cost classification, incremental analysis, and strategic financial decision making under infrastructure and capacity constraints. They will also examine how asset-heavy industries must balance contract structures, spot market opportunities, and long-term capital investments to remain competitive.

#### **Learning Objectives**

By the end of the case discussion, participants should be able to:

1.  Distinguish between fixed, variable, and semi-variable costs in capital-intensive industries.

2.  Perform incremental revenue and cost analysis for strategic pricing decisions.

3.  Evaluate multiple strategic options using a pricing strategy lens.

4.  Analyze the role of capacity utilization in impacting company profitability.

5.  Apply pricing strategies in capital-intensive, regulated industries.

#### **The Industry Context**

The midstream segment of the oil and gas industry plays a crucial connecting role in the transportation, storage, and initial processing of hydrocarbons. This sector includes companies that operate pipelines, compressor stations, terminals, and related infrastructure. Midstream companies are distinguished from upstream producers (which extract oil and gas) and downstream entities (which refine and market finished products).

At least five significant characteristics amplify the significance of incremental cost pricing strategy approaches for the midstream industry sector.

1.  **Capital-intensive business model**: Midstream companies must invest heavily in infrastructure that often has a lifespan of 20 to 30 years. These assets incur high upfront costs and ongoing regulatory and maintenance expenses.

2.  **A high proportion of fixed costs**: Due to the inherent nature of pipeline operations, a large proportion of costs is fixed. These include depreciation, control room operations, and right-of-way payments. Profitability is highly sensitive to throughput volume.

3.  **Contract structures**: Revenue is typically derived from long-term contracts with shippers, based on either fixed fees on a per volume basis or capacity reservation. Additionally, excess capacity may be marketed using spot contracts, allowing for a variable pricing component in the company’s price structure.

4.  **Regulatory environment**: Midstream operations are subject to federal and state regulations covering safety, emissions, and land use. Compliance costs are often substantial and are independent of transported volumes.

5.  **Competitive pressure and overcapacity**: In certain geographical areas of the United States, such as the Permian and the East Texas basins, pipeline overbuild has resulted in fierce competition. Companies are under pressure to set themselves apart from competitors through pricing, service differentiation, or cost control.

#### **Key teaching points and insights provided by this case**

**Understanding the cost structure and classifying costs**: Participants must classify a complex set of costs into fixed, variable, and semi-variable components to understand operating leverage and pricing flexibility.

**Incremental analysis**: Participants learn that only costs and revenues that change as a result of the business decision should be included in the analysis, which means many seemingly relevant costs should be ignored. Sunk costs and allocated overheads that are unaffected by volume changes should also be excluded. What this does is make prices that seem too low far more profitable than they appear at first blush.

**Asset utilization vs. asset expansion**: In infrastructure-heavy industries, increasing the throughput of existing assets is often more profitable than building new capacity. This case helps illustrate how marginal improvements in utilization can reverse significant losses. Dramatic new investments or strategy overhauls are not necessary.

**Service differentiation and pricing power**: By offering premium services, companies can practice price discrimination, extracting additional value from customers with specific needs, such as guaranteed capacity or flexible scheduling.

#### **Step 3: Option Analysis**

#### ***Option 1: Renegotiate Base Contracts***

**Incremental Analysis:**

Current contracted revenue: 400 MMcf/d × 30 × 1,000 × \$0.30 = \$3,600,000

New contracted revenue: 460 MMcf/d × 30 × 1,000 × \$0.28 = \$3,864,000

Contract revenue change: \$3,864,000 - \$3,600,000 = +\$264,000

Volume increase: 60 MMcf/d × 30 days = 1,800 MMcf/month

Incremental variable costs: 1,800 MMcf × \$160 = \$288,000

One-time legal fees: = \$75,000

Net monthly impact: +\$264,000 - \$288,000 - \$75,000 = -\$99,000

**Recommendation: Reject** - This option results in a monthly loss of \$99,000. The lower rate (\$0.28 vs \$0.30) doesn’t compensate sufficiently for the additional volume and associated variable costs.

#### ***Option 2: Spot Market Focus***

**Incremental Analysis:**

Incremental revenue: 85 MMcf/d × 30 days × 1,000 × \$0.345 = \$879,750

Incremental costs:

Variable costs: 2,550 MMcf/month × \$160 = \$408,000

Additional staff: 2 × \$15,000 = \$30,000

System depreciation: \$50,000/24 months = \$2,083

Total incremental costs: \$440,083

Net monthly impact: \$879,750 - \$440,083 = \$439,667

**Recommendation: Accept** – The option has excellent incremental profit improvement potential despite achieving a lower price.

#### ***Option 3: Capacity Expansion***

Incremental revenue:

New contract revenue: 200 MMcf/d × 30 days × 1,000 × \$0.25 = \$1,500,000

Incremental costs:

Variable costs for new volume: 6,000 MMcf/month × \$160\* = \$960,000

Additional maintenance: \$80,000

Additional personnel: \$60,000

Increased insurance: \$30,000

Additional compliance: \$25,000

Depreciation: \$12,000,000 ÷ 15 years ÷ 12 months = \$66,667

\*Note: \$160/MMcf for new volume (no overtime or variable pigging due to additional staff)

Electricity increases to \$50/MMcf and fuel to \$65/MMcf for ALL volumes

Existing volume cost increase: 450 MMcf/d × 30 days × \$10 = \$135,000

Total incremental costs: \$1,356,667

Net monthly impact: \$1,500,000 - \$1,356,667 = \$143,333

**Recommendation: Consider this option further; it could go either way**. It is profitable, earning \$143K/month but requires a \$12M investment. The simple payback will take approximately 7 years.

#### ***Option 4: Premium Service***

Incremental revenue:

Premium revenue: 150 MMcf/d × 30 days × 1,000 × \$0.05 = \$225,000

Incremental costs:

Additional staff: 2 × \$12,000 = \$24,000

Software depreciation: \$200,000 ÷ 36 months = \$5,556

Total incremental costs: \$29,556

Net monthly impact: \$225,000 - \$29,556 = \$195,444

**Recommendation: Accept** - Good margin improvement with minimal investment, although a lower premium reduces impact. Determining the premium to be charged is a key consideration.

#### ***Overall Recommendation***

The optimal combination of options ot pursue is 2 and 4. If this combination is pursued, the combined incremental profit will be \$439,667 + \$195,444 = \$635,111. The current loss of \$445,000 will change into a net monthly profit of \$190,111. This combination of strategies propels the company forward from a significant monthly loss to solid profitability while maximizing current asset utilization before considering major capital investments.

### **Key Points for Discussion**

Participants should be directed to pay particular attention to the following points as they relate to incremental costs and incremental cost-based pricing strategies.

#### **1. Emphasize understanding the behavior of cost components.**

- Fixed costs remain constant regardless of volume. Most fixed costs are not incremental to the pricing decision and, therefore, irrelevant to evaluating it. Some fixed costs, particularly those associated directly with implementing the pricing strategy, may be incremental to the pricing decision and should be considered.

- Variable costs change proportionally with volume and are typically incremental to the pricing decision and should be considered.

- Semi-variable costs have both fixed and variable components. These costs need particular attention, and sometimes, there is subjectivity involved in which amounts and types of these costs will be incremental to the pricing decision, and which ones won’t be.

- In asset-heavy industries, most costs are fixed, creating high operating leverage. This makes incremental cost discussions with the organization and the decisions made particularly impactful to the organization’s financial performance.

#### **2. Reinforce the core principles of incremental analysis.**

- The pricing decision maker should consider the changes in revenues and costs, not absolute costs.

- They should ignore sunk costs that are avoidable as well as many allocated costs that are non-incremental to the pricing decision.

- Their focus should be on the impacts of cash flow resulting from the pricing decision.

- Finally, the pricing decision maker should consider opportunity costs carefully; these can often fly under the radar, but have a significant impact on the results of the pricing strategy.

#### **3. Highlight the significance of capacity/ asset utilization for the business**

- It is usually far more advantageous to use existing spare capacity of the company’s assets, human or inanimate, than it is to acquire and use new assets. Maximizing utilization of existing assets typically provides the highest returns, even with seemingly low prices.

- Capacity expansion should only be considered after all current assets are employed as fully as possible.

- Offering a premium option for enhanced services (using available spare capabilities or psychological techniques) can improve margins without adding capacity.

#### **4. Consider increasing the complexity of the price structure to judiciously leverage low incremental costs.**

- Price discrimination through service differentiation is worth considering, especially given the opportunities for [servitization](https://blog.ifs.com/2017/04/the-servitization-of-the-oil-and-gas-service-industry/) and the [relative lack of service differentiation](http://chrome-extension://efaidnbmnnnibpcajpcglclefindmkaj/https://www2.deloitte.com/content/dam/insights/us/articles/iot-in-oil-and-gas-industry/DUP-1169_IoT_OilGas.pdf) thinking in the midstream sector. This principle applies to other B2B sectors also.

- Balancing long-term contracts versus the spot market exposure is a key consideration in this case. Monitoring and maintaining this balance efficaciously should be a key pricing strategy focus for SEP. Long-term contracts provide the core revenue, helping to cover fixed costs while judicious forays into the spot market have significant profit potential.

- SEP’s pricing strategists also need to pay attention to customer value. In particular, understanding customer willingness to pay for reliability and service is crucial to managing price performance over a more complex and variable price structure.

### **Responses to Discussion Questions**

This section of the teaching note provides suggested answers and teaching guidance for the discussion questions provided in the Streamline Energy Partners case. It is intended to support instructors in leading case-based executive education and MBA sessions.

#### **1. How does SEP's cost structure affect its pricing flexibility?**

SEP’s cost structure skews heavily towards fixed costs. In this sense, it more closely represents a software company than, say, a typical product vendor in the oil field. Costs such as depreciation, control room operations, and right-of-way payments do not vary with volume. This limits SEP's short-term flexibility in lowering prices. However, once fixed costs are covered, incremental volumes of natural gas can be transported at very low marginal costs (\$160/MMcf), making the marginal throughput highly profitable and very low prices tenable. Thus, SEP has the opportunity to improve profitability by maximizing pipeline utilization, even at lower per-unit rates.

#### **2. When does it make sense to privilege spot market exposure over long-term contracts?**

For SEP and other companies like it, spot market exposure makes sense when there is perishable unused capacity, demand volatility enables price premiums, and when contractors prefer flexibility. In SEP’s case, the existing 150 MMcf/d of spare capacity offers upside through short-term contracting at premium rates. However, relying too heavily on volatile spot volumes will likely increase the company’s earnings uncertainty; consequently, spot exposure should complement, rather than replace, stable long-term contracts. Using the sun analogy we have employed in other circumstances to describe the value of low marginal cost promotional strategies, long-term contracts should form the core of the business, while spot market sales should be viewed as the corona, providing the spark to the company’s financial performance.

#### **3. What are the potential risks of pursuing each strategic option?**

Option 1 (Renegotiation): Risk of revenue dilution without offsetting volume gains.

Option 2 (Spot market): Market volatility risk; requires staff and systems investments.

Option 3 (Expansion): Capital deployment risk; long payback; potential underutilization.

Option 4 (Premium service offering): Operational risk; failure to deliver promised flexibility could affect customer trust.

#### **4. How would considering competitive response affect your recommendations?**

Another point to note is that SEP operates in an overbuilt market with fierce pricing competition. Aggressive price cuts (as in Option 1) could easily trigger competitive retaliation, which means that SEP should focus on value-added differentiation through reliability and flexibility (as in Option 4) instead of just lowering price. Furthermore, deliberate and sustained competitive monitoring should inform spot pricing and contract renegotiation strategies.

### **Implementation Issues**

Time permitting and especially when the focus of the course or education program is on operations-focused pricing strategy, the instructor should engage participants in a discussion of how the recommended solutions will be implemented by SEP. One approach would be to consider the timeline for the alternatives chosen for implementation. Another would be to cast the net wider, and consider decisions and actions to be implemented over the short, medium, and longer-term.

#### **Decisions and actions to be implemented by SEP in the next six months**

- ***Launch the premium service first***: Develop and market the Premium Reliability Service offering to current contracted shippers. Ensure operations staff and technology are ready to meet service guarantees.

- ***Begin spot market sales***: Hire and deploy commercial representatives to identify producers with uncontracted volumes and structure spot agreements. Prioritize those with existing interconnections.

- ***Hire staff required for the chosen options***: Recruit experienced commercial and operations personnel with a midstream O&G background. Train them on SEP’s systems, sales, and service differentiation goals.

- ***Implement nomination and scheduling software***: Deploy scheduling enhancements and integrate with operational control centers. Test reliability and ensure customer access and ease of use.

#### **Decisions and actions to be implemented by SEP over the 6-month to two-year horizon**

- ***Evaluate the uptake of spot and premium volumes:*** Track monthly volume trends, service uptake, and customer feedback. Identify barriers to growth and adjust offerings or pricing as needed.

- ***Consider contract adjustments based on utilization trends:*** Use data from premium uptake and spot market sales to revisit existing contract terms or propose new tiered pricing models.

- ***Monitor operating cost efficiency:*** Benchmark variable and fixed costs per unit of throughput. Identify optimization opportunities in maintenance scheduling, energy use, and personnel deployment.

#### **Decisions and actions to be implemented by SEP over the longer term (2+ years)**

- ***Assess the market conditions for capacity expansion:*** Monitor regional production trends, regulatory developments, and competitor moves. Validate that demand growth supports expansion at favorable terms.

- ***Evaluate financing options and risk exposure:*** Prepare financial models for potential capital projects. Explore funding options, including partnerships, debt issuance, or equity financing.

- ***Monitor policy changes related to the renewable energy transition:*** Stay abreast of legislative changes or market developments (e.g., hydrogen blending, carbon capture incentives) that may alter the economics of natural gas infrastructure.

### **Conclusion**

This case demonstrates the importance of understanding the cost structure of a market offering and applying incremental analysis in capital-intensive industries. The optimal strategy combines the maximization of the current asset utilization through spot market development and the development and offering of relatively low-incremental cost premium versions that produce differentiation and generate high-margin revenue, in this case, generating over \$1M in monthly incremental profit and transforming the project from a heavy loss to healthy profitability.

The key insight that the participant should take away after engaging with this case is that in asset-heavy businesses, strategies that improve capacity utilization typically provide superior returns compared to capacity expansion, especially when markets are already oversupplied.
