Managers are [far happier when they are cutting prices](https://hbr.org/2001/10/are-your-prices-too-low) than when they are raising them. There are many reasons for this, of course. A sense of discomfort when your prices are higher than competitors (even when well-deserved). A focus on [short-term performance measures](https://hbr.org/2007/07/if-brands-are-built-over-years-why-are-they-managed-over-quarters) like [sales lifts and market-share gains](https://www.amazon.com/Manage-Profit-Not-Market-Share/dp/1591395267). Overestimating how much a price cut will boost your sales. The belief that [aggressive short-term pricing leads to permanent increases in profitability](https://hbr.org/2001/10/are-your-prices-too-low). A bias for decisions that can be easily explained in investor and analyst calls. And the list goes on. When we keep pushing this managers’ bias in favor of lower prices to its logical conclusion, the company can end up with prices that are too low. And too-low prices can do more harm than good.

As I’ve [written before](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/the-eight-psychological-affordances), a product’s [price level](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/understanding-the-difference-between), in and of itself, delivers useful and even diagnostic information about the product’s quality to buyers. The diagnostic effects of price are pronounced when the buyer is not knowledgeable about the product category or the seller’s brand or does not have the motivation, the opportunity, or the ability to investigate in detail.

In such cases, which are all too common, the price tag performs many of the same functions that a brand does. The price conveys meaningful associations, many of which have to do with the product’s quality (or lack thereof). And this is when the potential danger of charging a price that is too low is the greatest. It is this:

> #### **Ask for a price that is too low, and the potential customer will immediately associate your product or service with poor quality and low value.**

This quote from Professor Gordon Wills in the preface of his [1969 book,](https://www.amazon.com/Pricing-Strategy-Bernard-Taylor/dp/0286631512) *[Pricing Strategy](https://www.amazon.com/Pricing-Strategy-Bernard-Taylor/dp/0286631512)*, coauthored with Bernard Taylor explains this idea perfectly:

> *Basically people and markets have developed an accepted idea of price. This is particularly true of such things as frequently purchased consumer products. Marketing managements and corporate managements coming into this situation have to take cognizance of existing price expectations among consumers, e.g., one can study prices people expect to pay for something such as paint, and find they want to pay a high price because of the psychological associations which go with home-making and building up their furnishings and fabrics, and if they buy something which appears cheap then they associate it with poor quality. (p xii).*

Here are two case studies of pricing, one from a services setting and another from a Consumer Packaged Goods setting. Both cases illustrate the aphorism that seriously low prices can backfire seriously.

### **The Bargain Basement Convention Speaker**

Consultant [Dorie Clark recounts the case](https://hbr.org/2017/10/why-you-should-charge-clients-more-than-you-think-youre-worth) of a highly-regarded (unnamed) bestselling author who was invited to be a keynote speaker at a trade association’s annual convention. When he was asked his speaking fees for the talk, the superstar author quoted a modest fee of \$3,000. It turned out this amount was a fraction of what the association was expecting to pay. (The association’s board had earmarked ten times as much, \$30,000, for speaker fees in their convention budget).

Instead of being thrilled at having locked up a top-notch speaker at a 90% off sale price and ending up with a surplus in their convention budget, the association’s board members began to have second thoughts. They wondered whether they had chosen the right speaker and grew concerned about the quality of speech he would deliver.

Notice that this adverse reaction occurred among the prospective clients even when the author’s reputation and track record were beyond reproach. In other words, even when other informational cues about the likely quality of his speech were available, the convention’s board gave greater weight to the author’s quoted price. His cluelessness about the economic value of his output led to questions about his cluelessness, period. Extracting the core insight from this case, Clark advises,

> “Price is often a proxy for quality, and when you put yourself at the low end, it signals that you’re unsure of your value — or the value just isn’t there. Either can be alarming for prospective clients.”

The main lesson from the author’s pricing mistake is that the information communicated by the price should be congruent with the product’s quality. Some managers may believe that asking too low a price will draw customers. But they forget that customers are sacrificing money and other scarce resources in exchange for the seller’s product, and they are looking for a fair and reasonable trade. If you’re selling a high-quality product, customers expect to pay a commensurately high price.

Asking too low a price undermines perceptions of the product’s quality instead of increasing the buyer’s evaluations of the product. This is a particularly significant issue for services like the convention speaker in Dorie Clark’s example and for other services like consulting, accounting, legal, creative, and so on, where much of the value derived by customers is intangible and ineffable, and [largely experiential](https://www.amazon.com/Power-Moments-Certain-Experiences-Extraordinary/dp/1501147765) or [credence-based](https://www.emerald.com/insight/content/doi/10.1108/10610421111134969/full/html).

### **Olay Total Effects**

In the late 1990s, [Oil of Olay was Procter & Gamble’s](https://www.amazon.com/Playing-Win-Strategy-Really-Works/dp/1491528796) only skin-care brand, and it was struggling. Its core customers skewed older each year. Its flagship product, priced at \$3.99, was sold through drugstores and was losing customers to more expensive products sold by emerging competitors in department stores.

The company developed a brand new strategy for relaunching the brand. It changed the name to Olay to get rid of the oily association with its products. It shifted focus from 50-plus women involved in an all-out battle with wrinkles to 35-plus women who were just starting to notice the first lines and wrinkles on their faces and necks and getting concerned.

P&G formulated a line of more effective anti-aging products with better and more expensive active ingredients and decided to launch the flagship product of Olay’s line called Olay Total Effects. The launch ad video below highlights these aspects of the relaunched brand.

This brings us to the pricing strategy behind Olay’s relaunch. P&G’s managers tested three different prices, \$12.99, \$15.99, and \$18.99, to assess which price would be the most appealing to the targeted customers. All three prices were presumably acceptable profitable prices.

Now you would expect shoppers to find the \$12.99 price to be the best one and the \$18.99 price to be the least attractive. However, it shouldn’t surprise you that this was not the case. A fair number of mainstream consumers who shopped in grocery stores or drug stores expressed interest in Olay at the low price. However, Olay’s so-called “prestige shoppers” who purchased such products in department stores were not impressed. They thought the product was too cheap to be in department stores at \$12.99 and was probably sketchy.

When Olay Total Effects was offered at \$15.99, the amount of purchase interest from both mainstream and prestige shoppers declined appreciably compared to the \$12.99 price. Neither segment seemed to be interested at this price point.

The most interesting part of the story is what happened when Olay’s price was increased to \$18.99. The purchase intentions of both groups, and particularly those of the prestige shoppers rose to levels that were even higher than the \$12.99 price. The result of the price test showed that more shoppers wanted to buy the Olay Total Effects product at the highest of the three prices. As Joe Listro, [Olay’s R&D manager, explained](https://www.amazon.com/Playing-Win-Strategy-Really-Works/dp/142218739X/):

> “So, \$12.99 was really good, \$15.99 not so good, \$18.99 great. We found that at \$18.99, we were starting to get consumers who would shop in both channels. At \$18.99, it was a great value to a prestige shopper who was used to spending \$30 or more for a similar product. But \$15.99 was no-man’s-land – way too expensive for a mass shopper and really not credible enough for a prestige shopper.”

Who knows what would have happened if the Olay managers had tested an even higher price, say \$21.99, at the time? (This gets to the challenge of designing price tests, but that’s a topic for another day and post).

The Olay Total effects case shows that the same product, with an identical formulation and packaging, is perceived and preferred differently based solely on its price by consumers in the mass market. The \$18.99 price radiated assurance for the department store shoppers by signaling the product’s effectiveness. The price informed the shopper that the item deserved to be sold in a department store and they would feel comfortable purchasing it there. For drugstore and grocery store shoppers, on the other hand, the \$18.99 price point made the product appear to be an aspirational luxury item that they could splurge on every so often.

What occurred next is equally compelling. As Lafley and Martin tell it:

> “Momentum started to build. Olay followed up with an even more expensive premium brand, with a yet-better active ingredient: Olay Regenerist. Then, it introduced Olay Definity, and then the still-higher premium Olay Pro-X – which sold at a \$50 price point, something inconceivable ten years earlier… Following the 2000 relaunch, Olay had double-digit sales and profit growth every year for the next decade. The result: a \$2.5 billion brand with extremely high margins and a consumer base squarely in the heart of the most attractive part of the market.”

As this next advertisement video from 2016 illustrates, the same target segment and positioning continue to work for Olay Total Effects even a decade and a half later.

### **The Price-Quality Cue in Healthcare**

So we are back to acknowledging the insight in our aphorism. As the convention speaker and Olay Total Effects cases show, the informational value of the price is particularly powerful when the buyer has difficulty assessing the item’s quality beforehand. The convention speaker’s service was an experiential service with a dose of credence properties. Its quality could only be evaluated after it was consumed, and perhaps fully not even then.

The convention organizers used the author’s asking price as a way of predicting his competence in public speaking. The low price failed because it undermined their confidence. Similarly, the Olay Total Effects product was brand new and being introduced to the marketplace. Consumers did not know what to expect. They used their knowledge about the product category and drew inferences about the new product’s potency from its price.

This sort of effect has also been found in an industry where pricing information is notoriously opaque - the healthcare industry. One study found that when a company gave its employees a tool to check the prices of different outpatient services before choosing which healthcare provider to purchase them from, both out-of-pocket spending and total spending increased unexpectedly. Why? In the words of a public policy expert:

> *“The likely effect of providing information on price for services priced higher than consumers’ deductibles has been missed in much of the discussion around price transparency tools. If patients are comparing services based on price for which their share of the cost is \$0, the use of a price transparency tool may lead directly to patients selecting the higher-cost options given their likely perception that higher price is a proxy for higher quality and the lack of an incentive to price shop.”*

In this study, price transparency led to the use of a price-quality cue. In the authors’ words, “Imagine trying to pick a hotel in Colonoscopy-ville, where you don’t know the language and you only have access to price. It is entirely reasonable that you will pick the higher-priced hotel, assuming that higher price means higher quality. And if someone else, say, your employer, is picking up the tab? You will be even more likely to pick the higher-priced hotel.”

### **The Main Takeaway**

The main lesson for managers is this. When consumers are looking at the product’s price for hints about what to expect, a too-low price will produce an image of mediocrity that could cling to the product like a lingering stink, far beyond its introductory phase. Even more dangerous, a low price could create doubt and suspicion among potential adopters to such a degree that sales may never take off. Startup founders should take particular note of these cautionary cases about too-low prices. They often tend to be among the most reluctant pricing decision-makers to ask for prices that their products and services deserve.

We will conclude with this [excellent quote from pricing consultants](https://www.amazon.com/Price-Advantage-Walter-L-Baker/dp/0470481773) Walter Baker, Michael Marn, and Craig Zawada about the virtue of high prices:

> “\[Setting a high price\] is not about gouging customers or employing tricks to gain undeserved revenues. Quite to the contrary, the real price advantage is a source of organizational pride. The highest compliment a customer can pay a supplier is to knowingly pay more for that company’s goods and services. In doing so, the customer is saying, “You are higher priced, but you are worth it; you are superior to my other supplier alternatives.”
