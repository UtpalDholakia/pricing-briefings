*“I normally read at least a book a day and get all my books from the library. In short, my library is priceless to me!”* - [Vel Oakes](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/prince-edward-island/pei-library-total-savings-1.6034379?__vfz=medium%3Dcomment_share_twitter#vf-7041300020345).

Photographs of receipts from local public libraries like the one below pop up on Reddit from time to time. When they share how much money they saved by borrowing books from the library, posters are basking in the glory of financial prudence. Not every US public library provides the saving calculation on patron receipts, but many do, like this one:

![](https://bucketeer-e05bbc84-baa3-437e-9518-adb32be77984.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/b28f25aa-496b-4e16-b2be-44c7519bca75_1813x2095.jpeg)

Source: Post on the frugal subreddit by u/Mapuches_On_Fire.

The message on the receipt says:

> “You just saved \$49.99 by using your library. You have saved \$1,953.22 this past year and \$3,942.07 since you began using the library.”

From a pricing communication perspective, giving an estimate of how much money the library patron saved by borrowing books is a terrific idea. It reinforces and quantifies the individual’s virtuous behavior. The simple “money saved” values make people feel good about reading, about using the library, and about seeing their tax dollars at work in a concrete way. And by providing three amounts, today’s savings, last year’s savings, and total savings, the message avoids [the “lonely number” problem](https://www.goodreads.com/notes/36185940-factfulness/13635667-harald-groven/eb2fbdfd-3bb1-4518-b796-d2cea4952f3b?ref=rnlp) of having to interpret standalone numbers. Patrons get a quick, comprehensive, and useful economic accounting of their book-borrowing behavior from three sentences. For pricing managers, there is a powerful lesson here.

Now [promotional claims](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/(SICI)1520-6793(200003)17:3%3C257::AID-MAR4%3E3.0.CO;2-P?casa_token=snI9gy3fvRQAAAAA:_lGepw0o2lvfPvlYmH1bBtab1WwlGfu_D8FKe_KZoBt6YdICrkIyxMWsuqks_Ko-3LY885sarV5aZ7yA) that frame customer savings like a sign which says “1/2 off only today,” or a “Buy One, Get One Free” digital coupon are common forms of pricing communication. But these messages are transactional. They are designed to elicit specific customer behavior, i.e., a purchase, in the *here and now*. In contrast, money saved communications, like the one used by libraries, are relational. They are meant to praise and reinforce customer behaviors that the company values after purchase.

Despite the win-win for customers and companies, relatively few companies give relational “money saved” information to customers post-purchase in any meaningful way. (Some grocery stores and health insurance providers are exceptions; see examples below). Pricing strategists can learn from this motivating and useful post-purchase messaging and consider adopting it. However, there is also one potential weakness with the information in the library receipt which can be improved upon.

#### **Quantifying the value of virtuous customer behavior is useful but difficult.**

In the course of their journeys, customers engage in numerous virtuous behaviors. Purchasing a store brand (vs. a national brand), buying during a promotional period, using cloth bags instead of plastic bags, walking instead of driving, doing laundry during an off-peak time in summer, keeping the thermostat at a lower temperature in winter, and ordering a lower-calorie entree instead of a steak are examples of virtuous behaviors. The list goes on and on.

Virtuous customer behaviors are difficult to quantify, for two reasons. First, it is not always clear which units are appropriate and should be used for quantifying the behavior’s value. And second, a [comparison baseline](https://hbr.org/2016/08/a-quick-guide-to-value-based-pricing) for assessing value is required but is not always available or [readily used](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article-abstract/38/4/595/1856292) by customers.

The following example illustrates the challenge. Let’s say I choose to run for an hour. How can I quantify my gains? First, I have to pin down the units of the gain. The number of calories I burnt? The intensity of the jogger’s high I experienced? The money I saved from improving my health? It’s not clear. Adding to the challenge, I will also need a comparison baseline. What would I have done had I not gone running? Only after I determine the alternative activity (i.e., my opportunity cost), can I measure my gain in a meaningful way. For example, I may be able to estimate that I burnt 500 calories more from running than if I had just continued to couch-surf.

The library quantifies its patron’s gain using money saved from borrowing books. That’s straightforward. It’s the baseline comparison that’s a bit tricky.

Before we get into that, for pricing strategists the main lesson here is that you should make the effort to quantify your customers’ virtuous behavior in dollars and cents and communicate it to your customers whenever and as often as appropriate. In relational settings, this is typically the money that the customer saves after a purchase or a transaction. Money saved = value realized. Providing this information in black and white helps to explain, justify, and validate your price to customers. It also works to reassure them and strengthen their connection with your company.

#### **Make the effort to calculate the customer’s actual savings. Avoid inflating the amount saved.**

In the library receipt displayed at the top of this article, the patron checked out Tolkien’s “The Two Towers.” The receipt stated they saved \$49.99. This savings calculation is based on the library’s *replacement cost* - what the patron would have to pay the library if they lost the book. This amount includes what it [costs the library to buy the book plus significant processing costs](https://library.calvin.edu/policies/lost_book/faq).

The library is calculating the amount saved using *its* next best alternative, not the patron’s next best alternative. The savings estimate is grossly inflated. On Amazon, a mass paperback copy of [The Two Towers costs \$5.30](https://www.amazon.com/Two-Towers-Lord-Rings-Part/dp/0345339711/ref=sr_1_1?dchild=1&keywords=the+two+towers+mass+paperback&qid=1623102504&sr=8-1) (including shipping), and a dollar or two less than that for a used copy. With the correct baseline comparison, the patron saved around \$5 by borrowing the book from the library, not \$50. Not that there’s anything wrong with that.

This receipt from another library provides a more reasonable, although still somewhat inflated estimate. It uses new hardcover copy values to calculate the patron’s savings:

![](https://bucketeer-e05bbc84-baa3-437e-9518-adb32be77984.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/35024ecb-b0bf-4e80-87c7-c8fcf23e027b_359x428.jpeg)

The lesson is this. Pricing strategists who want to use “money saved” messaging to transactional customers should be careful not to provide overestimates about how much customers saved. That is a quick way to lose credibility, hurt your brand, and potentially trigger [negative social media reactions](https://www.reddit.com/r/Frugal/comments/ms64a2/my_library_receipt_shows_how_much_money_ive_saved/).

#### **The Pricing Communication Vacuum**

CX-focused companies manage customer relationships by enhancing every stage of their decision journey. They spend a lot of time and effort in navigating the customer through the consider and evaluate stages to the purchase stage (see figure below). During the Consider stage, they provide price information and set up the context to influence the customer’s understanding and acceptance of the price.

Once the customer moves to the Evaluate stage, companies provide specific references to help the evaluation, generate offers, provide incentives, and negotiate, all with the goal of closing the sale. Their pricing strategies, which include price offers and communication, are geared towards getting the customer over the purchase finish line.

![](https://bucketeer-e05bbc84-baa3-437e-9518-adb32be77984.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/d461e069-9f08-49c6-83f6-1a844fd57a0f_813x593.png)

Once the customer completes the purchase, many companies fall silent. This is what I call the *Pricing Communication Vaccum*. (There is a larger *Pricing Vacuum* at work here, too, but that’s a discussion for another day). In the Enjoy, Advocate, and Bond states of the decision journey, companies rely on customers and their peers to do all the talking and communicating about the product (and its price) through reviews, referrals, and so on. This is where the money saved message has the potential to improve the customer’s experience significantly, by reassuring the customer and underscoring the value received.

I have driven a series of [Toyota Prii](https://www.auto123.com/en/news/toyota-announces-the-plural-of-prius/8614/) over the past fifteen years or so. I continue to be amazed by how little — by which I mean, virtually none — communication I receive from Toyota after I’ve purchased my Prius. Wouldn’t it be nice if they sent me a “Kudos” email or text every quarter with an estimate of how many gallons of gasoline and how much money I had saved by driving my beloved Prius instead of a gas guzzler?
