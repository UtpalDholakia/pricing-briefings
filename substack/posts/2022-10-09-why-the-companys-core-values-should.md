Most pricing decision makers tend to focus on one or more of the three basic pricing inputs – costs, customer value, and reference prices. Some go a step further and consider a fourth factor, the value proposition behind the company’s marketing strategy. As they should. There’s no question each of the factors is crucial to making effective pricing decisions. They *must* be considered.

However, there’s a fifth equally significant input, and I would even argue it’s the most significant one of all, that relatively few companies bring to their discussions of pricing strategy in any meaningful way. This factor is the company’s core values.

The most effective pricing strategies tend to be harmonious with the company’s core values and business philosophy. They consider the core values that the company has adopted and formulate pricing objectives, structures, actions, and governance mechanisms that are congruent with the core values. And the pricing strategies that fizzle out often run contrary to what the company stands for, or are based on not considering core values at all, but instead focusing on narrower tactical concerns devoid of strategic heft.

### **What Are Core Values?**

In the discussion that follows, I assume that your company has carefully chosen and adopted core values that it wants to stick with for a long time. If that isn’t the case, the first order of business is to sit down and [consider the question of core values carefully](https://hbr.org/2011/10/employee-values-stakeholder-va), choosing values that are meaningful and substantial. In many companies, the company’s core values reflect the core beliefs of its founders, senior executives, and eventually, they come to be embraced by every rank-and-file employee and become part of the company’s culture.

Here’s Steve Jobs talking about Apple’s core values that he adopted from the very beginning of Apple’s founding:

[Embedded content: www.youtube-nocookie.com](https://www.youtube-nocookie.com/embed/keCwRdbwNQY?rel=0&autoplay=0&showinfo=0&enablejsapi=0)

As Jobs explains, Apple’s core values are “it’s who they are and what they are about.” Actually, this is a good definition of the core values concept. A company’s core values articulate, at an abstract and deeply meaningful level, the fundamental principles that the company’s people believe in, endorse as essential and use as the foundation for making every decision. Essentially, core values are shorthand for what the company stands for.

To give one example, one of American Express’ [core values is superior quality](https://www.americanexpress.com/in/company/mission.html). This translates into statements of purpose and direction like these:

- “We’re driven by our commitment to deliver exceptional products, services and experiences to our customers.”

- “We deliver an unparalleled standard of excellence in everything we do, staying focused on the biggest opportunities to be meaningful to our customers.”

These articulations of purpose and emphasis, in turn, drive small and big decisions of managers at every level within the company.

Luxury brand [Hermes’ core values are](https://finance.hermes.com/en/strategy/), “spirit of conquest, creativity, craftsmanship, quality, authenticity, and independence.” These values guide the choices of which products it sells, which [sales](https://www.vox.com/2015/6/26/8850883/hermes-birkin-bags) and [distribution channels](https://digital.hbs.edu/platform-rctom/submission/hermes-the-ultimate-luxury-company/) it uses (and steers away from), and of course, its pricing policies and [the prices it charges](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/why-does-a-hermes-birkin-handbag). Many companies in the Oil & Gas industry [endorse safety as a core value](https://www.mckinsey.com/business-functions/organization/our-insights/transforming-safety-culture-insights-from-the-trenches-at-a-leading-oil-and-gas-company), which in turn, drives business operations, recruiting, and strategy in broad-based ways.

In all these companies, if managers are not careful, the pricing strategy, or lack thereof, can [become a hurdle](https://risktec.tuv.com/risktec-knowledge-bank/process-safety-management/boom-or-bust-the-impact-of-low-oil-prices-on-process-safety/) to harmonizing activities with the chosen core values. Imagine a Hermes Birkin bag selling for \$100 at your neighborhood mall kiosk, or the [Amex Centurion card](https://thepointsguy.com/guide/amex-centurion-black-card/) being given away to checking account customers with incentives. Unthinkable. Not going to happen.

The relation between the company’s core values and pricing strategy works both ways. Core values guide pricing strategy and a pricing strategy that is harmonious with the company’s core values supports core value-bolstering activities and programs that cement the company’s choice.

When explicitly articulated, core values provide every company employee from the CEO down to the entry-level line worker with broad guidelines for making decisions and acting. As consultant [Denise Lee Yohn points out](https://hbr.org/2018/02/ban-these-5-words-from-your-corporate-values-statement), they should “ideally function as the ‘operating instructions’ of the company. The goal of articulating the essential and enduring principles of your organization is to inform, inspire, and instruct the day-to-day behaviors of everyone who works at your company.”  This includes strategic decisions that will impact the company’s directions over the years and even decades. And it includes (or for many companies, should include) the company’s pricing strategy.

The list of core values is a [relatively short one](https://hbr.org/2018/02/ban-these-5-words-from-your-corporate-values-statement), and includes the values we’ve already touched upon. Those that directly guide pricing strategy form an even shorter list. Values, after all, are broad, [all-encompassing, abstract concepts](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/00222429990634s104?casa_token=MHzXelPsiJIAAAAA%3AjdNwPch74DjZL3LODit8bVBMWxHerMd2UqkojtEEtaGkeri0EKUJylbLfjxVV9Ts9o61J_MCBr9o_w), and most companies tend to gravitate towards the same ones. What’s more, many core values overlap, but each one supports a distinctive worldview so that adopting more than one or two would likely mean that the company is paying lip service rather than really using the chosen core value(s) as guides. Just as too many cooks spoil the broth, too many core values muddle strategic thinking.

### **How Transparency Affects Pricing Strategy**

Even though the company’s core values are relatively abstract, they provide useful guidance to the manager for making actionable pricing decisions. Let’s consider the case of transparency. When a company adopts transparency as a core value, it pledges to make its external constituents such as customers, suppliers, investors, and the community at large in which it operates, privy to information that is usually proprietary. Depending on the context, the company is forthright with its customers about its cost inputs, its revenue models, the process by which it sets prices, its price components, and its final price. When appropriate, it also involves customers in the pricing process and gives them meaningful information during the processes of negotiation and setting prices.

For the company’s pricing strategy, in particular, transparency can be a sensitive and potentially fraught core value because it endorses pricing strategies that are restrained, simple, and open to competitor and customer scrutiny, all of which can have adverse effects on the company’s revenue and margin models.

But transparency is also powerful. When the principle is applied competently and judiciously, the company’s openness and honesty create a sense of trust and reciprocity in customers like nothing else. When the company shares not only its pricing method but also actual percentages and dollar values, the transparency is even more palpable and appreciated. When transparency reveals concerns beyond dollars and cents, it can uplift customers and burnish the brand.

All of this is to say that transparency can go a long way in attracting the right types of customers to the company, those whose core values are aligned with the company’s values, and those who support the company’s strategic objectives. Transparency becomes the engine that drives the company’s pricing strategy, affecting every aspect of its decisions about price levels and the price structure. ### **Seattle Coffee Works, Pachamama Coffee & Peers: The Transparency Pledge**

Consider Seattle Coffee Works, a Seattle-based coffee roaster and retailer. Its [core values are](https://www.seattlecoffeeworks.com/) “to make coffee better, in terms of quality, equity, sustainability, and transparency.” Of course, each of these values is relevant to pricing strategy, and emphasizing any one of them will take the company’s strategic pricing choices such as the pricing levels and structure in a different direction. Seattle Coffee Works emphasizes transparency, and its pricing strategy reflects the choice of the core value. In its own words:

> “Full price transparency holds us accountable, tracks our progress, and serves as a valuable metric for farmers, consumers, and fellow members of the coffee community. Farmers can use price transparency to incentivize improvements in coffee quality and the quality of life for their employees. Consumers can use this report to see how their purchasing power impacts the livelihoods of coffee workers worldwide.

For Seattle Coffee Works, the concept of “full price transparency” really means purchase cost transparency. The relevant price-related information to the company’s stakeholders has to do not with prices charged to coffee drinkers in its stores but with whether the company is paying fair and reasonable prices for the coffee beans it buys from farmers. The prices paid by Seattle Coffee Works to each farmer per pound of coffee beans are the relevant basis of its concern with transparency.

![](https://bucketeer-e05bbc84-baa3-437e-9518-adb32be77984.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/05894ec9-1542-4df9-8afb-6b745d3b1837_1814x509.png)

Seattle Coffee Works’ diagram of its statement of authenticity.

It turns out that Seattle Coffee Works is not alone. Transparency is a core value embraced by many of its peer coffee companies. They have formed a collective to enact purchase cost transparency in a very public and publicized way. Seattle Coffee Works, with sixty-three other coffee companies (and counting), have adopted the [Transparency Pledge](https://www.transparency.coffee/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/The-Pledge-A-common-code-for-transparency-reporting-in-green-coffee-buying-July-2019.pdf).

The transparency pledge commits the coffee company to generate and publicly share purchasing reports that include all its suppliers, the specific types and volume of coffee purchased from each one, and the FOB price paid on a per-pound basis. Many of the signatories were providing this information on their websites and in printed catalogs even earlier.

In its “[2020 authenticity report](https://www.seattlecoffeeworks.com/authenticity-report-2020-seattle-coffee-works),” for example, Seattle Coffee Works reported that it purchased a total of 63,006 pounds of coffee and paid an average of \$3.89 per pound. The company also reported that it paid its employees a starting wage of \$17 per hour and full-time employees \$24.50 per hour. Pachamama coffee, a Sacramento-based chain went even further, towards full price transparency. In addition to purchase sources and prices paid for coffee beans to its suppliers in line with the transparency pledge, [Pachamama also disclosed](https://pachamamacoffee.com/pages/transparency) how much revenue it earned, the price per cup it charged, plus its net profit and profit margin.

It is interesting that for Seattle Coffee Works, Pachamama, and most other peer coffee companies who have signed the transparency pledge, providing detailed purchase cost information is only tangentially related to the focus on *their customers*. Where transparency goes, they care much more about the farmers spread around the world in places like Ethiopia, Kenya, Peru, Honduras, and Guatemala than about the reaction of their customers. See this explanation by Counter Culture Coffee, another signatory to the Transparency Pledge.

[Embedded content: www.youtube-nocookie.com](https://www.youtube-nocookie.com/embed/9hKtNKFUG_c?rel=0&autoplay=0&showinfo=0&enablejsapi=0)

Tim Wendelboe, the owner of a Norwegian espresso bar also echoes this focus on coffee farmers with no mention of his customers:

> “I really hope that The Pledge can inspire more roasters to not only be transparent about their green coffee prices but also that farmers can use the information provided in the various transparency reports to have better leverage when negotiating the price of their coffee. Hopefully The Pledge will set a new standard when it comes to transparency and also a new benchmark for what the market price for quality coffee really is.”

This selfless concern for the well-being of suppliers does contribute to customers perceptions. The positive effect on customers is palpable and works on two levels. For most customers who mostly just want a good cup of coffee but want to consume in as socially responsible and sustainable a way as possible, just the transparency report’s availability and the pains taken by the company to be transparent are strong positive signals. It reinforces the casual customer’s relation with the coffee brand.

And then, there is a second group of coffee drinkers who are serious environmentalists. They are the ones who will pore over the supplier lists given in the transparency report and read about the different coffees purchased and prices paid by the company to become more knowledgeable connoisseurs of coffee drinking.

### **The Main Takeaway**

I have only scratched the surface for the big idea that when it comes to designing and implementing an effective pricing strategy, the core value of a company matters a great deal. There are so many other examples and cases that I could draw upon that underscore the basic fact that many different types of pricing strategies, whether it is to [sell a \$40,000 leather handbag](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/why-does-a-hermes-birkin-handbag), an [assortment of low-priced, relatively undifferentiated merchandise](https://www.hbs.edu/faculty/Pages/item.aspx?num=43132) in a department store, or a [niche fashion label](https://www.everlane.com/about), are effective because they synchronize with the company’s chosen core value.
