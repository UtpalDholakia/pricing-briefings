This case study[^1] focuses on pricing strategy within the volatile upstream oil and gas sector, where Ironwood Resources produces crude oil, natural gas, and natural gas liquids in the Permian Basin. After analyzing this case, students will develop skills in developing effective price structures for upstream oil and gas settings, conducting pocket price and margin analysis, customer account management, and price execution, while considering price dispersion and stakeholder resistance. They will also study how firms in competitive, commodity-driven markets can implement data-driven pricing reforms, balance customer relationships with profitability, and leverage transaction-level pricing decisions. The teaching note, posted separately, provides a comprehensive solution to the pricing analyses and additional context on industry dynamics and implementation challenges.

#### **Introduction**

James Wilson stared at the spreadsheet on his computer, the numbers blurring together as fatigue set in. Three weeks into his new job as Ironwood Resources’ first-ever Pricing Director, James was still discovering and untangling layers of complexity. He was quickly learning that the oil and gas industry operated by its own rules, with pricing structures that seemed to have evolved through a combination of market forces, legacy customer relationships, and intuitive judgment rather than effective pricing strategy principles.

Analytical by nature and having earned a stellar reputation for building price departments from the ground up, James was recruited by Ironwood specifically for his outsider perspective and pricing acumen. At forty-two, he had the quiet confidence of a seasoned pricing expert who had proven himself repeatedly and turned around under-performing business units with rigorous, data-driven pricing strategies. His previous employer, a major specialty chemicals company, had seen margin improvements of 4.5 percentage points after implementing his value-based pricing framework.

Now, sitting in his sparse office at Ironwood Resources’ Midland headquarters, with the faint sound of drilling rigs in the background, James knew he faced a different kind of challenge. The margin pressure that had prompted Ironwood’s CEO Maria Sanchez to create his position and recruit him aggressively wasn’t just about setting prices. It was about the company’s identity and future in an evolving industry.

“The deeper I dig, the more things become complex,” James thought, running a hand through his prematurely graying hair. He would need to build a compelling case for change to persuade the analytical minds in Ironwood’s executive suite, the relationship- and remuneration-focused sales team, and the technically driven operations department. With a notepad in hand, he began sketching out his approach to understand Ironwood’s actual profitability picture.

#### **The Industry Landscape**

James spent his first week immersing himself in the complex ecosystem of the oil and gas industry. Unlike the more stable chemicals sector, this world operated in boom-and-bust cycles driven by geopolitical influences and increasing environmental scrutiny. Since 2020, the industry had faced unprecedented volatility, with WTI crude prices swinging from the negative territory during the pandemic to over \$100 per barrel during supply chain disruptions and conflicts, before settling into the current \$70-85 range. He knew that the next upturn or downturn could happen at any time.

The Permian Basin, where Ironwood primarily operated, remained North America’s most prolific hydrocarbon region, accounting for approximately 40% of U.S. oil production. Consolidation had intensified competitive pressure on Ironwood, with majors like ExxonMobil acquiring prime acreage and mid-sized independents like Pioneer Natural Resources adopting disciplined, margin-focused strategies. Ironwood’s secondary operations in the Eagle Ford Shale and emerging assets in the Powder River Basin provided some geographic diversification, but the Permian remained its crown jewel.

The market dynamics were complex in this industry. OPEC+ continued to exert significant influence through production quotas, although compliance varied. Following previous boom-and-bust cycles, the U.S. shale producers had adopted capital discipline, focusing on free cash flow rather than production growth at all costs. The global oil demand had recovered from the pandemic lows but still faced long-term uncertainty due to energy transition efforts. Natural gas prices showed regional disparities, with U.S. Henry Hub prices relatively subdued compared to international LNG benchmarks.

The regulatory environment added yet another layer of complexity. With tightening methane emissions regulations, operators faced financial penalties for excessive flaring and venting. ESG considerations increasingly influenced capital allocation from institutional investors, an area where Ironwood had made some proactive investments. Finally, carbon pricing mechanisms affected operating costs in some jurisdictions, and water management became a critical operational and environmental concern.

Technology was also rapidly transforming the industry. Advanced analytics and AI were revolutionizing reservoir management and production optimization. Digitalization efforts that sought to reduce operating costs and improve efficiency across the value chain were being adopted enthusiastically by the majors. The electrification of oilfield equipment reduced on-site emissions but required significant capital investment. And finally, carbon capture, utilization, and storage projects were gaining traction as strategies to reduce emissions.

Within this complex industry landscape, Ironwood Resources had carved out a distinctive position for itself with three main business segments: (a) crude oil production, primarily light, sweet crude (65% of revenue); (b) natural gas production, primarily dry gas with some rich gas areas requiring processing (25% of revenue); and (c) natural gas liquids (NGL), including ethane, propane, butane, and natural gasoline (10% of revenue).

The company served a diverse customer base that included regional refineries (primarily mid-sized independent operators), major integrated oil companies (purchasing both for their own refineries and trading operations), petrochemical manufacturers (focused on specific NGL components), and marketing and midstream companies (who further aggregate and distribute to end-users).

Ironwood also had an iron-clad reputation for customer service and flexibility, routinely accommodating special requests from long-term customers, whether it was for custom blends or for unconventional delivery schedules. Its salespeople jokingly referred to it as the “Four Seasons of the Permian.” While this “customers first” approach had built strong relationships, James suspected it might also be contributing to the margin erosion that had prompted his hiring.

#### **The Initial Findings**

After three weeks of interviews and data gathering, James sat down with Maria in her spacious office. Maria’s direct gaze and hint of impatience signaled that she wanted actionable insights from James, not just surface-level observations.

“I’m beginning to understand why our margins are under pressure,” James explained, sliding a preliminary report across the polished conference table to her. “We’ve been looking at production costs and market prices, but the real story is happening between the price we quote to our customers and what we actually put in our pocket.”

Maria leaned forward and said, “Give me the headlines, James. What are we missing?”

“In short, we’re excellent at producing oil and gas, but our approach to pricing is reactive. The sales team is measured on volume targets rather than profitability, so they are making inconsistent discounting decisions. There’s no formal system to track adjustments to base price, and special requests are rarely priced to reflect their true cost to the company.” Then he went on to describe his findings from his research over the past three weeks.

Robert Chen, the VP of Sales, had initially been skeptical of James’s questions. A charismatic former college athlete with twenty-plus years in energy sales, Robert had built the sales team from three people to fifteen, emphasizing strong relationships with customers and volume growth. When James questioned the ad-hoc discounting practices, Robert had defended them as necessary in a relationship-driven industry. “This isn’t retail,” he had said during their first meeting. “Every customer has unique needs, and our flexibility is what differentiates us from the majors.”

James had also spent time with Sarah Johnson, the Chief Financial Officer, a precise and methodical accountant who had joined Ironwood from a Big Four firm three years earlier. Sarah had been more receptive to James’ inquiries, acknowledging that while the company had sophisticated models for production costs, its view of transaction-level profitability was limited. “We track overall margins by product line and division,” she had explained, “but we haven’t had the tools to see how specific pricing decisions impact profitability across different customer segments.”

The operations team, led by Miguel Fuentes, a veteran field supervisor who had risen through the ranks, had provided valuable insights into how customer requests affected production schedules and costs. Miguel had been blunt: “Some of these special requests play havoc with our efficiency metrics, but no one ever calculates what that’s really costing us.”

Now, James outlined his plan to analyze six months of transaction data across all customer segments to understand the difference between the list price and what Ironwood actually realized after all discounts, rebates, and costs were accounted for, and the resulting profitability. In the pricing world, these metrics were known as the pocket price and the pocket margin, respectively[^2].

#### **The Pricing Challenge**

By analyzing six months of transaction data across all customer segments, James wanted to understand the difference between the list price and what Ironwood actually realized after all discounts, rebates, and costs were accounted for (the “pocket price”), as well as the resulting profitability (the “pocket margin”).

His analysis would need to account for the following elements:

***On-Invoice Adjustments:***

- Base price (tied to benchmark indices like WTI or Henry Hub)

- Volume discounts (typically 2-8% based on order size)

- Early payment discounts (1-2% for payments within 15 days)

- Competitive situation discounts (ad-hoc discounts to match competitor offers)

- Quality adjustments (premiums or discounts based on API gravity, sulfur content, etc.)

- Term contract discounts (1-3% for multi-year commitments)

***Off-Invoice Adjustments:***

- Annual volume rebates (retroactive rebates of 1-5% based on yearly purchase volumes)

- Market development funds (contributions to joint marketing efforts with distributors)

- Freight allowances (subsidized shipping costs for distant customers)

- Cash management costs (impact of extended payment terms)

- Inventory carrying costs (storage of custom blends or reserved capacity)

- Technical service costs (free engineering support provided to key customers)

***Data Analysis Results***

James collected data on 2,500 transactions across 120 customers and organized them by segment. He constructed a *pocket price waterfall* to visualize how the list price eroded through various discounts and adjustments before becoming the actual pocket price received by Ironwood (see Exhibit 1 for an example).

The analysis provided a number of surprising insights:

1.  The average pocket price was only 78% of the list price, much lower than the 85% that management had estimated.

2.  There was tremendous price dispersion within similar customer segments – some customers were effectively paying 30% more than others for very similar products and volumes.

3.  The highest volume customers were not necessarily the most profitable due to demanding additional services and steep discounts.

4.  Rush delivery fees were being waived for about 40% of expedited orders, costing the company approximately \$2.3 million annually.

5.  Technical support services, initially offered as a competitive differentiator, had grown to consume significant resources without corresponding price adjustments.

***Pocket Margin Analysis***

Taking his analysis further, James constructed a pocket margin waterfall that started with the pocket price and then subtracted the various cost components:

- Direct production costs (lifting costs, processing, etc.)

- Transportation and logistics

- Direct selling costs

- Customer-specific service costs

- Allocated overhead

This analysis revealed that more than 20% of transactions were actually unprofitable when all costs were factored in. Even more significant was the fact that three major customers that the sales team considered to be strategic accounts were among the least profitable relationships.

#### **Strategic Options**

James prepared a presentation for Maria and the rest of the executive team outlining several potential actions:

1.  ***Price Structure Reform*****:** Redesign the base pricing model to better reflect the true value of Ironwood’s products and services, including implementing tiered pricing (also known as good-better-best pricing) based on both volume and profitability.

2.  ***Discount Governance*****:** Implement a formal discount approval process with clear guidelines and authority levels based on metrics primarily geared around account profitability.

3.  ***Unbundling Services*****:** Begin charging separately for premium services that customers valued tremendously but were currently included for free with base products, such as rush delivery, custom blending, and technical support.

4.  ***Customer Segmentation*****:** Develop a new customer segmentation model based on profitability and growth potential rather than just relying on volume, with tailored value propositions for each segment.

5.  ***Performance Metrics*****:** Revise sales team incentives to include profitability measures in addition to volume targets.

6.  ***Digital Tools*****:** Invest in pricing analytics software to provide real-time visibility into transaction profitability and to support more informed negotiation and pricing decisions.

#### **The Implementation Challenge**

Maria and the executive team were impressed with James’ analysis, but at the same time, they were concerned about potential customer reactions to the pricing changes. The industry had long-established practices, and customers were accustomed to negotiating aggressively. Some executives feared that changing pricing practices would drive customers to competitors.

Additionally, the sales team expressed resistance to what they saw as limitations on their ability to win deals. The VP of Sales, Robert Chen, argued that the company’s growth had been built around the value propositions of flexibility and customer accommodation. As the last rays of the sun faded away in Midland, Ironwood’s executive team debated about what they should do next.

#### **Suggested Discussion Questions**

1.  Which of James’ strategic options should Ironwood prioritize, and what criteria (e.g., ROI, customer impact) should guide this decision?

2.  How can Ironwood implement pricing changes while minimizing customer pushback and competitive risks?

3.  What steps can James take to align the sales team with the new pricing strategy, addressing their concerns about flexibility?

4.  What KPIs should Ironwood track to evaluate the success of its pricing reforms, and how should they vary by business segment?

5.  How should Ironwood anticipate and counter competitor responses to its pricing changes, considering Permian market dynamics?

#### **Exhibit C: Price Dispersion Analysis**

Price dispersion analysis is the study of variations in prices for the same or similar products or services across different sellers, markets, or time periods. It examines factors driving these differences, such as market competition, customer behavior, information asymmetry, or pricing strategies. Often used in economics and business, it helps firms optimize pricing, assess market efficiency, or understand consumer search costs. For example, analyzing price dispersion in retail might reveal how discounts or geographic factors influence pricing.

James’ price dispersion analysis revealed striking variations in realized prices across seemingly similar customers. The visualization he created plotted each customer’s average pocket price as a percentage of list price, revealing patterns that had previously remained hidden in spreadsheets and disconnected transaction records.

Within the regional refineries segment, which comprised 45% of Ironwood’s sales, pocket prices ranged from 65% to 87% of list price—a 22-percentage-point difference that couldn’t be explained by volume, quality, or location alone. The analysis controlled for product specifications and delivery locations, focusing on comparable transactions to ensure valid comparisons.

When James dug deeper into the data, he found that historical relationship factors played a significant role. Customers who had been with Ironwood since its founding years received substantially deeper discounts than newer customers with similar profiles. Some of these longstanding relationships had accumulated discounts and free services over time through a series of incremental decisions, never evaluated comprehensively.

Another factor driving price dispersion was the variability in account manager practices. Some account managers consistently secured stronger pricing while still maintaining high customer satisfaction, while others more readily offered discounts during negotiations. The analysis revealed that this difference in approach could account for as much as 8 percentage points of price variation within similar customer segments.

Customer sophistication in procurement also emerged as a significant factor. Customers with formalized procurement departments and analytical approaches to supplier negotiations typically received deeper discounts than those with less structured purchasing processes. These sophisticated buyers frequently referenced competitive offers and market indices during negotiations, effectively anchoring discussions at the lower end of the pricing spectrum.

The most concerning finding was that price dispersion showed no correlation with customer profitability or strategic importance. In fact, some of Ironwood’s highest-potential growth accounts were paying premium prices while legacy accounts with limited growth potential received the steepest discounts.

When analyzing price dispersion by product line, James found less variation in natural gas and NGL pricing than in crude oil transactions. This reflected the different market dynamics and competitive landscapes across product categories, with crude oil pricing more susceptible to relationship factors and natural gas pricing more tightly linked to published indices.

James used this analysis to develop a series of price bands for each customer segment, establishing guardrails for account managers that maintained necessary flexibility while reducing unjustified price variation. These bands would serve as the foundation for the more structured approach to pricing that he envisioned for Ironwood’s future.

#### **Exhibit D: Transaction Case Study - “Bad Deal” Example**

**Customer Profile: GulfStream Refining**

- Mid-sized independent refinery (125,000 bpd capacity)

- 10-year relationship with Ironwood

- Annual purchase volume: 3.6 million barrels (10,000 bpd)

- Vocal about competitive offers and frequently threatens to switch suppliers

**Transaction Details:**

- Product: Light sweet crude oil, 40° API

- Order volume: 150,000 barrels (5-day supply for dedicated processing unit)

- Delivery: FOB Midland Terminal with customer-arranged transportation

- Payment terms: Net 45 days

**Pricing Breakdown:**

**Red Flags:**

- Multiple layers of standard discounts plus situational discounts

- No verification of competitor pricing before matching alleged offers

- Free technical support that requires expensive specialized expertise

- Extended payment terms without compensating finance charges

- Customer receives priority production scheduling without premium pricing

- Custom quality testing provided at a minimal charge

- Specialty chemicals manufacturer

- 4-year relationship with Ironwood

- Annual purchase volume: 2.2 million barrels (6,000 bpd)

- Values consistency and quality, with minimal price negotiation

**Transaction Details:**

- Product: Light sweet crude oil, 42° API, ultra-low sulfur custom blend

- Order volume: 100,000 barrels (multi-month supply under predictable schedule)

- Delivery: Pipeline direct to customer facility

- Payment terms: Net 15 days

**Success Factors:**

- Value-based premiums for differentiated product attributes (API grade, sulfur content)

- Predictable ordering pattern enabling efficient production planning

- Low customer service requirements, reducing hidden costs

- Customer values quality and reliability over price alone
