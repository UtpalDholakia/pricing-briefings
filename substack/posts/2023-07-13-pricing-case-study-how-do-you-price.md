*“In terms of shock value, little could compete with a late 19th-century horsehair stuffed sperm whale penis - an impressive 1.67m long. Consigned to the auction house by a private collector, who had owned it for about 20 years.*” - [Sworders Fine Art Auctioneers Catalog](https://www.sworder.co.uk/news/everything-but-the-ordinary/).

Pricing’s fundamental task is to establish and then earn the economic value of objects. But sometimes this can become pretty difficult to pull off. A case in point: an ancient 5 1/2 feet sperm whale penis, a taxidermological specimen, filled with equally ancient horsehair.

Standard methods of establishing value don’t work well. Starting with functional value, believe it or not, the taxidermied whale penis had functionality. According to Matthew Taub, [writing for Atlas Obscura](https://www.atlasobscura.com/articles/sold-a-stuffed-whale-penis-for-6000): “Sailors were said to store tobacco in whale penises like this one, to keep the tobacco moist and fresh over the duration of a long journey.” Perhaps this was useful in 1880, but not today. Considering functional value alone, the artifact’s worth is zero.

Moving to aesthetic appeal, it’s hard to argue for its pleasing appearance. It’s true some people like to pose with dead animals and whale penises are no exception (see below). But this value applies to hunters and fishermen who’ve killed the creature themselves.

What about the price of other similar items? What have 5 1/2 foot taxidermied whale penises sold for in the past? The competitor price approach of valuation doesn’t help either in this case.

The only other stuffed penises were to be found in [a museum in Iceland](https://phallus.is/en/) called the Icelandic Phallological Museum, devoted to “an encyclopedic collection of mammal penises” (see below). Unfortunately, none of these penises at the museum were on sale, so reference prices were not forthcoming.

Before the auction, Sworders the auctioneer selling it, set an estimate of £3,500 - £4,500. The estimate was set arbitrarily, based on “donor input.” When the auction ended, the taxidermied whale penis sold for £3,500, the very bottom of the estimate given in the [lot details brochure](https://www.sworder.co.uk/cms/lotdetailspdf/241971/?o=WVd4VG5sc2VtQWNsZjdPUDdPS1p3a2RUbktSY3BEVklSZS9Rbyt6czJQbz0%3D).

Bidders were equally clueless about the value of the whale penis. They used the auctioneer’s estimate as the basis for their own valuations. We can only wonder what would have happened if the auctioneer had given a different, higher arbitrary estimate in the catalog, or even a narrower range with a higher low-bound, say £4,000 - £4,500.

The bottom line is that for many objects, no one knows the price, not even the experts, not even the market, and not even those who actually shell out the money. The price everyone converges on is nothing more than an arbitrary price or an offshoot.
