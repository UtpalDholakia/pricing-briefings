##### ***Summary***. In my [previous post](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/can-a-lesser-known-retailer-win-over), I found that a lesser-known retailer was consistently rejected when an AI shopping agent followed a strict rule that “it is imperative that the top five products come from well-known merchants,” even when it offered a large discount on the exact same product. Without the rule, matching a well-known retailer’s lowest price was enough to warrant further consideration. In those studies, however, the lesser-known retailers’ listings were always made available to the AI shopping agent. But in practice, the AI agent searches for retailers and decides which ones to consider. ***How does a lesser-known retailer fare when the AI shopping agent searches and then decides which options to consider further?*** In this post, I report the results of a study in which three AI shopping agents completed the entire simulated shopping journey autonomously, searching for retailers, deciding which ones to consider, filtering and ranking the selected options, choosing one, and preparing the final purchase by checking out. Results showed that when the initial search contained only store names, the agents consistently ignored the lesser-known retailer, but when the search results also showed each retailer’s price, the agents considered it further most of the time. Buying sessions with the Meta Muse app found its product catalog to be dominated by well-known manufacturer brands and Walmart, with relatively few independent, lesser-known retailers. These results show that up-front price transparency (i.e., displaying the product price to the agent immediately) is one bright spot for lesser-known retailers in a generally challenging AI shopping environment.

### **An AI Shopping Agent Working Autonomously**

In a [previous post](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/can-a-lesser-known-retailer-win-over), I asked whether a lesser-known retailer can win over an AI shopping agent with a lower price. When I framed this question, the real question in my mind was how much lower a no-name retailer would have to go on price to be included in the same set as well-known retailers. I tested three AI models separately on each of the steps conducted by a shopping agent: (1) sifting through a list of products, (2) ranking the ones that are retained, and (3) recommending one option, using rules replicating those reportedly used by Meta’s Muse shopping agent. I found that the instructions given to the AI agent made a decisive difference. When the agent was instructed to emphasize well-known retailers in selecting its top five choices, the lesser-known retailer was effectively eliminated from consideration, even though it offered a drastic 60% discount on the exact same item. But when the agent was asked only to prefer well-known merchants, thereby giving it more discretion, the lesser-known retailer had to offer only a 5% discount (or match the lowest price from a well-known retailer) to be considered further. However, one important caveat was that in those tests, the lesser-known retailer’s listing was already in the experimental stimulus given to the shopping agent. In reality, AI shopping agents search autonomously and decide which options to include in their initial list on their own. This raises another existential question for lesser-known retailers: Will the AI shopping agent even find them at all, or will they be ignored into oblivion?

For context, there’s quite a bit of research on human shopping search that’s relevant here. The general finding in the decades-old marketing and consumer behavior literature is that shoppers don’t look for as many buying sources to consider as you might expect. For instance, in [one study](https://archive.nyu.edu/handle/2451/29518) of online book buyers, 76% visited a solitary bookstore in the week before their purchase, and only 17% of Amazon buyers considered a second retailer. If the shopper knows a particular brand, they search even less. In a [classic experiment](https://doi.org/10.1086/208544), participants who recognized a particular peanut butter brand chose it without considering other options. In contrast, those who didn’t recognize any brand sampled more options and often chose the better one.

In the 2000s and 2010s, the introduction of price comparison sites was expected to change this sort of shopper behavior, but it did not. For instance, on [one early “shopbot”](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-6451.00162), branded book retailers could still charge a premium over generic sellers, meaning people were willing to pay more to buy the exact same book from a well-known retailer, which the authors attributed to the shoppers’ desire for reliability. On the whole, consumer households that used price comparison sites [allocated about 10%](https://www.nber.org/papers/w9942) of their shopping dollars to lower-priced, non-branded retailers, spending most of their money with well-known brands.

Perhaps not surprisingly, more recent research is finding that AI models have their own biases and inclinations. For instance, AI agents [associate global brands](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.13997) with positive attributes and local brands with negative ones, and have a [strong bias toward listings](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/can-a-lesser-known-retailer-win-over) that appear earlier in the list rather than later. For instance, in a Microsoft simulation, shopping agents [accepted the first acceptable offer](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.25779) in most cases, giving sellers who made offers quickly a 10X-30X advantage over slower responders. In [a recent study](https://searchengineland.com/chatgpt-google-ai-favor-large-retailers-shopping-answers-492590) of approximately 460,000 AI shopping answers, small and large retailers were invoked equally often, but large chains were recommended first approximately 2.5X as often. My own studies in this series are consistent with these emergent findings. In my [first post on machine buyers](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/are-behavioral-pricing-tactics-dying), AI shopping agents were far more inclined to choose whichever of two comparable products was listed first. And in my [previous post](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/can-a-lesser-known-retailer-win-over), a rule favoring well-known retailers kept a lesser-known retailer off the top five list generated by AI agents, even though it offered the exact same blender at a price 60% lower than well-known retailers. In this post, I extend the consideration of lesser-known retailers to a more realistic scenario in which the AI shopping agent undertakes the entire shopping journey autonomously, starting by identifying retailers to consider further and concluding with the submission of the approval card.

### **Procedure**

In this study, the AI shopping agent completed the entire shopping journey autonomously, allowing me to study two research questions: (1) Does the AI shopping agent find the lesser-known retailer at all on its own? (2) When it does find the retailer, do the rules of strict recognition and soft recognition tested in the previous study have the same effects?

To answer these questions, I ran a total of 495 simulated shopping sessions in two conditions using the same three AI models as the previous post: Meta’s Muse Spark 1.3, OpenAI’s GPT-6.1 Sol, and Anthropic’s Claude Sonnet 5.5. The agents shopped in one of two versions of a simulated store, which differed in what they could see when searching for stores to buy the blender. In the *names-only version* (135 sessions), the store search results showed only each retailer’s name and a generic description, so the agent had to decide which stores to explore without any sense of the prices they might charge. The purpose of this version was to study retailer discovery by AI shopping agents. In the second *price-included version* (360 sessions), the store search results showed not only the retailer but also its featured blender and price, providing greater up-front price transparency earlier in the agent’s shopping journey. In both the names-only and price-included versions, I exposed each of the three AI shopping agents to the three rule conditions (no rule, soft recognition rule, strict recognition rule; see [Table 2 from the previous post](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/can-a-lesser-known-retailer-win-over)).

To replicate how current shopping agents work, I built a simulated store in which an AI shopping agent could use six tools. These tools included (1) searching a product catalog, (2) searching a directory of stores, (3) opening a store’s page, (4) recording a shortlist (which products it retained, its top five choices, and its recommendation), (5) opening the checkout page, and (6) submitting the approval card the shopper could confirm before the purchase. Each simulated shopping session began with a shopper’s request for a blender and ended either when the shopping agents submitted their approval card or after 12 turns (which was never reached in any session). The rules used were identical to the ones reported in [my previous post](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/can-a-lesser-known-retailer-win-over) (see Table 2 there), and the figure below shows the process.

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/2cf735b2-7937-4a5e-80f1-fdca06be0772_2951x1065.png)

Figure 1. The AI shopping agent’s journey through the simulated store.

As in the previous studies in this series, the focal blender was listed at \$80 by Target, Best Buy, Walmart, and Kohl’s. The challenger was the same blender sold by a lesser-known retailer with a fictitious name, such as Hearthline Supply, at \$72 (10% discount) or \$48 (40% discount). The challenger was sold only in its own store, which always appeared in the second position in the list of 12 options in the store search results, and it never appeared in the catalog. Thus, to be considered, the challenger had to be found by the AI shopping agent. The next figure shows an example of the stimulus.

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/b7bb651d-ada0-403f-8178-4b432a40a14f_3280x2407.png)

Figure 2. Example of an experimental stimulus used in the study.

Two factors were manipulated in the study. First, the store search results were manipulated such that in the names-only version of the simulated store, only the store’s name and generic description were provided, whereas in the price-included version, each search result showed the store’s featured blender and its price, i.e., the focal blender where the store sold it, and the store’s cheapest blender. Second, as in the previous study, rules were manipulated so that the AI shopping agent was assigned to a “no rule” baseline condition, the price rule (discard products outside the normal price range) + the soft recognition rule condition (prefer products from well-known merchants while weighing that preference against price and product suitability, or the price rule + the strict recognition rule condition (imperative that top five products come from well-known merchants). For purposes of tractability (and also budget constraints), each combination of the two factors was tested on the three shopping agents with 15 sessions per condition for the names-only versions at a price of \$48 for the focal blender and 20 sessions per condition for the price-included version.

After adopting the study design, I preregistered three predictions on [AsPredicted](https://aspredicted.org/bd76td.pdf): (1) under the strict recognition rule, the challenger would be retained among the top five choices in very few, if any, sessions irrespective of shopping agent or price, (2) when compared to the no-rule baseline, agents in the strict recognition rule condition would result in a significantly lower inclusion of the challenger in the top five choices, and (3) the shopping agents would be more likely to consider the challenger’s store when prices were shown than when they were not. As discussed below, all three hypotheses were supported by the results. The rest of the findings discussed in the next section were uncovered post-hoc. The data and code for this study are available in this [ResearchBox](https://researchbox.org/10540%20(CODE:%20KAMXJU)).

### **Results**

#### ***1) The impact of showing prices up-front on store search outcomes***

A key goal of the study was to examine the impact of up-front price transparency on the challenger’s likelihood of being considered by the AI shopping agents. Results showed that when the store search results displayed only the names, two of the three shopping agents almost never found or considered the challenger (priced at \$48, a substantial discount from the standard \$80 for the same blender at well-known retailers). Under the prices-included conditions, the challenger, whether priced at \$48 or \$72, was almost always found and considered further. See the figure below for the results.

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/4c0e3df7-bde0-414a-b3bc-d7da5e14d7ef_2160x1006.png)

Figure 3. Percentage of simulated shopping sessions in which the agent considered the challenger’s store by condition.

Furthermore, under the names-only condition, Muse Spark considered Target, Best Buy, and Walmart in nearly every shopping session, and in 31 of 45 sessions (69%), these were the only three retailers it considered. It never considered the challenger’s store even once. Sonnet rarely considered any stores, instead using the catalog (See Figure 1), with only 4 store visits across 45 sessions, including one to the challenger’s store. GPT-6.1 Sol was the exception in the sense that it considered 4 or 5 stores in each shopping session, including the challenger in the set in 33 of 45 sessions (or 73%). Under the prices-included conditions at \$48 and \$60, as shown in Figure 3, all three agents found and considered the challenger further in almost all simulated shopping sessions.

#### ***2) The impact of the strict recognition rule on the lesser-known retailer’s consideration***

The strict recognition rule specifically targets the shopping agent’s selection of the top five choices, which first requires the agent to search for options available from the different retailers. Interestingly, in GPT-6.1 Sol’s case, the rule also influenced its earlier search decision. In the names-only condition, Sol found the challenger in all 30 sessions across the no-rule baseline and the soft recognition rule conditions combined, but in only 3 of 15 sessions (or 20%) under the strict recognition rule condition. In 10 of the 15 strict recognition rule condition shopping sessions, the first five stores it accessed were Target, Walmart, Best Buy, Kohl’s, and Wayfair.

This pattern is quite consistent with the classic peanut butter experiment referenced earlier in the sense that recognizing a brand (in that case, a manufacturer’s brand; here, retailer brands) reduced the shopper’s willingness to sample alternatives. However, it must be noted that in the present case, the shopping agents were explicitly instructed to privilege well-known retailers, whereas the human shoppers in the peanut study relied on the brand recognition heuristic on their own. Nevertheless, the consequence for the lesser-known retailer is the same. A rule intended to determine which retailers appear in the smaller consideration set is found to restrict which retailers are considered in the first place, and it hurts the small, lesser-known businesses.

#### ***3) The impact of the strict recognition rule on the shopping agent’s final recommendation***

The results showed that the strict recognition rule influenced the shopping agents’ buying process in nuanced ways. In every one of the 115 strict recognition-rule shopping sessions in which the agent found the challenger, the challenger was not retained in the top five options. However, interestingly, the rule did not have the same degree of influence on the agent’s final recommendation, as shown in the next figure. Muse Spark recommended the challenger as its final choice in 9 of 40 sessions (23%), while GPT-6.1 Sol did so in 4 sessions (10%).

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/88b25665-ffa8-475d-8579-1d9c791179c9_2160x1042.png)

FIgure 4. Inclusion of challenger in the top five choices and the final recommendation under the strict recognition rule by model.

The reasons given by the two agents for doing this were different. Sol’s approvals all pointed out that the retailer was unknown so that the human shopper would consider this in their final choice, saying things like “Hearthline Supply is a lesser-known merchant; the product rating does not establish the merchant’s reliability. Please approve only if you’re comfortable with the trade-off.” On the other hand, Muse Spark only noted the savings, saying things like “Same Corvo B500 sells for \$80 at Walmart/ Target/ Best Buy – this listing at \$72 with free shipping saves you \$8.” Figure 5 shows examples of two such approval cards. In contrast to Sol and Muse Spark, Sonnet never recommended the lesser-known retailer in sessions operating under the strict recognition rule.

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/e67fa9b2-2062-4400-b007-512da28ed104_5600x2528.png)

Figure 5. Examples of approval cards recommending the lesser-known retailer under the strict recognition rule

One interpretation of these findings is that Sol and Muse Spark applied the rule narrowly, only to the short list of five options to consider, and then accounted for the shopper’s interest in saving money separately when choosing which specific option to recommend as its final option. Whether this is a flaw in how the agent’s decision making process unfolds, or something that favors the consumer depends on the intention behind the strict recognition rule. On the one hand, it can be argued that it doesn’t make sense to recommend an item that is not in the top five list, whereas on the other, it can be argued that the agent is fighting back against the seemingly biased instruction it has been given by its designer in favor of doing what the shopper actually wants. Either way, this deviation from the conventional consumer decision making process ends up benefiting the smaller, lesser-known retailer.

#### ***4) The impact of the strict recognition rule on the filtering step of the shopping journey***

In the case of Claude Sonnet 5.5, the strict recognition rule also affected the agent’s filtering step, which occurs before ranking. Instead of being governed by the price rule alone, in shopping sessions that also imposed the strict recognition rule, Sonnet did not retain the challenger at the filter stage in 15 of 17 sessions (or 88%) at the \$72 price point and in all 20 sessions at the \$48 price point. For context, in the previous study, when filtering was examined separately rather than in a consolidated way as here, Sonnet had retained the challenger in 30 of 30 trials at \$72 and in 18 of 30 trials (60%) at \$48.

In understanding this discrepancy, it turned out that under the strict recognition rule, only 9 of the 324 listings retained by Sonnet (or only about 3%) came from lesser-known retailers, when compared with 40% under the no-rule baseline and 38% under the soft recognition rule. In effect, it appears that Sonnet interpreted the instruction that “the top five must come from well-known merchants” to mean that only well-known retailers are eligible to be considered, and dropped virtually all lesser-known retailers right away. Muse Spark and Sol did not exhibit this propensity. Under the strict rule, lesser-known retailers made up 35% of Muse Spark’s retained listings (compared to 44% in the no-rule baseline), and the corresponding number was 44% for Sol (compared to 44% in the no-rule baseline). Thus, the key practical takeaway is that in understanding an AI shopping agent’s decision making process, testing each step of the process separately may provide a distorted view of how the decision making unfolds in a consolidated way. A rule that is targeted by the AI platform for one stage of the decision making process may influence other stages of the shopping agent, then affecting subsequent steps, and the final choice.

#### ***5) The impact of a deep discount on AI shopping agent decision making***

In my [previous study](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/can-a-lesser-known-retailer-win-over), the price rule (discard products outside the normal price range) led the shopping agents to exclude a challenger that was discounted at 40% or greater relative to the reference price. In the present study, where the shopping journey was treated holistically, this result disappeared. Under the price rule and the soft recognition rule, at a price of \$48, the challenger was retained in the filtering process across all sessions of all AI models. One explanation for this finding is that, in the current study, the agent considered an average of 33 listings during a simulated shopping session, many of which were budget blenders. Thus, a \$48 blender may not have stood out as much and may not have seemed like an “abnormal” price in this lower-priced, more popular set of options.

Under the no-price rule or the soft recognition rule, any discount was sufficient to be selected through the choice process. At a price of \$48 (when the competing options were all \$80), all three models recommended the challenger in 20 of 20 sessions. At a 10% discount (i.e., a price of \$72), Muse Spark still recommended it in 90% of its shopping sessions, Sol in 50%-90%, and Sonnet in 60%-75%, depending on the condition.

#### ***6) Results of actual shopping tests with the Muse app***

As described earlier, in addition to the simulated shopping sessions, I also conducted actual shopping tests through the Muse app. Specifically, I made ten shopping requests in Meta’s Muse app, each in a new chat, and recorded the 101 product listings produced. The app responded to shopping requests using its existing product catalog, without browsing individual retailers’ websites. Its responses showed groups of product cards, each providing the brand or retailer’s name, website, and price. Walmart was referenced most often (19 times), followed by Williams-Sonoma (7 times) and eBay (6 times). The product brand’s own website was referenced 39 times, national retailers 38 times, and independent retailers only 7 times. Figure 6 below shows an example of product cards shown by Meta’s Muse app.

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/f46b8f64-bf44-43ae-b459-d60d02ea9920_1254x1362.png)

Figure 6. Screenshot of product cards from the Muse app.

The presence of independent retailers was category-dependent in Muse’s lists. For the 3 general blender requests, none of the options provided by Muse were small, independent retailers. For coffee grinders, small coffee roasters appeared in 2 of 14 cards and 3 of 6 cards when the request set a \$50 spending limit. All 17 yoga mat cards provided information from the brands’ own websites, and several were small, lesser-known brands such as Yoloha, Yogo, and Asivana (that I, for one, had never heard of before; but then my knowledge of yoga mats is not great). Thus, at least based on my small sample of requests, it appears that small brands selling directly to consumers are faring better on Meta Muse at the moment than small retailers selling other companies’ products.

There were also several other interesting findings from these shopping tests. First, when asked to seek out the lowest price, Muse was quite effective. For the Ninja Professional Plus blender, for instance, it recommended the \$89.99 listing on Amazon or Walmart; Google Shopping’s lowest price for this SKU that day was \$89.99 at Walmart. For the Vitamix 5200, it recommended the \$279.95 listing through a deal website, well below Google Shopping’s lowest price of \$499.95.

Second, when asked to find “the cheapest good blender” (an oxymoron?), Muse did not find the cheapest blender; instead, it tried to balance both attributes. For instance, Muse chose a \$39.99 Hamilton Beach blender from the brand’s website over a \$22.88 Hamilton Beach blender offering, citing its superior power, blades, and warranty. Third, Muse routinely showed the same product at two prices side by side from two different retailers, very much like the simulated shopping sessions in the study. For instance, it showed a Fellow Opus 2 grinder priced at \$199.95 at Williams-Sonoma, alongside a card showing the exact same grinder at \$249.95 on the brand’s own website.

Fourth, Muse took liberties with requests about the merchant. For instance, when asked to find a product from “a small independent store,” it offered three brands’ own websites (Koblenz, Hamilton Beach, and Nutribullet), and only one actual independent retailer, Riverbend Home. When asked for a yoga mat from “a brand that sells direct,” its first card was a SuperMats yoga mat sold by Wayfair. It seems that Muse may still be working out the terminology that shoppers use colloquially. Fifth, Muse personalized its product cards by location, even though I did not ask it to. For instance, 6 of the 10 options referenced the greater Houston area, where I live. One suggested a local scratch-and-dent appliance outlet, providing its address and telephone number, and another checked Facebook Marketplace listings nearby. A local store that is in the AI shopping agent’s catalog, or easy to find on the web, may therefore be shown to local shoppers in ways not predicted by a study of national scale.

### **Study Limitations**

The following limitations of this study must be acknowledged. First, the shopping sessions used a simulated store, a highly stylized request, a single product category (countertop blenders), and a single reference price (\$80). Furthermore, the challenger’s store always appeared second of 12 in the list and was never in the catalog, limiting the generalizability of the findings. Most of these things were consistent with prior studies in this series, and the limitations are primarily due to budget constraints. With a couple of thousand dollars more to spend at today’s AI inference prices, the study could be expanded easily to cover more store variations, different requests, product categories, and prices.

Second, the Meta Muse app itself was tested separately and in a smaller, observational way, with only 10 sessions from a single account, with one run per request. I could not identify 7 of the 101 sellers, and I checked the lowest price only on Google Shopping, rather than really looking for the lowest available price. Finally, I did not actually buy anything (otherwise my house would be full of blenders for making smoothies), and the results are highly context-specific. As these models are updated and newer ones are released, AI shopping agents may vary significantly. One thing this study, and the three previous ones in this series, does is establish a baseline and a fairly complete portrait of AI shopping agent behavior as of the fall of 2026, which may be super helpful for future comparisons.

### **Practical Implications (mostly for lesser-known retailers)**

The findings here are, by and large, consistent with the previous study where I concluded that “for small, lesser-known retailers that are dealing with or will deal with rule-bound AI shopping agents in the future, these results are ominous.” In the simulated shopping sessions conducted with AI agents in this study, when only retailer names were provided, the lesser-known store was pretty much condemned to obscurity by two of the agents regardless of price, never even making it into the AI agent’s list. But when the retailer made its featured product and price visible in the search results, the lesser-known retailer reached the consideration stage.

The significance of this finding cannot be overstated. All retailers, but particularly smaller ones, need to pay close attention to how their offers appear in catalogs, product feeds, and search results used by AI shopping agents. At a minimum, the listed products need to be identified correctly, and current prices and availability need to be provided, all on a real-time basis (or as close to it as feasible). Providing this information once the customer (or agent) visits the website is simply not enough; the retailer’s website may never be reached by the agent.

A second implication is that retailers need to distinguish among stages in the buying journey, such as being found, making the top-five list, and being recommended. Unlike our traditional models of (human) consumer behavior, my findings here suggest that these decisions do not operate as a funnel, in which earlier decisions completely determine later ones. While a retailer could be discovered in the first stage, it may not make it to the short list of options consistent with a funnel model; it can be excluded from the top five list and then recommended, unlike in a funnel model.

Third, this study shows that while price remains an effective means of competing for lesser-known retailers once agents consider it, its actual influence depends on the rules under which the agent operates and the other options in the set. In the absence of the strict recognition rule, for example, the \$48 challenger (i.e., a 40% discount on the very same blender) was recommended every time under the price-included condition. But under the strict recognition rule, it never made it to the top five. Moreover, my results suggested that whether a low price appeared abnormal depended on whether the agent encountered more budget-priced products. This suggests that the lesser-known retailer may need to keep an eye on which other offers its offers are being compared against, rather than assuming that a deeper discount will always work in ways we are used to.

Finally, my findings suggest that lesser-known retailers may need to deal with their low familiarity by boosting their signals of reliability. For example, while recommending the product from the lesser-known retailer, Sol’s warnings explicitly distinguished the product’s overall attractiveness from the merchant’s reliability. Thus, a lesser-known retailer may need to provide credible evidence of fulfillment performance, return handling, and customer service to thoughtfully counter concerns about being unfamiliar to the shopper. While I did not test whether such information will affect the shopping agent’s choices and make it more amenable to lesser-known retailers, the distinction introduced is important. Selling a well-reviewed blender at a really attractive price does not, by itself, establish that the retailer will deliver it successfully. All in all, these results show that up-front price transparency (i.e., displaying the product price to the agent immediately) is one bright spot for lesser-known retailers in a generally challenging AI shopping environment.
