As readers of the Pricing Conundrum will know from these notes on [the Four Pillars of Pricing Strategy](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/the-four-pillars-of-pricing-strategy) and the [Value Pricing Framework](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/the-value-pricing-framework-updated)[^1], a consistent process that carefully considers the four pricing decision inputs – costs, customer value, reference prices, and the value proposition contributes to effective pricing decisions:

> *“The Value Pricing framework begins with the four pricing decision inputs, the so-called four pillars of pricing. Of these, three inputs help to establish the price, and the fourth input regulates the first three by determining their relative importance in the firm’s pricing strategy. The three pricing decision inputs are the company’s costs that are relevant to the pricing decision, the customer value, expressed in terms of the buyer’s monetary valuation of the benefits provided by the offering, and reference prices used by customers to make buying decisions. The fourth input, the firm’s adopted value proposition, regulates the respective weights of costs, customer value, and reference prices in the pricing decision.”*

It is true that managers and entrepreneurs can readily make pricing decisions by using just one or at most two of the four pricing decision inputs. Many of them do exactly this. Marking up a set of well-identified costs is the most popular pricing approach, with no attention given to reference prices or customer value. It is the default for many companies.

### Using a single input in pricing decisions

When setting menu prices, most restaurants select a set of costs to track (commonly food) and then mark them up by a certain predetermined percentage to arrive at the price. In the restaurant industry, this is known as the Ideal Food Cost Pricing Model (IFC)[^2]. When using the IFC model (or a variation), restaurants first track their food costs and non-food costs using one of numerous software options available, such as Toast, Restaurant365, Marginedge, Wisk.ai, Piecemeal.io, or QuickBooks (or even pencil and paper if an operator is old school). Food costs are simply the costs of ingredients, and non-food costs are everything else, including things like rent, utilities, labor costs, taxes, and insurance[^3].

Once these two costs have been calculated, then, somewhat arbitrarily, the operator sets an IFC percentage value. A common rule of thumb used by many restaurant operators is that IFC should be 30% of the menu price, but a fine dining restaurant may use a higher value, say 33% or 35%, if it uses relatively expensive ingredients, whereas a labor-intensive operator like a bakery may use a lower value like 23%.

The IFC percentage value serves two purposes. First, it works as a target. The restaurant wants to keep the food cost value below this number, and if it drifts up, the operator will have leeway to make changes to the supplier, to the menu, or to the recipe, to bring it back in line. Second, the IFC percentage value is the only input into setting the price. Whatever IFC value is used by the operator, the pricing formula is the same:

## Food Cost \* (1/IFC percentage) = Price.

Here’s an example showing how this pricing method works. Imagine you run a fine dining restaurant, and French onion soup has been on your menu for a long time in the starter section. Let’s also say a bowl of this soup costs you \$4.00 to make at the moment. With the IFC method, you will mark it up to \$4/30%, and list it on your menu at \$13.33 (perhaps you may round it up to \$13.50). The logic behind the IFC method is quite reasonable, that the \$9.33 markup will cover all your other costs (non-food costs in the restaurant industry terminology ), and hopefully, there will be something left over to leave you with a profit. This is the strict application of the IFC method using the single input of costs to make the pricing decision for each item on your menu.

Usually, the method is modified to make it more sensitive to a particular restaurant, such as to account for differences in the popularity of menu items given its clientele, the restaurant’s reputation and preferred brand image such as upscale trattoria or exclusive fine dining, and even the basis of its competitiveness relative to others in the area, **using only costs** as the input to make the pricing decision.

This can be done in any number of ways: by using different IFC percentages for different menu items (e.g., a lower value for more popular items), varying the IFC percentages for special occasions to take advantage of predictable demand (e.g., a lower IFC value for seasonal menus like Valentine’s week menu and Thanksgiving week menu, a higher value for happy-hour menu, and so on), and even including some non-food costs like hourly employee labor into the formula (and adjusting the markup down).

While cost is the most common single input in pricing decisions across companies and industries, the other inputs are also used in isolation. Perhaps the most obvious case of using customer value alone in the pricing decision is the use of a pure [Pay What You Want](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/when-does-pay-what-you-want-pricing?utm_source=publication-search) pricing method. As I’ve explained in [another piece](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/when-does-pay-what-you-want-pricing?utm_source=publication-search):

> *PWYW pricing is simple: Provide a good-quality product to customers, let them enjoy it, and then give them complete discretion to pay whatever they want for the product. No bills, no claims (although sometimes there may be a posted suggested price), pay if you want to (and as much as you want), don’t if you don’t. The business logic behind PWYW pricing is two-fold. First, the company conveys confidence, essentially telling the customer, “We are so sure you will like our product that you will pay for it.” The second reason behind PWYW pricing is to empower customers. The company gives total control to the customer to weigh the value they have gotten from the product, and pay an appropriate price (weigh then pay).*

As a social marketing campaign, the CPG brand Newman’s Own [has a food truck](https://newmansown.com/newmans-own-inc-kicks-off-pay-what-you-want-food-truck-tour-on-tax-day/) that it has been sending out into different cities over the past two years ([20 stops in 2024](https://newmansown.com/newmans-own-inc-kicks-off-pay-what-you-want-food-truck-tour-on-tax-day/) and [over 100 in 2025](https://newmansown.com/food-truck/)) on a “Pay What You Want” Food Truck Tour. The food truck sells pizzas, salads, and other food items, and customers decide how much they want to pay for whatever they purchase. All the earnings from the food truck sales are donated to the brand’s charitable foundation.

[Embedded content: www.tiktok.com](https://www.tiktok.com/player/v1/7493642945120177451)

In a [press release in April 2025](https://newmansown.com/newmans-own-inc-kicks-off-pay-what-you-want-food-truck-tour-on-tax-day/), the brand explained:

> *“The Pay What You Want Food Truck first launched last year serving pizza and making over 20 stops to test the power of the pay-what-you-want model. Across all cities, customers paid more than the average retail prices, proving that people give generously when they know their purchase makes a difference. This year, the truck will visit communities surrounding cities such as New York City, Philadelphia, Washington D.C., Charlotte and Atlanta, bringing delicious meals and snacks made from high-quality, recognizable-ingredients to local, family-friendly events.”*

As should be clear with this example, the only input into setting the price of the food sold by this truck is customer value. Neither the costs nor any reference prices come into play in the pricing decision. Finally, let’s consider the pricing approach of British shoe brand [Oliver Cabell for its Men’s Low 1 Shoe](https://olivercabell.com/products/low-1-belmont-mens), which is priced at \$199.99[^4].

In explaining its pricing, Oliver Cabell uses the [following graphic](https://olivercabell.com/products/low-1-belmont-mens):

As can be seen in the figure, Oliver Cabell’s pricing strategy is to position its brand relative to other brands it has identified as its competitors. The shoe’s \$199.99 price should be evaluated in the context of what its price should be (\$220), which itself is with reference to comparable shoes from competitor brands Saint Laurent, Maison Margiela, and Common Projects. The cost to produce the shoe is conveyed as equivalent to other brands in the figure. Customer Value is not considered at all, either, but there’s an implicit assumption that by offering a price that is less than half the price of its competitors’ shoes, the customers will react positively. The brand’s value proposition is also not clear from the pricing strategy or the explanation.

In all the cases so far, only one of the three pricing inputs, costs, customer value, and reference prices, is used to make the pricing decision. The other two pricing inputs are not considered. However, the preferred way to make effective pricing decisions, as advocated in the [Value Pricing Framework,](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/the-value-pricing-framework-updated) is to use all three inputs to obtain greater insight into what a “good price” should be for the offering, and use the value proposition to calibrate the weights given to the three inputs.

### Using multiple inputs for the pricing decision

Contrary to the IFC method, which only marks up costs, some restaurants also consider the price of competitive offerings as a second input into their pricing decision of individual menu items. This approach is called the [competition factor](https://yukaichou.com/chou-musings/restaurant-advice-how-much-should-you-price-your-menu-items/) because both food costs and competitors’ prices are considered in setting menu prices. For pricing a bowl of French onion soup, it may turn out that the restaurant’s main competitor sells a comparable soup for \$16.00 (while another nearby restaurant that is less upscale sells their version for \$12). Recall that the IFC method yielded a recommendation of \$13.33. Using the competitor factor method provides another price signal for the pricing decision, the main competitor’s price of \$16.00. Given the fine dining ethos of the restaurant and its preference to convey quality, it could price the bowl of soup anywhere between \$13.33 and \$16 using a combination of the two approaches, or even go higher than the competitor, say \$17 or \$17.50.

According to the *Value Pricing Framework*, even using this method, considering two input factors is still incomplete because the other two inputs are not utilized. The operator did not consciously account for the restaurant’s value proposition, which is the image it wants to convey to guests with its menu prices. Does it want to be seen as a casual upscale trattoria in a rich neighborhood that some customers visit for dinner several times a week? Or is it aspiring to be a special event destination restaurant where couples go for a celebration dinner or to get engaged? It makes little sense to charge the same price for the French onion soup in these two cases. In fact, irrespective of costs or competitor prices, customers would expect the dish to have a significantly higher price in the destination restaurant positioning than in the neighborhood trattoria positioning[^5].

Nor did the restaurant consider how much its customers value a bowl of French onion soup, how much they are willing to pay for it, and whether they care if it’s priced at \$13, \$17, \$20, or more, or whether the item can be offered for a higher price during a certain period of the year. For instance, it could be that the restaurant has received positive reviews from famous food critics and bloggers, and patrons are clamoring for a reservation. Or the city is in the middle of a deep recession, and hardly anyone wants to spend even \$10, let alone \$17, for a bowl of soup (even if it's hearty and delicious). Or French onion soup is trending on TikTok right now, and everyone wants to order it and shoot a video eating it. Each of these situations will warrant a different price.

The restaurant would consider its value proposition, customer value, and the pricing environment in addition to its costs and reference prices to determine a price for its French onion soup (and everything else on its menu) when using the *Value Pricing Framework*.

The figure below summarizes the role played by the four decision inputs in pricing the soup. Costs set the floor, meaning that the restaurant must charge more than \$4 if it wants to cover its food costs, and more than \$7 if it wants to cover all its variable costs for the bowl of soup. Since customers are unwilling to pay more than \$20, this is the ceiling on the price. The prices of the competitors, \$12 (nearest restaurant) and \$16 (major competitor), and the restaurant’s own previous price, \$10.50, provide the reference prices, forming the range of reasonable prices. The restaurant’s value proposition, to serve as a neighborhood café, dictates the weights that will be assigned to costs (W1), customer value (W2), and reference prices (W3). Because it wants to be seen as an affordable restaurant to visit frequently for dinner, it may choose to keep prices close to its main competitor’s price or even a little lower, weighing reference prices more, and costs and customer value to a lesser degree.

Role of the four pricing decision inputs in pricing a bowl of French onion soup

In the pricing calculus of the French onion soup (and every other item on the restaurant’s menu), perhaps the hardest input to consider accurately is customer value because of its instability and its contingency on the prevailing pricing environment. Seemingly idiosyncratic occurrences like a video of someone slurping the soup with delight going viral will send customer willingness to pay and the soup’s valuation through the stratosphere. Even \$20 will be too low then. On the other hand, if a video of someone fishing out a baby cockroach from the soup goes viral instead, no one will want it at any price for weeks or months afterward. Adding to such random things over which the operator has little control, observable and trackable environmental factors also have an impact. For example, the current period is decidedly challenging for restaurants, as [many Americans have cut back](https://www.wsj.com/personal-finance/young-american-consumer-spending-cut-f2b482e5?) on discretionary spending, and factors like [oversaturation, economic anxiety, and reduced tourism,](https://www.miamiherald.com/miami-com/restaurants/article310168345.html) coupled with inflation, are making pricing very challenging. Choosing to price high is decidedly risky right now.

When working out the different pricing inputs, managers and entrepreneurs should realize that customer value and other pricing decision inputs are not fixed quantities that are available and ready to be measured, combined, and converted into a corresponding price. The value derived by customers is a moving target, contingent on the environment in which the customers and the company operate. The same thing is true of the other pricing decision inputs. Each input is affected by the environment. Effective pricing decisions can only be made after understanding and accounting for how the pricing environment affects the four decision inputs.
