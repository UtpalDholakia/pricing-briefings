##### ***Summary:*** Many seemingly modern pricing practices are far, far older than we think. In the Middle Ages, the Catholic Church sold indulgences whose benefits the buyer could never verify. It had no competitors and no benchmark to set price against. Yet it deployed a pricing strategy that seems remarkably modern, with fairness-based price discrimination, tiered pricing, an effective GTM strategy, branded marketing collateral, a fear-based sales pitch, and a scaling technology that transformed unit economics. This medieval-era pricing case study also reveals the limits of pricing power, showing how a price structure built around a single shared narrative can erode quickly when that narrative loses its appeal.

If you want an idea of just how old some of the core concepts of modern pricing strategy are[^1], consider this scenario. It is 1517, and you are a prosperous Catholic textile merchant living in Mainz. Like everyone else, you are a sinner and deeply concerned that when you die, you will go straight to Purgatory for a long, long time before you will be allowed to enter Heaven. You are not looking forward to the agony that awaits, but it seems unavoidable; it is the only way to be cleansed of your sins and unholy attachments to this world.

Now, along comes an offer from Pope Leo himself in Rome: “Buy a Certificate of Indulgence[^2] for 3 florins (approximately 1.5 percent of your annual income) and receive remission of the temporal punishment still due for sins already forgiven.” A “bypass Purgatory” coupon for a very low price!

A fundamental task of pricing strategy is to establish and then capture the economic value of an object. But what happens when the object in question is entirely invisible, has zero functional utility as long as the purchaser lives[^3], and has no way of proving it works after they’ve died? To make it even harder, there is no competitive benchmark and no way to get a money-back guarantee[^4]. The seller and the verifier are the same institution, and the core benefit is a shortcut to eternity in heaven. The sale of indulgences was, no doubt, controversial. But it is also a fascinating early case study in pricing strategy. What follows is a look at the many plays in the modern pricing playbook that were already in place five centuries ago.

#### **The Treasury of Merit**

How could the Church offer these certificates in the first place? The narrative behind the value-creating mechanism for indulgences was compelling. Jesus, Mary, and the Saints had lived such holy lives that they accumulated a limitless surplus of goodness and virtue, a so-called *thesaurus meritorum*, that the Pope was authorized to dispense to ordinary sinners[^5].

The Treasury of Merit addressed both the supply and legitimacy challenges. The supply of virtue to fuel the indulgences was infinite, so the marginal cost of issuing one more certificate of indulgence was effectively zero. And the only legitimate distributor was the Pope, because only he held the keys to this infinite treasury.

#### **Setting the Price of a Certificate of Indulgence**

Standard methods for establishing value or setting price didn’t work well in this space. The certificate’s functional value was minimal (a small scrap of parchment), and its experiential value was intangible, based entirely on faith and belief. There were no competitors, and there was no baseline. So how do you price something like this?

Historical evidence suggests that the Church relied on a combination of spiritual authority and the strong aversive human motivation of avoiding agony and suffering. The implied valuation question to the purchaser was, “What is the price of reducing the punishment attached to all the sins you have already committed and confessed?” That’s a really hard question to answer, given there is no anchor and no reference price. The only real price-setting constraint to the seller is how many florins are in the buyer’s wallet.

Having this latitude, the Church bypassed customer value entirely, setting prices based on its funding requirements. When Pope Leo X wanted to accelerate the construction of St. Peter’s Basilica, he leaned heavily on revenue from indulgence sales[^6]. What’s more, the 1515 Instructio Summaria, the official handbook given to Church staff, set out one of the earliest documented, formally written, income-graduated, i.e., tiered-price structures in European history[^7]. Kings, queens, and archbishops were expected to pay 25 florins, counts, barons, and abbots 10 florins, merchants and local elites (like our textile merchant) 3 florins, while ordinary citizens got out of Purgatory for a single florin, approximately a week’s income[^8]. (The destitute were subsidized by the others; they were instructed to give nothing but prayer, on the explicit grounds that “the kingdom of heaven should be open to the poor no less than to the rich”).

Note that the Instructio Summaria is doing two things at once. First, it is engaging in *reasonable* price discrimination based on willingness and ability to pay, using the exact same logic that we use today for any tiered pricing scheme, whether it is airline tickets, SaaS, or [ticket prices to see the Taj Mahal](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/why-dual-ticket-pricing-at-indias?). Second, it is dressing up price discrimination in the moral language of “access for all,” boosting its fairness quotient. In other words, the same fairness rhetoric that justifies a “good” product today in a [Good-Better-Best scheme](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/pricing-concept-understanding-the?) was justifying access to heaven more than five centuries ago.

#### **The Medieval Go-to-Market Strategy**

In addition to setting price, the Church also needed a Go-to-Market Strategy for indulgences. Here, too, the 1517 St. Peter’s Basilica building campaign was based on a remarkably modern multi-tier distribution structure in Germany. At the top was Pope Leo X. Below him, Archbishop Albert of Brandenburg held the right to sell indulgences authorized by the Pope across his three dioceses[^9]. Below the Archbishop was the Dominican friar Johann Tetzel, who managed the field operation, the 16th-century version of a commissioned salesperson.

Friar Tetzel’s field operation was as good an example of an effective go-to-market strategy as you can find. He traveled with an entourage and sent advance teams to towns to build up anticipation. When he entered a town, he bore a large cross emblazoned with the papal coat of arms, and preached fear-based sermons in which he often channeled the voices of dead relatives of audience members, crying out from the flames of Purgatory. It was a pitch-perfect fear appeal: Buy an indulgence and bypass this torment.

When the printing press was invented, the sale of indulgences scaled up. Interestingly, the earliest precisely dated European document printed with movable metal type is a printed indulgence from 1454; the better-known Gutenberg Bible was completed around the same period. Before the printing press, indulgences were written out by hand. Now, they could be printed by the thousands.

#### **The Lifecycle of Indulgences**

Indulgences began as something far more modest and meaningful (in a religious sense) than what they became later on. In the early Christian church, bishops could reduce or commute the heavy penances they had assigned to contrite sinners, accepting a pilgrimage or an act of charity in place of years of fasting or public humiliation. There was compassion and reason behind this logic. Pope Urban II formalized the idea of a plenary indulgence, i.e., the full removal of all temporal punishment due to forgiven sins, in 1095 during the First Crusade, granting it to those who participated in the war. This set the stage for centuries of “value-based” spiritual rewards.

In 1300, Pope Boniface VIII declared the first “Jubilee Year,” drawing roughly 200,000 pilgrims to Rome, according to the Florentine chronicler Giovanni Villani[^10]. They came to visit the basilicas of Saint Peter and Saint Paul a prescribed number of times and received an indulgence in return for their devout pilgrimage. Over the next century or so, the Church added the option of making financial contributions as an alternative way to receive indulgences. By the mid-fifteenth century, the practice had become thoroughly commercialized, and once the printing press arrived, indulgences were mass-produced.

The sale of indulgences peaked in the early sixteenth century, during the construction of the new St. Peter’s Basilica in Rome. The aggressiveness of Friar Tetzel’s campaign at this time, combined with the fact that only half the proceeds went to Rome while the other half serviced Archbishop Albert’s debts, provoked a backlash the Church could not contain, and led to the Reformation.

The Church responded at the Council of Trent in 1563 when it condemned “all evil gains” associated with indulgences. Four years later, in 1567, Pope Pius V issued a formal decree banning the sale of indulgences[^11]. Today, indulgences remain a part of Catholic practice, but they are obtained through prayer, pilgrimage, or charitable works rather than monetary contributions.

#### **Lessons from the Indulgences Case**

The most striking thing about the sale of indulgences by the Catholic Church is how chock-full of modern pricing practices this case is. On the supply side, there is the Treasury of Merit, with its belief-based origins, infinite distribution, and near-zero marginal cost. It might as well be today’s NFTs. On the demand side, the value is generated by the powerful human motivations of fear and pain avoidance, and amplified by faith. There is also a story of value built on the fear of an alternative too painful to contemplate, and the anchoring of price at whatever the buyer can afford to pay. Then there is income-graduated tiered pricing by decree, dressed up in the language of fairness. There is also a multi-tier GTM strategy with commission-based salespeople, an external financier, and branded marketing collateral, along with the printing press's scaling technology, which turns artisan production into mass production and changes the unit economics overnight. There is even a [seller-supplied reference price](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/pricing-case-study-the-power-of-seller?), anchoring buyers at a fraction of their annual income, nearly five centuries before De Beers told a generation of Americans that an engagement ring should cost two months’ salary.

The indulgences case from the Middle Ages also gives us some good insights into the limits of pricing strategy. When a product is intangible, when its benefits are non-verifiable, and when its seller is the only authority on whether it works at all, the value that can be extracted is considerable, at least for a while (again, think NFTs). The market participants’ trust fuels the success of the pricing strategy. But once the underlying narrative came under sustained challenge, the legitimacy of the price structure that had worked so well for centuries crumbled within a few years.

The lesson is worth taking seriously. Pricing power that depends on a single, widely accepted story is more fragile than it looks, and a credible challenge or refutation can erode it quickly. Many modern brands, especially in intangibles, have been built on stories of varying robustness, from premium subscriptions and luxury brands to certain parts of the crypto industry. The textile merchant in 1517 Mainz, trading off 3 florins in his pocket against unimaginable suffering in Purgatory, was performing a belief-based valuation very similar to someone buying an \$8,000 diamond engagement ring for a partner or indulging in the purchase of a \$65,000 luxury handbag.
