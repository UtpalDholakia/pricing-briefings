### Brief Case Synopsis

Maya Okafor, the founder and CEO of Predicate AI, must decide on her launch pricing strategy for her startup’s main product, a vertical AI tool for plaintiff-side litigation firms, by the end of March 2026. After four months of closed beta testing with eleven law firms, she has a product that demonstrably works and provides value to customers, three firms already prepared to convert to paying customers, a board meeting in ten days, and seven months of cash runway. She has done meticulous work on customer value (preparing three estimates), isolating variable and fixed costs, and determining competitor pricing as best she could, but the data on these issues is incomplete, leading to some inconsistent conclusions.

Compounding the difficulty, her two principal investors hold opposing views on what launch pricing approach to use. Daniel Reyes (Northpath) wants to use a penetrating approach using low-price, high-volume entry, citing a contract-review tool that died at \$50K per year per firm because the sales cycle stretched beyond the runway. Linda Park (Verity) favors price skimming with a premium pricing entry, citing an AI marketing tool that failed at \$99 per month because gross margins could not support the significant AI inference variable cost increases, and existing customers refused price increases. Okafor must decide on (1) price level, (2) price structure, (3) launch staging, (4) investor alignment strategy, and (5) what residual uncertainty she can feasibly reduce in the ten days before the meeting.

### Teaching Objectives

By the end of the discussion, students should be able to:

- Assess economic customer value or willingness-to-pay using multiple value-estimation methods (time savings, labor displacement, outcome lift, declared WTP) and think through the credibility of each one and how to use them together;

- Distinguish price level and price structure as separate components of pricing strategy, and work out their interactions;

- Apply unit economics (contribution margin, break-even, gross margin sensitivity) to AI-native businesses, where variable cost scales with usage in ways that are starkly different from pure SaaS startups;

- Evaluate the strategic trade-offs of different pricing structures (per-seat, per-use, hybrid two-part, flat firm-wide) under heterogeneous customer usage;

- Assess different launch pricing strategies for an AI-native startup (free trial, paid pilot, founding-customer pricing), considering the constraints of each option;

- Consider the “soft skill” issue of persuading powerful investors who have substantive disagreements between themselves.

### Suggested Course Positioning and Audience

The case is best suited for MBA or Executive Education courses in pricing strategy (especially in modules on pricing for new ventures and pricing for AI-native businesses), entrepreneurship (early-stage commercialization decisions), vertical SaaS companies considering their AI strategy, and marketing strategy for new products.

This case presents two significant sets of issues for the student to consider. The first concerns establishing the price level using the decision inputs of customer value, costs, and competitor pricing. The second set of issues involves thinking through the price structure and communicating one’s choices to investors. The case is best discussed in a 1.5-hour session, although it can be shortened to 75 minutes if some of the math calculations are assigned to be completed before the session.

### Suggested Discussion Questions

Students should arrive having considered the following questions. The instructor may ask for a case report that addresses some or all of these questions prior to class.

1.  What is Predicate AI worth to an average plaintiff firm per year? Show step-by-step calculations using at least two of Okafor’s three value estimates.

2.  At a \$1,000 price per deposition prep, how many paying firms does Predicate AI need to sign on to break even to cover its fixed costs of approximately \$130,000 per month?

3.  What price level (say, revenue earned per firm) and price structure do you recommend? Please be sure to explain the logic behind your recommendation.

4.  Which of the two investors’ pieces of advice should Okafor weigh more heavily in her decision making? Why?

5.  What can Okafor do in the next ten days to reduce her uncertainty and gain more confidence in her pricing decision?

### Detailed Analysis of the Five Decision Questions

#### Customer Value

In this case, Okafor generates three quantitative estimates of customer economic value and one qualitative assessment of customer WTP. Each of the three measures a different aspect of customer value, and they combine in ways that involve nuance and require thoughtful consideration. They are not substitutes! The instructor should make this point explicit during the discussion.

##### Method 1 — Time savings (\$4,350 per prep)

Economic value calculation: (21 hours − 6 hours) × \$290/hour = \$4,350. This is the most clearly observed (by Okafor with a time-and-motion study) and therefore the most defensible economic value. Also, the blended rate of \$290 is a fully loaded internal cost rate, not a billing rate, so this is the actual cost that Predicate AI displaces for its customer.

However, there are two limitations to note. The \$290 blended rate assumes the saved hours are a combination of partner and paralegal time. However, if the firm primarily reassigns paralegals to other work and keeps partners on existing tasks after shifting to Predicate AI, the realized savings will be significantly lower than this estimate. The second issue is that saved time only translates into savings for the firm if employees use it for other revenue-generating activities. If the saved time is used for activities that don’t generate revenue, these estimates are overestimates of economic value.

##### Method 2 — Labor displacement (\$2,350 per deposition prep)

Economic value calculation: The junior associate’s cost to produce comparable output: 14-18 hours × \$130-\$160/hour = \$1,820-\$2,880; The midpoint of this range is \$2,350.

This is analogous to a “make vs. buy” benchmark calculation using the cheapest plausible substitute. Its weakness is exactly what Okafor identifies in the case, which is that, in practice, it is partner time, not junior associate time, that Predicate AI displaces, so this calculated economic value is the lower bound on the cost savings. The usefulness of this approach lies in the fact that it is the number the customer will instinctively use to push back, so it is what Okafor’s price must beat.

With a strictly value-based pricing approach and focusing only on cost savings, Okafor would land on a price quote somewhere between \$2,350 and \$4,350. But regardless of which of these two methods is selected, Method 3, which focuses on incremental revenue generation, is additive.

##### Method 3 — Case-outcome lift (\$1,416 per deposition prep in expected partner profit)

This estimate focuses on incremental revenue earned by using Predicate AI, driven by enhanced deposition quality. It involves the greatest uncertainty and is likely to be the hardest sell to a potential customer. The calculations are as follows:

> Average settlement value = \$340,000.
>
> Conservative value lift per consequential deposition from using Predicate AI = 10% × \$340,000 = \$34,000.
>
> Hit rate (consequential depos / total depos) = 1/8 (case states 5-6 of 40 depositions; this is at the low end).
>
> Expected case revenue lift per deposition = \$34,000 × (1/8) = \$4,250.
>
> Contingency capture by firm at one-third = \$4,250 × 33.3% ≈ \$1,416.

As noted, this estimate is additive to the first two methods, and most students should grasp this. Predicate AI benefits on both sides of the transaction, by saving employee time, and producing better quality depo support materials that contribute to winning larger settlements. Despite Okafor’s objection that no managing partner will admit in writing that settlement values depend on a vendor, the point is that, admission or not, this economic value is generated by the AI tool. A key challenge is to convince the managing partner or decision maker at her customer firm to acknowledge this value and pay for it.

##### The WTP clusters (\$400 and \$900 per attorney per month or the \$200-\$4,000 range per depo prep)

Okafor is right to treat the per-attorney number as most likely anchored to Westlaw’s subscription price and, therefore, a questionable signal about the customer’s real willingness to pay for her service (since there isn’t much of an overlap between Westlaw and Predicate AI). In contrast, the per-depo prep range is so wide precisely because it is based on an incomplete understanding of the product’s value and driven entirely by an irrelevant anchor. Thus, this wider range is quite informative, and this should be explicitly brought out in the class discussion. It reveals the market’s openness to higher prices than might seem reasonable at first. For instance, the \$4,000 ceiling shows that the most engaged attorneys seem to see substantial value in Predicate AI, while the \$200 floor shows that some attorneys will mentally value automated depo prep work at 1 billing hour, likely a mental shortcut. Okafor should be careful not to give too much weight to the numbers in this section, and students should realize the noisiness and potential to misdirect the decision here.

[Embedded content: datawrapper.dwcdn.net](https://datawrapper.dwcdn.net/R3LRA/1/)

Another lesson that can be drawn here (which applies to entrepreneurs/ startup founders and managers in established businesses alike) is that in the value-based pricing space, it is virtually impossible for the business to capture all the economic value generated for a customer. A reasonable pricing rule of thumb is to capture 15-25% of total value in early-stage SaaS launches, and perhaps a little more if you are an established business selling to your entrenched customers. Within this range, greater value can be captured when it is clearly visible and demonstrable to the customer, and less when it is opaque or harder to quantify and demonstrate easily. For an AI tool whose primary visible value is time savings and whose case-outcome lift value is opaque and subject to contestation, a 15-20% capture suggests a per-deposition price in the \$565-\$1,153 range. We will return to this when we work through the price level and price structure decisions and interactions later in the case discussion.

#### Cost Structure and Unit Economics

##### Variable Costs per Depo Prep

Currently, the variable cost total amounts to \$159 per depo prep, consisting of \$74 in AI inference + \$85 in paralegal review. AI inference costs have wide dispersion (\$35-\$220), driven by case complexity, and are also likely to be affected by future market forces. The case tells us that paralegal review costs may drop to approximately \$30 per depo prep at some point in the future as the company’s AI model improves and human review of the output becomes selective rather than full.

##### Should paralegal costs be considered as fixed or variable?

This is a non-trivial question that will significantly affect the pricing decision. The case suggests that Okafor is confused about this, but we need to land on one side in the class discussion. The correct answer is that this cost could be either one, depending on the time horizon:

- In the short run, up to and past the launch stage (one paralegal with spare capacity, doing fewer than 100 depo preps per month), this cost is fixed. The paralegal is paid \$8,500/month regardless of whether she reviews 5 depo reps, 35 depo reps, or 100 depo reps. Treating this cost as variable during this stage will overstate the marginal cost of a depo rep and understate the contribution margin, leading to a launch price that’s higher than necessary. This is certainly not a good idea for Okafor at the moment.

- In the long run, once the company goes beyond selling 100 depo preps a month, but still has to do the full human review, it will be a step-fixed cost. Then each increase in 100 depo preps will require hiring another paralegal at \$8,500 per month. Another reasonable possibility for Okafor to consider is to outsource this work as she scales up to shed fixed costs so that the work is paid for at approximately the \$85 per depo prep rate. In this case, the variable cost classification will be correct, but it will mean that a higher price will be needed to cover this cost and maintain the same contribution margin.

From the perspective of analyzing and discussing the case in class, the instructor should consider both possibilities: a short-run contribution margin that excludes the paralegal cost by considering it as fixed, and a long-run “full review” contribution margin that treats it as variable, revealing that the price recommendation will increase substantially.

##### Fixed Costs

It is given that the total fixed costs amount to \$130,000 per month at the moment, in addition to some increases that will need to be made by the close of the Series A funding. These include:

- Okafor’s salary increases which amounts to approximately +\$14,000/month (midpoint of the \$12K-\$16K range);

- Additional paralegal reviewers as scale grows: +\$8,500 per month each (one extra reviewer is needed when monthly preps exceed 100); consequently, a 50-firm base at 3.2 preps per month already needs one. Also see the discussion in the previous section on variable costs.

- By the end of 2026, when Predicate AI has established a base of 50 paying customers, the fixed costs will be approximately \$152,500-\$165,000/month.

##### Per depo-prep breakeven pricing and sensitivity

These calculations assume a fixed cost rate of \$130,000 per month and \$159 per depo prep.

[Embedded content: datawrapper.dwcdn.net](https://datawrapper.dwcdn.net/0mCrO/1/)

If and when the paralegal review cost falls to \$30 (leading to a drop in variable cost per dep rep to \$104), contribution at \$1,000/prep will rise to \$896, and the break-even will go down to about 145 preps per month or an installed base of 45 firms.

##### Per-seat pricing breakeven & sensitivity

A 10-attorney firm at \$400 per attorney per month generates \$4,000. The variable cost at average usage (3.2 preps × \$159) = \$509/month. Contribution = \$3,491 per month per firm, leading to a breakeven of 37 firms (\$ 130,000/\$3,491).

But this value is highly sensitive to usage levels. If some customers take advantage of the All-You-Can-Eat pricing for whatever reason, and the firm does 20 depo preps a month, the variable cost will balloon to \$3,180, while revenue stays the same at \$4,000. Contribution falls to \$820, and breakeven rises to 159 firms (\$130,000/\$820), which appears out of reach for Okafor. Pure-seat pricing does not seem like a good idea at all.

#### **Price Level**

The analysis above suggests a per-depo prep price in the \$750-\$1,200 range and a per-seat price of at least \$400 per attorney per month. But we also need to consider the price structure. Higher per-depo prep prices are easier to explain when each charge maps to a discrete, valuable output. On the other hand, lower per-seat prices make it easier to close the sale. Because the choices are coupled, level and structure must be resolved jointly (see next section).

Consider the price level alone, the parameters are as follows:

##### **Price Floor**

This is the price that must cover both fixed and variable costs in the longer run. At 3.2 preps per firm per month, an average firm must produce more than \$1,000/month in gross profit to amortize a reasonable share of fixed cost across a 50-firm installed base. That implies a minimum revenue of \$1,500-\$1,800 per month from each firm, or \$18,000-\$22,000 annually.

##### **Price Ceiling**

The ceiling is what the customer is willing to pay and is driven by the customer’s perception of fairness relative to the labor-displacement floor of \$2,350 per depo prep established earlier. Anything approaching that number per depo prep will lead to the conclusion that “it’s cheaper to maintain the status quo.” Note that the status quo is likely to be preferred anyway, so this might actually be a theoretical ceiling, with the actual ceiling somewhat lower (3.2x12x\$2,350x50% = \$45,120).

##### **Launch price as long-term anchor**

As mentioned in the case, the price Okafor chooses to launch, regardless of transparency, will be the number plaintiff lawyers associate with Predicate AI for a long time in the future, given the industry's social dynamics. This provides a strong argument for using at least some form of price skimming and setting a much higher starting price rather than lowballing the list price at launch to sign up a lot of lawyers quickly.

##### **Recommended price level**

Thus, we end up with a price that is significantly higher than the floor, something like \$35,000-\$45,000 per average customer per year (note that this is for a 10-attorney firm at approx. 38 preps/year). This is high enough to support the unit economics, low enough to be a sale to a managing partner with a quick turnaround, and fits within the price range of \$20,000 - \$45,000.

##### Gross Margin for this price structure will work out as follows:

> Per-prep margin at launch: \$500 − \$159 = \$341 contribution, 68% margin.
>
> Per-prep margin once paralegal review automates: \$500 − \$104 = \$396, 79% margin.
>
> Per-prep worst case (complex matter, \$220 AI inference + \$85 review): \$500 − \$305 = \$195, 39% margin. Crossing this hurdle is very helpful, variable costs are covered in the most expensive situation.
>
> Per-seat revenue: essentially 100% incremental margin (the platform has been built; an additional seat costs nothing). This is like Costco’s membership fee, it can be used to pay the fixed costs.

##### When Okafor has an installed base of 50 firms (hopefully by the end of 2026), the math will work out like this:

> Revenue: 50 firms × \$3,600/month = \$180,000/month = \$2.16M ARR.
>
> AI inference variable cost: 50 × 3.2 × \$74 = \$11,840/month.
>
> Fixed cost (steady state, including +\$14K founder salary and one additional paralegal): \$152,500/month.
>
> Total cost: approximately \$164,340/month.
>
> EBITDA: approximately +\$15,660/month, or +\$188K/year. Predicate AI clears its operating cost base at 50 firms with a modest cushion. Customers beyond that become highly profitable.

Note that this scenario understates leverage in the medium term. As paralegal review is automated and the per-prep variable cost declines toward \$104, EBITDA at 50 firms will rise to approximately \$44,000 per month (\$540,000 annually) without any price increases or changes to the price structure.

#### Starup Launch Pricing Strategy

##### Free trial vs. paid pilot

The two investors are at loggerheads over launch pricing. For this issue, Daniel favors a 30-day free trial, whereas Linda supports a \$7,500 paid pilot. Neither one may provide the perfect solution. A free month may not work because depositions arrive at unpredictable, choppy rates; what if no depositions are taken during the trial? A \$7,500 paid pilot asks for money without the customer knowing what they are getting, and puts the cart before the horse.

A sensible, pragmatic approach is to offer something like “first deposition free” or just one deposition to try things out. This will cost Predicate AI \$159 in variable costs but will give the customer a clear understanding of the economic value they stand to gain. Note that some handholding may be required to ensure the customer receives high-quality depo prep support.

##### Founding customer pricing

A common pricing strategy used in the SaaS space (not to mention other service-based offerings) is to offer a locked-in low price to the first adopters. The duration of the offer can vary; in extreme cases, a permanent low price might be offered. More commonly, a low price may be offered for the first year, two years, etc. Here, a time-limited founding customer rate is ok and may offer some benefits, but a lengthy term, and certainly a permanent low rate, is a bad idea. For example, 20% off the per-depo fee for the first year or the first 2 years for the first 10 or 15 customers might be an attractive offer (note that the per-seat fee is not discounted here).

##### Annual vs. monthly contracts

This is where Predicate AI can borrow from the SaaS playbook: make the annual contract the default and charge a higher rate (e.g., 25%) for monthly contracts. In this case, the monthly rates would be \$250 per attorney per month and \$625 per prep. Such a price structure would quickly drive customers to the annual rate, and the monthly rate may even serve as an extended paid trial for those who are a bit uncomfortable committing.

##### Price disclosure transparency

A key issue that is often underappreciated is transparency. Should the prices be publicly available and published online, or should they be quoted individually to customers? Normally, when the price is similar across all customers as our recommendation suggests, the tendency is to go with transparency. However, given the startup setting, the new market, and the potential for competitors to crop up quickly in this space, there are upsides to opacity. Why share price information with potential competitors and make their life easy? Second, the two-part pricing and even the price tags on each part could turn some potential customers off unless they are communicated with. I always advocate for contextualizing the price as part of effective price structure realization, and this is one case where talking to a potential client about pricing, at least initially, will pay off. A prominent “request pricing” form with a quick call back from Okafor will do the trick. It may make sense to move to an online “starting-from price” price (e.g., “plans start at \$2,000/month”) only after she has 25+ paying firms and the price has stabilized.

#### Managing Investor Relationships

This is the soft-skills part of the case, essential to pricing strategy for startup founders and entrepreneurs. While managers of established businesses don’t have to deal with this issue, it is crucial to address it here. The case shows that Daniel and Linda hold very different views on launch pricing, and at first glance, it appears Okafor will have to choose one over the other, essentially alienating one of the two power hitters. But upon closer reading, there is some validity to both positions, and Okafor’s skill lies in threading the needle and reaching for a compromise. This is an important point for the instructor to make and a powerful lesson from the case, because students’ natural tendency is to pick one of the two options.

A good insight is that both failures narrated in the investors’ stories stem from choosing the wrong price structure, leading to either a price that is too high or too low. The hybrid two-part tariff structurally avoids both. The low per-seat base is Daniel’s friction-reducing entry point; the per-prep usage fee is Linda’s variable-cost-protected margin engine.

When the board meets, rather than pitting these two powerhouses against each other, with one emerging the winner and the other the loser, Okafor should present her recommendation as a combined approach drawn from both experiences. What this means is the following:

Okafor’s message to Daniel could be something like, “The \$200 per seat entry is the friction-reducing element you wanted. We can land 50 firms by year-end at this entry point because no procurement is triggered — a 10-attorney firm pays \$2,000/month base, which is below the discretionary spend authority of a managing partner.” At the same time, she can say to Linda: “The \$500 per prep variable component is what protects gross margin when the burgeoning AI inference costs surprise us. Our contribution per prep will be positive even in the worst-case scenario of \$220 AI inference costs, and our per-seat revenue is essentially 100% margin.”

From both, she can obtain joint agreement and commitment about things like engaging in a quarterly pricing review with a clear set of if-then rules to manage pricing as Predicate AI makes headway (for instance, if customer count exceeds X by Q2, raise per-prep to Y; if not, hold). Investors will disagree less when they are given clear decision rules they can pre-commit to rather than when they are asked to settle a philosophical dispute.

### Reducing Uncertainty

Okafor can reduce uncertainty during the short window of time (approx. 10 days) in the following ways.

1.  Quote the recommended price to three firms in the beta-test who have been asking for pricing. This provides an instant price test because the customer knows the product, has assessed its value, and is well-positioned to gauge the price. The offer could be positioned as “founding-customer pricing,” with an assurance of no price increases for 24 months (or even 30 months, push comes to shove). Okafor will get useful information on how well this price offer is received and will have useful data either way for the board meeting. What’s more, she also has room to maneuver, i.e., change her mind, if the response is very negative.

2.  Another idea is to approach a small group of new firms that she hasn’t included in her beta-test with the recommended pricing strategy to receive another independent signal from more realistic prospects, with a poorer understanding of the product’s economic value and connection with the firm. Positive responses here will be another valuable piece of information.

3.  This is the hardest (and perhaps infeasible), but Okafor should put in some effort to work out the trajectory of the AI inference costs. The huge range of \$35 to \$220 in AI inference costs is the largest unmodeled risk in her cost analysis. It would be very useful to get a sense of measurable depo prep features like page count, witness type, and number of exhibits that impact AI inference costs. This may lead to further gradations in pricing (not just per depo-prep, but several categories of depo prep cases ranging in complexity, for example), or at least provide her with a better idea of the robustness of her margin prediction.

4.  Confirm the ethics rules with a 30-minute call to a state-bar ethics specialist. Outcome-based pricing was ruled out on a single conversation; success-fee structures invoiced as flat fees may be permissible in some jurisdictions, and even partial reopening would change strategy options later.

5.  Get a verbal pre-read from Daniel and Linda separately on the hybrid synthesis before the board meeting. Surprises in board meetings are how good ideas get killed for political reasons. A pre-aligned room turns the board meeting into ratification rather than debate.

What she should not do: run a survey of attorney WTP. She has correctly identified that this will not yield credible numbers, and ten days is not enough to design and run a discrete-choice study that would.

### The Instructor’s Recommended Position

A defensible pricing strategy recommendation is as follows:

Launch at a hybrid two-part tariff: \$200 per attorney per month plus \$500 per deposition prep, sold on annual contracts (monthly available at a 25% higher price), with one free prep as a trial (without any time constraints), a 20%-off-per-prep founding-customer rate locked in for 24 months for the first ten firms, and pricing kept opaque at launch. The narrative to the board is that this price structure combines Daniel’s and Linda’s recommendations judiciously without rejecting either. Both have skin in the game and a say in Predicate AI’s pricing. To summarize the points covered earlier in this note, this is the main reasoning behind the recommendation.

- The per-seat base is a low-procurement, easy-yes entry point that supports volume (supports Daniel’s ideas).

- The per-depo prep fee is a usage-aligned margin protector against AI-inference cost shocks, as it moves with variable prices (which supports Linda’s ideas).

- Total Annual Recurring Revenue from an average customer will be approximately. \$43,000, and is reasonable given the value-based pricing work done by Okafar. It is about 17-20% of the economic customer value generated by Predicate AI, well above the cost floor and well below the customer fairness ceiling.

- With this price structure, Okafor has a good chance of achieving breakeven with a customer base of approximately 50 firms by the end of the year. This is neither a “go for 200 firms” plan nor a “go for 15 elephants” plan, both of which are too extreme and fraught with aforementioned risks. This is a balanced 50-firm plan.

- Another really useful feature of this price structure is that customers self-segment themselves. Firms will spend (and derive value) based on their size and their aggregate needs for high-quality deposition preparation.

Furthermore, the instructor should be candid that while this is one defensible answer, other defensible answers are also possible and could co-exist (for example, a \$1,200 per-depo prep pure-usage price quote for firms that want simplicity, or a richer three-tier seat structure with usage caps).

### Two Alternative Recommendations Worth Considering

If the discussion takes a different direction, two other positions can be defended on the basis of the case facts. These positions are more aligned with one of the investor’s views in the case, and are briefly considered next.

#### Alternative A: Daniel’s volume play — \$300/attorney/month flat

This is an aggressive acquisition approach that could be used if there is an impetus to quickly build a large installed base, and it implicitly assumes that Predicate AI can raise prices later as customers become locked in. The economics work at 50+ firms only if average usage stays moderate (the case’s heavy-usage example collapses margins). The risk flagged by Linda that the \$300 price tag will be a permanent anchor and weigh down future price increases is real and historically common in SaaS businesses, and is also being seen in fledgling AI businesses. This is a reasonable bet only if Okafor truly believes she can later transition to a different model with existing customers, which the case suggests she cannot.

#### Alternative B: Linda’s premium positioning — \$1,500/prep, no per-seat

The second option is to lean into Linda’s ideas, maximize value capture per use, simplify the contract (to one number), and signal quality through price. In this case, the breakeven will be at a much lower 30 firms. The risks are obvious. First, the price will likely mean longer sales conversations and greater resistance from the maverick managing partners, making it hard given the tight runway. Second, law firms with low deposition volume will reject this price right away, leading to a smaller pool to sell to, and third, the customer will be highly sensitive to usage, and may use Predicate AI only for intense, complex (high variable cost) cases This is a reasonable bet only if Okafor is targeting the top quartile of plaintiff firms (with respect to size and usage) and willing to accept a smaller customer base in exchange for higher revenue per customer.

### Optional Extensions

For instructors who want to extend the case for a second session or beyond a 90-minute session into a longer session, here are some possibilities:

1.  Build a 24-month financial model under the recommended pricing approach that accounts for different customer acquisition rates (e.g., 5, 8, and 12 per month) and average usage (e.g., 2, 4, and 7 preps per firm per month). This can allow questions such as when Predicate AI becomes cash-flow positive and when it needs to raise its Series B round to be discussed and considered.

2.  Role-playing will work very well, especially if the audience is entrepreneur- or startup-founder-heavy, rather than experienced pricing managers trained in price negotiations. Here, the instructor could set up an exercise involving Okafor’s negotiation with one of the three firms from the beta-test that are ready to buy. The firm requests a 40% discount with the price locked in for three years. Where does Okafor hold the line? How much leeway is she willing to give? This can lead us toward price negotiation skills for the founder, which would be another module to add to this case.

3.  Design the Series A pitch around the recommended pricing strategy. How does the pricing strategy appear in the deck, and what investor objections does each slide anticipate and aim to address?

4.  Stress-test the recommendation with an exogenous shock. Suppose a frontier-model API provider raises inference prices by 75% six weeks after launch, with the possibility of further substantial increases incoming. How should Okafor respond under each pricing structure? Which structures could absorb the shock without contract renegotiation?
