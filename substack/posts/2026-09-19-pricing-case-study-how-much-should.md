##### *Summary*. Gilead’s lenacapavir illustrates how a tiered pricing strategy can have plausible rationales for the high and low price tiers, yet still struggles to provide a coherent explanation for the structure as a whole. How much should Brazil pay for a drug that seems to have a reasonable generics price of \$40 per patient per year, and when the seller has refused an offer of \$400, or ten times as much? This case examines why credible boundaries between price tiers, the use of a single pricing method across tiers, and greater price transparency are essential to making tiered pricing work, and why customer value is often hard to apply in practice to pricing decisions.

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/8f35fff4-98a0-44bb-a8b5-279fe4b1a571_1200x630.png)

*“For the moment, countries are paying approximately \$40 per person per year for oral PrEP. Anything that diverges drastically from that level of price will really impose a big stress on budget lines on HIV.” – [Carmen Pérez Casas, Unitaid](https://www.npr.org/sections/goats-and-soda/2025/06/18/g-s1-73130/hiv-prevention-drug-lenacapavir-fda-breakthrough).*

### **Introduction**

Ever since the late 1990s, the issue of pricing HIV drugs has been at the forefront of ethical pricing dilemmas. In a [2002 case study](https://hbsp.harvard.edu/product/502061-PDF-ENG), Professor Gourville described how 85% of HIV cases were in Africa, Latin America, and Asia at the time, and received no treatment at all to alleviate their suffering. There was no way they could afford the prohibitive prices of the highly effective AIDS drugs sold by Merck, GSK, and Bristol-Myers Squibb at the time, costing thousands of dollars per patient per year. 25 years on, this issue still remains unresolved. The basic issue is that the same drugs are vitally important to consumers in two completely different markets, those where well-funded insurers and governments can afford to pay [tens of thousands of dollars](https://www.idsociety.org/news--publications-new/articles/2025/hiv-preventive-treatment-could-be-sold-for-one-thousandth-of-current-list-price) for patient treatment, and others where [\$100 for the same drug](https://www.forbesafrica.com/current-affairs/2026/03/13/a-more-discreet-and-convenient-option-eight-african-countries-receive-40-hiv-prevention-injection-as-rollout-set-to-expand) is too expensive. How should a company selling a clearly valuable, indeed life-saving, product price it so that all its potential customers can benefit from it, even when they have drastically different abilities to pay?

When compared to 2002, there are now [many more antiretroviral drugs](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4309625/) tackling HIV and AIDS, from those suppressing HIV and enabling people with the infection to live longer, healthier lives, to those reducing the risk of HIV infection among people who don’t have it. These people need to keep taking these drugs consistently over long periods of time to benefit from them. The core pricing conundrum is that there’s a huge misalignment between the people who would benefit most from these drugs and their ability (not their willingness, mind you) to pay for them. As Gourville and others before him have pointed out, the fact that the companies making these drugs are highly profitable and based in Western countries further complicates the dilemma.

A pricing strategy solution to this conundrum, commonly used in the pharmaceutical industry and elsewhere, is [tiered pricing](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4463402/). The drug company sorts its customers into geographic segments, charges different prices to each segment, and builds legal and commercial fences to prevent customers from receiving the low price from reselling the drug to those paying the high price. But what seems simple and straightforward in theory can get very tricky in practice. The questions about how many tiers to use, which countries go into which tiers, and what the prices for each of these tiers should be become important when saving (or not saving) millions of lives is at stake.

In this post, I want to consider the interesting challenge faced by US pharmaceutical company [Gilead Sciences for lenacapavir](https://www.gilead.com/news/news-details/2025/yeztugo-lenacapavir-is-now-the-first-and-only-fda-approved-hiv-prevention-option-offering-6-months-of-protection), its highly effective twice-yearly injection for preventing HIV infection. On the surface, Gilead’s price structure for lenacapavir seems perfect: it is differentiated, with different margins earned from different products, buyers, payers, and countries, and a margin portfolio that makes sense given that this is a cutting-edge AIDS drug lifting the industry’s heavy baggage of previous botched pricing. Despite offering lenacapavir at cost to 120 of the world’s poorest countries, Gilead still faces a storm of discontent from excluded countries, specifically Brazil, and the unanswered question of what its price should be. The fact that it seems to be doing all the right things for Africa and South Asia doesn’t make the pricing challenge any easier for Brazil and other excluded countries.

### **The Facts Behind Gilead’s Pricing Dilemma**

#### ***The company and the drug***

Gilead Sciences is a leading seller of HIV drugs for prevention (known as pre-exposure prophylaxis or PrEP), such as Descovy, and treatment, such as Biktarvy, with revenue from its HIV portfolio reaching [\$20.8 billion in 2025](https://investors.gilead.com/news/news-details/2026/Gilead-Sciences-Announces-Fourth-Quarter-and-Full-Year-2025-Financial-Results/default.aspx). Relevant to the present case, the company has had mixed success with tiered pricing. For example, in 2014, Gilead priced its Hepatitis C drug Sovaldi at \$84,000 for a 12-week course in the United States, \$900 in Egypt and India, and licensed generic makers for sales in 91 countries. However, it left Malaysia out of this pricing framework. After extensive price negotiations that went nowhere, the Malaysian government decided to [issue a compulsory license](https://www.citizen.org/news/compulsory-licensing-for-hepatitis-c-medication-in-malaysia/) for [government use in 2017](https://www.mac.org.my/v3/malaysian-aids-council-welcomes-government-move-to-issue-compulsory-license-on-lifesaving-hepatitis-c-medicines/) to ensure that the 400,000 Malaysians with Hepatitis C would get affordable treatment.

Lenacapavir is a highly effective HIV prevention drug that the World Health Organization has [compared to a vaccine](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-07-2025-who-recommends-injectable-lenacapavir-for-hiv-prevention). In a [trial run in South Africa and Uganda](https://www.gilead.com/news/news-details/2024/full-efficacy-and-safety-results-for-gilead-investigational-twice-yearly-lenacapavir-for-hiv-prevention-presented-at-aids-2024), not a single participant who received the drug was infected with HIV, and another trial across seven countries [resulted in a 96% reduction](https://www.gilead.com/news/news-details/2024/gileads-twice-yearly-lenacapavir-for-hiv-prevention-reduced-hiv-infections-by-96-and-demonstrated-superiority-to-daily-truvada-in-second-pivotal-phase-3-trial) in HIV infection. Although it isn’t a vaccine, the protection that lenacapavir offers through a dose every six months is a significant improvement for people who struggle to take a daily pill.

#### ***Lenacapavir’s pricing strategy***

Consistent with its pricing strategy for other drugs, Gilead adopted a tiered pricing approach for lenacapavir from the outset. It launched in the US under the brand name [Yeztugo](https://www.yeztugo.com/) at a list price of \$28,218 per person per year (i.e., \$14,109 per injection). For context, in the United States, other comparable HIV PrEP drugs are priced at \$26,400 for [Descovy](https://www.goodrx.com/descovy?srsltid=AfmBOootoma8aoQAbrASSHOXkcMRfoBX-pTKDApF9TlIPAI9YKxIzc6V) (an oral pill to be taken daily), approximately \$22,500 for [Apretude](https://viivuspricing.com/apretude.html) (injections taken every two months), and \$350 for generic Truvada (daily oral pill), suggesting that the price was very much based on current reference prices, i.e., slightly higher than alternatives, given this is the longest-lasting option requiring only two doctor visits a year. Note that these are list prices; insurers may pay lower prices based on their arrangements with Gilead. This is lenacapavir’s high price tier.

The situation regarding the low-price tier is interesting and encouraging. Even before it was approved by the FDA, in October 2024, Gilead signed royalty-free licenses with six generic manufacturers in India, Egypt, and Pakistan to enable access to the treatment in 120 “high-incidence, resource-limited” countries. Part of the arrangement was to supply its branded product to these countries [at cost](https://www.managedhealthcareexecutive.com/view/ias-president-calls-for-price-transparency-in-gilead-global-fund-lenacapavir-agreement) until the generic manufacturers could ramp up production to meet demand. After the generic manufacturers begin making generic lenacapavir, the price is expected to be around [\$40 per patient per year](https://www.gatesfoundation.org/ideas/media-center/press-releases/2025/09/hiv-prevention-lenacapavir).

[Embedded content: www.youtube-nocookie.com](https://www.youtube-nocookie.com/embed/9fAkyTC1sa8?rel=0&autoplay=0&showinfo=0&enablejsapi=0)

“At cost” does not mean that Gilead has been transparent about how much the research and innovation activities to get lenacapavir to market cost the company, or how much it costs to manufacture lenacapavir. Cost estimates come from various sources, and include [\$60 from a budget line](https://bhekisisa.org/health-news-south-africa/2025-07-15-sa-gets-r520-million-to-buy-the-twice-a-year-anti-hiv-jab-but-theres-a-snag/) in a Global Fund grant letter for South Africa, [\$64 from an estimate](https://www.eatg.org/hiv-news/idweek-2025-reduced-pricing-for-lenacapavir-is-possible-and-profitable/) in a study by Andrew Hill and colleagues, and [\$100 from the Health Justice Initiative](https://healthjusticeinitiative.org.za/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/NO-EXCUSES-FAQ-2_August-25.pdf). These cost estimates indicate that US customers are paying a markup of several hundred times for partaking of lenacapavir.

In July 2025, Gilead [finalized a supply agreement](https://www.gilead.com/news/news-details/2025/gilead-finalizes-agreement-with-the-global-fund-to-accelerate-access-to-twice-yearly-lenacapavir-for-hiv-prevention-for-up-to-two-million-people-in-primarily-low--and-lower-middle-income-countries) with the Global Fund for up to 2 million people over three years, which was later expanded through its commitment with the [Global Fund and PEPFAR](https://www.gilead.com/news/news-details/2026/pepfar-and-the-global-fund-make-additional-investment-in-gileads-lenacapavir-for-hiv-prevention-to-further-expand-access) to 3 million people. The price Gilead charges the Global Fund has never been disclosed and, to my knowledge, remains confidential as of this writing. As part of this agreement, the first doses of lenacapavir were given to [people in Eswatini and Zambia in November 2025](https://www.gilead.com/news/news-details/2025/gilead-joins-partners-for-delivery-of-first-shipments-of-breakthrough-twice-yearly-lenacapavir-for-hiv-prevention-to-sub-saharan-africa), leading Gilead’s CEO, Daniel O’Day, to boast that “a new HIV medicine is reaching communities in sub-Saharan Africa in the same years as its U.S. approval.” It seemed that the disparities identified by Gourville and other writers from the early 2000s were really a thing of the past. Not so fast!

One essential aspect of a successful geography-based tiered pricing strategy is having high, impermeable fences between the tiers. Those who have to pay the high price should not be able to readily obtain the product available in the low-priced markets. Gilead’s licenses establish this separation through territorial restrictions that limit the areas that licensees can sell the product. As the [Health Justice Initiative’s analysis](https://healthjusticeinitiative.org.za/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/NO-EXCUSES-FAQ-2_August-25.pdf) explains, these are actual prohibitions. Numerous countries with a significant incidence of HIV are excluded through these restrictions. Among others, they include Brazil, Argentina, Mexico, China, Malaysia, and Turkey, plus most of the Middle East and Eastern Europe, where [41% of new HIV infections occur](https://www.unaids.org/en/resources/presscentre/pressreleaseandstatementarchive/2024/october/20241002_gilead-lenacapavir). These countries fall in neither the low price tier of the 120 countries nor the high price tier that includes the United States (I have excluded Europe from this discussion because the prices paid by Western European countries are not in the public domain; however, we may surmise that they will pay a substantially lower price than the United States and a higher price than the 120 resource-limited countries).

#### ***The case of Brazil***

Brazil is an intriguing exclusion by Gilead, especially given its remarkable proactivity in HIV treatment. For instance, it has been providing free antiretroviral treatment to every citizen who needs it since 1996. At the moment, it supplies oral PrEP drugs to [about 350,000 of its citizens](https://ndmais.com.br/saude/prep-injetavel-chega-ao-sus-em-2026-min-da-saude/). Brazil’s drug regulator, ANVISA, approved lenacapavir for HIV prevention use in January 2026, but Gilead did not have either a launch date or a price at that time for the Brazilian market. In a sign that Brazil really wants its citizens to have the drug, the Ministry of Health took the initiative and, at a meeting in July 2026, offered Gilead R\$2,000 (approximately \$400) per person per year for the drug. While declining this offer, Gilead said that it is discussing pathways to provide access to lenacapavir for Latin America by collaborating with the Pan American Health Organization (PAHO). The public record does not provide details regarding why Gilead rejected a price offer that was [10X the price](https://www.gatesfoundation.org/ideas/media-center/press-releases/2025/09/hiv-prevention-lenacapavir) paid by countries like Indonesia and Thailand, but it is clear that the company engaged in tough price negotiation with Brazilian authorities. The figure below shows all relevant lenacapavir prices available in the public domain, along with a list of unknown prices.

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/939d8ea4-8ea8-4609-86d8-16daaad2827e_1280x720.png)

The range of prices of lenacapavir from public sources, September 2026

As can be seen from the figure, prices cluster at the low end, ranging from \$60 to \$100, reflecting the costs of producing lenacapavir. At a price of \$40, estimates suggest the manufacturer will still earn a healthy profit margin (though those estimates are from activists, so they should be taken with a grain of salt). At the high end, prices cluster around reference prices, between \$22,000 and \$28,000. Even Gilead’s CEO has explicitly acknowledged this pricing for Yeztugo, [saying that his company](https://www.npr.org/sections/goats-and-soda/2025/06/18/g-s1-73130/hiv-prevention-drug-lenacapavir-fda-breakthrough) “made a decision to price \[Yeztugo\] in line with existing PrEP options.” Interestingly, there’s nothing in the vast middle of the figure, from \$400 to \$22,500 (UnitedHealthcare’s reimbursement only partially accounts for the total price paid, since the provider also has to shell out a significant amount).

Depending on your point of view and the reference price you consider relevant from the figure, Brazil’s offer of \$400 per patient per year appears either remarkably low or surprisingly generous. Compared with the price paid by American consumers, Brazil’s offered price is a very small fraction. But relative to the price of generic lenacapavir to be paid by 120 countries, many of which are not too different from Brazil in their economic characteristics, the \$400 price tag is a tenfold premium. The interesting question is what a reasonable price would be for Gilead to offer Brazil, and why.

Using our [Value Pricing Framework](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/pricing-tool-the-value-pricing-framework) logic and the current prices for HIV infection prevention from the figure, two things are clear. The first is that, not surprisingly, Gilead has a strong preference for pricing Yeztugo based on reference prices. This allows it to earn the premium price it believes it deserves, and it can even be argued that the reference price captures customer value, in the sense that insurers and governments view prices in the \$22,500+ range as feasible. The second is that the ability to pay is an important part of the pricing context here. Most of the rest of the world (including uninsured consumers in the US) simply cannot afford to pay Yeztugo’s list price of \$28,218. Regardless of how valuable the product is, or how many HIV infections it prevents, those who benefit from it are not going to be able to use the drug at that price.

Considering Brazil’s case in more detail, treating HIV infection through Brazil’s public medical infrastructure costs the country somewhere between [\$500](https://itpcglobal.org/2016/10/28/brazil-use-dolutegravir-first-line-hiv-treatment/) and [\$1,600](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3142155/) per patient per year (depending on whether the potential transmission of the virus by a patient is taken into account). With simple back-of-the-envelope math, anything below \$500 is a good price for Brazil to prevent the infection, though it could be argued that something like \$800 would still be a good price. A more rigorous analysis concluded that at Brazil’s cost-effectiveness threshold of \$8,740 per year of life saved, providing lenacapavir to at-risk gay and bisexual men and transgender women, the highest-incidence groups, will be cost-effective only if it [costs no more than](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13176634/) about \$740 per person per year. On the other hand, in a speech at a recent conference, the Brazilian health minister said that Gilead had wanted a price that was “10 times higher than being paid by Indonesia and Thailand,” which would amount to between \$600 and \$1,000. Thus, based on the publicly available information as of this writing, a price between \$400 and \$740 would suit both parties.

### **The Challenge of Pricing the Middle Tier**

This case study, which examines Gilead’s challenge in pricing its blockbuster drug lenacapavir in Brazil, offers broadly applicable lessons on implementing tiered pricing strategies effectively. Conceptually, a tiered pricing strategy simply offers products of different quality levels for different prices to different segments. The differences in quality levels don’t have to be real; the demarcation will work as long as the intended customers perceive them to be different. Tiered pricing is a commonly used price structure simply because it [offers many significant benefits](https://www.amazon.com/How-Price-Effectively-Managers-Entrepreneurs-ebook/dp/B073PK2SC5/). However, the lenacapavir case highlights some nuances of the successful implementation of a tiered pricing approach that I want to consider.

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/a3803e1f-1376-4d19-aec1-6dc03f8432a8_886x1134.png)

First, when using tiered pricing, the inherent scheme to communicate (real or perceived) quality has to be credible and communicable. In the lenacapavir case, we run into the issue of legitimacy (or lack thereof) that I’ve written about before [in other contexts](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/authority-legitimacy-and-13th-century?). Consider the figure above, which shows the prices of different lenacapavir variants as of this writing. On the low end, we have a \$40 price tag for the generic version (still controlled by Gilead through licenses), and on the high end, we have \$28,218 for Yeztugo, Gilead’s branded version. Is the name Yeztugo worth 705 times more to anyone? Even a sensible billionaire is likely to answer “no” to this question. The implicit logic that insured Americans should be subjected to a ridiculously high price for the drug because other, slightly inferior options cost about the same doesn’t hold up well, especially when those inferior options have been priced using similarly questionable logic with a chicken-and-egg circularity. In a nutshell, the tiers in this pricing scheme are suspect in their legitimacy with respect to both the price differences between tiers and the fact that the quality differences are minimal, or even non-existent.

Second is the issue of a consistent pricing method carrying through the price tiers. As the figure highlights, the low price tier is mostly based on costs—either selling the product at cost or with a modest markup of costs. At the high end, they are based entirely on reference prices (which in turn are based on some calculation of customer value). Thus, the extreme tiers in this pricing scheme use entirely different, incompatible criteria in establishing prices. Compounding this problem is the fact that the tiers are demarcated with the self-serving and faulty premise that American (and to a lesser extent, Western European) consumers are willing and able to pay 700 times the price paid by the rest of the world. Given the incompatibility between the high and low tiers, it is not at all clear which pricing method or criterion can reasonably be used for the middle tier, or what the middle price should be.

Third, in a tiered pricing structure, the prices of the different tiers need to be clearly available to customers. The pharmaceutical industry is notorious for low price transparency, and in the lenacapavir case, the lack of available prices, especially for the low tier, is a problem. All the prices in my figure are estimates from various third-party sources, many of which have vested interests of their own; Gilead has been silent about its costs and its price to the Global Fund. Either or both of these values would be very useful to Brazil, Mexico, Malaysia, or any of the other excluded countries. Similarly, more than a year after the European Commission approved lenacapavir, Gilead still hasn’t started selling the drug anywhere in Europe, and [in some cases](https://www.ggg.at/2026/01/20/spritze-gegen-hiv-infektion-laesst-in-deutschland-weiter-auf-sich-warten/), hasn’t even begun to negotiate price. Relative to the exorbitant US price, a visible European price would be a more reasonable reference price for upper-middle-income countries like Brazil or Chile.

Nor can the problem be solved simply by invoking customer value. A drug can generate substantial value and have the potential to save many, many lives, and still be unaffordable at the scale at which it needs to be used. This is particularly important for prevention, because a government’s task becomes more nebulous: protecting as many of its citizens as possible, only some of whom might otherwise become infected. It therefore becomes far more difficult to calculate the drug’s economic value. The annual cost of treating one HIV patient cannot be used as a cap on the annual price of protecting each person at risk. For Brazil (and other countries still negotiating with Gilead), the relevant purchasing decision involves a consideration of how many people it can bring under the umbrella of protection on a long-term basis, how to define the criteria for which citizens should be included and which ones excluded, and what things it will not be able to do or stop doing to finance the purchase of this drug. For Gilead, the task goes beyond demonstrating the drug’s efficacy to quoting a price that makes sense for getting a government-funded program up and running sustainably.

What makes this case so insightful is that a company can have a plausible rationale for the extremes of the tiered price structure and still struggle to provide a coherent explanation for the structure as a whole. A tiered pricing structure is effective when customers have different abilities to pay, only when the price tiers are legitimate, the pricing method used is consistent across them, and the demarcations are perceived as credible. It feels like we have come full circle; 25 years after US pharmaceutical companies’ fiasco of pricing AIDS drugs in African countries, a not-rich country that has offered free HIV treatment to every one of its citizens since 1996 still cannot buy a preventive treatment from the American pharmaceutical behemoth that tested it on Brazilian volunteers.
