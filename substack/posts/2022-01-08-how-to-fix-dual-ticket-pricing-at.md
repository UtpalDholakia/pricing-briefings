In a recent [blog post](https://www.psychologytoday.com/blog/the-science-behind-behavior/201604/why-dual-ticket-pricing-india-s-taj-mahal-is-wrong), I described how the dual ticket pricing at India’s Taj Mahal [fails](https://www.psychologytoday.com/blog/the-science-behind-behavior/201604/why-dual-ticket-pricing-india-s-taj-mahal-is-wrong) on many levels. Here is what is wrong with it:

- Instead of charging tourists different prices based on whether they want a premium experience (e.g., shorter lines), dual pricing discriminates between tourists simply based on whether they are Indians (“domestic”) or non-Indians (aka “foreigners”), with a huge, unjustified [price differential](http://www.tajmahal.gov.in/ticketing.html). Indian citizens pay \$.70, and foreign tourists pay \$15 to get in.

![Untitled by Imke Stahlman Flickr Licensed Under CC BY 2.0](https://bucketeer-e05bbc84-baa3-437e-9518-adb32be77984.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/8b0f6fd7-ede1-4c58-b721-52b1e0cfde5c_320x213.jpeg)

- Because of this segregation, Indian tourists are treated like (and feel) inferior, [second-class visitors](http://www.wanderink.com/archives/back-to-raj-at-the-taj/) when visiting a monument in their own country. For example, foreign tourists are called “high value ticket holders” on various signs. They have a special counter to buy tickets. And only Indians are asked pay 3 cents (2 Rupees) to use the toilets in the complex. All this is [quite humiliating](http://www.thehindu.com/thread/arts-culture-society/article7783596.ece).

- On the flip side, many foreign tourists [feel discriminated against](http://qz.com/392883/visiting-indias-ancient-monuments-prepare-for-racism/). They have to pay the higher price, with no choice in the matter. Whether they can afford it or not.

- What is more, dual pricing [encourages corruption](http://www.navhindtimes.in/dispute-between-up-tourism-asi-over-number-of-visitors-at-taj/), which is already a [serious problem](http://www.ey.com/IN/en/Services/Assurance/Fraud-Investigation---Dispute-Services/Bribery-and-corruption-ground-reality-in-India) in India. For example, [foreign tourists](http://www.ey.com/IN/en/Services/Assurance/Fraud-Investigation---Dispute-Services/Bribery-and-corruption-ground-reality-in-India) who look like Indians can offer a bribe to avail of the lower-priced ticket.

### **So what should a well-designed pricing structure look like?**

According to pricing experts, a well-designed pricing structure for any product or service should possess [three core principles](https://hbr.org/2012/06/pricing-to-create-shared-value): (1) ***fairness*** – customers should perceive prices as fair and reasonable; (2) ***flexibility*** – customers should be able to choose the price they wish to pay (corresponding to the quality they want); and (3) ***transparency*** – the rationale for the pricing structure should be clear and make sense to everyone concerned.

A well-designed pricing structure maximizes profits earned by the company. For a non-profit organization like the [Archaeological Survey of India](http://asi.nic.in/asi_monu_whs_agratajmahal.asp) (ASI) that manages the Taj Mahal, pricing should raise sufficient revenue to cover expenses and upkeep. And it should be designed to contribute to the tourists’ once-in-a-lifetime experience.

Unfortunately, Taj Mahal’s current dual ticket pricing fails on all these points.

### **Four Suggestions for revamping Taj Mahal’s ticket pricing structure**

Here are some suggestions to apply the principles of fairness, flexibility, and transparency to Taj Mahal entrance ticket pricing.

### **1) Replace dual ticketing structure with two-tiered pricing.**

[Tiered pricing](http://summitevergreen.com/why-tiered-pricing-is-the-only-way-to-price-your-product/) is used in virtually every industry and is commonly used to price tickets. For a play or a concert, for example, a customer can choose a cheaper ticket in the balcony or a more expensive one up close. Similarly, many theme parks like Six Flags [sell](https://www.sixflags.com/fiestatexas/store/vip-tours) a “regular” priced ticket and a “VIP tour” ticket that gives expedited access to buyers for a higher price. The key is choice! If this sounds like Taj Mahal’s current pricing structure, in some ways, it is. But there is one crucial difference. The distinction is based on how much a person is willing to pay for a shorter line and quick entry. It is not based on who the person is. With this minor modification, anyone, Indian and non-Indian tourists alike, will be able to purchase the ticket they want. Corruption will wither away.

### **2) Lower the premium ticket price.**

In addition to replacing the forced Indian/foreigner segregation with general/ premium tickets, the price levels need to be adjusted a bit. When we actually work out the math, the high tourist ticket prices are not really bringing in that much money to the Taj Mahal. According to ASI’s ticket sales data, [approximately 700,000](http://timesofindia.indiatimes.com/india/Number-of-foreign-visitors-to-Taj-Mahal-on-wane/articleshow/45723601.cms) foreign tourists visited the monument in 2014. (I couldn’t find numbers for 2015). At \$15 per ticket, that’s \$10.5 million (not counting foreign children who got in for free). Not exactly mega-bucks.

![Misty Morning by Nomad Tales Flickr Licensed Under CC BY 2.0](https://bucketeer-e05bbc84-baa3-437e-9518-adb32be77984.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/c490bec5-bb01-4bbf-bfe8-4738992ca039_320x213.jpeg)

In comparison, the Statue of Liberty had [4.2 million visitors](http://www.statista.com/statistics/254218/number-of-visitors-to-the-statue-of-liberty-in-the-us/) in 2014 and the Louvre had [9.3 million visitors](http://www.diplomatie.gouv.fr/en/french-foreign-policy/tourism/events/article/the-louvre-the-most-visited-museum) that year (and 70% of them were foreign tourists).

For world-class attractions, ticket prices are only a small fraction of the economic value generated from tourists. The example of Louvre in Paris makes this point clear. Even though it charges all tourists a fixed 15 Euros per ticket, a majority of visitors still get into the museum for free. How? Everyone gets in for free on Sunday, younger visitors under 18, artists, teachers, etc. never pay, EU residents under 25 never pay, etc. Assuming half of Louvre’s visitors got in for free, it still made around 62 million Euros from ticket sales, or around \$70 million. Yet its “[direct economic impact](https://www.google.com/url?sa=t&rct=j&q=&esrc=s&source=web&cd=1&cad=rja&uact=8&ved=0ahUKEwicjb22kZTMAhUD92MKHZJGBZwQFggfMAA&url=http%3A%2F%2Fwww3.grips.ac.jp%2F~culturalpolicy%2Fpdf%2FThe%2520Economic%2520Impact%2520of%2520the%2520Louvre%2520Coointeg..pdf&v6u=https%3A%2F%2Fs-v6exp1-ds.metric.gstatic.com%2Fgen_204%3Fip%3D76.238.179.143%26ts%3D1460843314846214%26auth%3Dvjwcirggk4ffkgxtdug3sg42wrwxw4f3%26rndm%3D0.37070340782104394&v6s=2&v6t=146195&usg=AFQjCNFy1ZZDGPuAaiSUtvcGSFhfRvWvaA&sig2=P0ifKKos-eAdA-GUE3XGLA&bvm=bv.119745492,d.cGc)” that included such things as spending by tourists during the visit, concession sales, special events, and filming was between 417 and 756 million euros, 7 to 12 times higher.

Much the same kind of amplification and economic impact model applies to the Taj Mahal. Ticket sales don’t have to be a significant source of revenue. In fact, ticket prices could be easily subsidized through creative ways such as soliciting large donations from private foundations or individuals (after all, India has [plenty of billionaires](http://www.forbes.com/india-billionaires/list/), any one of whom could foot the annual bill easily), or by creating a “patron club” which provides free access to the Taj in exchange for a substantial donation, or commissioning and selling exclusive merchandise at a premium imitating many [museums and monuments](http://www.thestatueoflibertymuseumstoreonline.com/) in the United States. Or with a combination of these methods.

Just look at the major [museums in England](http://www.telegraph.co.uk/travel/destinations/europe/uk/london/weekly-poll/8938699/Should-Londons-free-museums-and-galleries-start-charging.html). They are free for everyone, all the time.

My main point here is that the \$10 million of revenue generated from foreign tourists could be easily obtained from any one of a number of potential sources. The high ticket prices and the resulting angst are unnecessary.

### **3) Stop charging people to use the toilets.**

Charging for toilet use is probably (no, certainly!) the worst bit of the current pricing scheme. No one should have to pay to use the facilities when visiting the Taj Mahal. There is no economic justification for charging a pee fee. It is far too little to do any good. After labor costs of monitoring, collecting, tallying, depositing, etc. are factored in, the ASI quite likely loses money on this venture. More importantly, it lowers the quality of experience of Indian tourists drastically, and is like a slap in their collective faces.

### **4) Provide free admission to students, wherever they are from.**

![The colorful Taj Mahal by Tim Moffatt Flickr Licensed Under CC BY 2.0](https://bucketeer-e05bbc84-baa3-437e-9518-adb32be77984.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/e617033a-9416-483a-a2fd-b90a47629ff0_320x263.jpeg)

Finally, it doesn’t make much sense to charge students, no matter where they are from. A visit to the Taj Mahal does more than earn a few bucks from tourists. Done correctly, it creates a life-long memory for the visitor, and an [affinity for India](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vZyZvKivcpw) as a tourist destination to visit again and again. Many students, domestic and foreign, who have a marvelous experience on their visit, will return in the future and spend a lot more money on touristic pursuits. There is no need to nickel and dime them when their wallets are thin. Currently, Taj Mahal’s ticketing approach is not to charge children under 15. This could easily be extended to cover all students (perhaps every one under 21, and of any age with a valid student ID).

There you have it. From an implementation point of view, these four changes are **relatively minor tweaks** to the existing pricing structure. But I would argue that they would entirely dismantle the sense of discrimination that infuses the current dual ticket pricing approach. It makes it fairer, flexible, and transparent, and allows tourists of every nation to have a pleasant experience when visiting one of the world’s most amazing monuments.
