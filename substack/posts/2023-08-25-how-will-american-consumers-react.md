Recent data from various sources indicates that inflation is moderating or even reversing from its high levels of 2022. It also appears this downtrend is driven by major spending categories that matter the most to the personal finances of American consumers, such as [rent](https://www.wsj.com/articles/renters-are-about-to-get-the-upper-hand-f6387df4) and [vehicle purchases](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2023-07-10/used-car-prices-in-us-tumble-most-since-the-start-of-pandemic). (The [trends for things like groceries and gasoline](https://www.wsj.com/articles/after-pulling-inflation-down-gasoline-and-food-threaten-to-nudge-it-up-51cba41f) are choppier).

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/f0550b85-2b83-41c5-9dcb-393eee1d5035_789x519.png)

Overall inflation and core inflation in the US, August 2023

To those of us interested in the specific question of consumer response to inflation, consumer expectations of inflation are also coming down. At the aggregate level, inflation expectations surveys suggest that consumers are paying attention to the tempering of prices. In the latest results from the July 2023 Survey of Consumer Expectations conducted by the New York Fed, expectations of inflation over the next year (also called short-term horizon expectations) came down to 3.5% from 3.8% in April. (As the graphic below shows, the long-term benchmark is around 2.5%).

This is potentially good news because where consumer buying activities are concerned, perceptions often precede and drive buying behavior (A [nomological chain](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2023-08-11/us-consumer-year-ahead-inflation-expectations-unexpectedly-fall?sref=d3S20v77) that goes something like *lowered inflation expectations -\> greater optimism and financial confidence → more risk-taking -\> greater spending & consumption* seems reasonable).

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/c1845a60-639b-4a91-ac5d-8b8d1da49077_625x377.png)

Inflation Expectations of American Consumers, August 2023

These dual trends of lower inflation and lower inflation expectations have led many experts to heave a sigh of relief and predict that consumer buying behavior [will normalize soon](https://www.nytimes.com/2023/07/12/business/economy/inflation-economy-fed-rates.html). In the current context, such a prediction implies a [cautious optimism](https://www.cnn.com/2023/08/16/business/holiday-sales-2023-forecast-coresight/index.html#:~:text=%E2%80%9CWe%20are%20cautiously%20optimistic%20about,digits%20from%20a%20year%20ago.) that the robust rate of buying and spending that we saw in [consumer durables during the pandemic](https://www.clevelandfed.org/en/publications/economic-commentary/2021/ec-202116-durable-goods-spending-during-covid19-pandemic), [consumer discretionaries afterward](https://www.washingtonpost.com/business/2021/06/18/luxury-boom-recovery/), and [consumer staples throughout](https://www.wsj.com/articles/consumers-have-powered-through-the-pandemic-and-inflationuntil-now-11659265202) will continue or even trend higher as the 2023 holiday season approaches, and into 2024.

Such predictions beg the question of what our understanding of consumer psychology tells us about consumer reactions to moderating inflation expectations after a period of rising inflation. In this post, I want to rely on this lens and explore the question, *“How is consumer behavior likely to change in response to moderating inflation?”*

The question is interesting because many of us, in our roles as managers or as consumers, haven’t encountered this situation in decades, and a significant number of us (those born in the 1980s or afterward) haven’t encountered this at all.

Here’s my prediction in a nutshell, and then I will explain why in detail.

Any significant aggregate behavioral response of consumers to sustained moderating inflation, if and when that occurs, is likely to occur slowly, hesitantly, and over a relatively lengthy period of time, over the course of years rather than months.

To consider this issue, I use a model of consumer behavior that is influenced heavily by routines, uses biased and incomplete information frequently, and relies on simple and convenient heuristics for decision making. In other words, a consumer who’s a cognitive miser and a creature of habit.

For us humans, these ways of choosing, buying, consuming, indeed of *living* if we want to be properly expansive – in terms of the inputs and processes involved – are hard to change nimbly, even when external economic conditions change. They take time and cannot be hurried.

It is worth exploring how these notions of cognitive miserliness, reliance on habits, and responding to economic change, which in our specific case is moderating inflation, play out. We can do so by considering some specific factors, such as disrupted reference prices, the use of pricing structures by businesses to conceal inflation, and the role of buying cycles and purchase timing of consumers, that highlight the relation between inflation, prices, and consumer perceptions and responses.

#### **After a lengthy period of relative stability and behavioral reinforcement, consumers’ internal reference prices have been disrupted.**

Throughout the 2000s and 2010s, inflation in most categories consumers purchased and paid for regularly remained low. What that meant was prices also increased relatively slowly and steadily, or not much at all.

Take the case of the Subway \$5 footlong promotion, where you could buy a 12-inch Subway sandwich for \$5. Subway launched this promotion nationally in 2008 (and even earlier, [in 2003 in Florida](https://thehustle.co/the-rise-and-demise-of-subways-5-footlong-promotion/)), and although it was withdrawn from time to time because it was [not the most profitable](https://www.restaurantbusinessonline.com/financing/why-subway-franchisees-hate-5-footlong) promotion for operators, it persisted all the way through mid-2020.

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/32c8ab58-65cd-45d6-b9af-c6c6208d8aac_1751x588.webp)

Note: This promotional offer from Subway is from June 2020, during the height of the Covid shutdowns.

When you can buy the same substantial and delicious lunch for close to two decades for the same price, that price becomes entrenched. It becomes a price point[^1]. Not surprisingly, \$5 became a significant [internal reference price](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/the-science-behind-behavior/201509/what-shoppers-should-know-about-reference-prices) for lunch for many consumers. Other fast food and even some fast-casual operators adopted it enthusiastically. The result was you could get all sorts of combos and meals at the \$5 price point for nigh twenty years; inflation was marginal, and even disgruntled operators could find a way to make money at \$5.

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/82878c03-3951-4301-b641-2b9e03268594_1283x640.png)

A variety of \$5 meal deals from different US-based fast food operators.

But with the inflation over the past two years, the \$5 lunch and its associated reference point are [not tenable](https://www.marketplace.org/2023/02/02/how-fast-food-chains-are-buffeted-and-buoyed-by-inflation/). American consumers can no longer find lunch for \$5. Below are the prices from a popular food truck in Houston called Dripped Birria from 2022 and 2023. Every menu item increased in price, some more than others. A quesadilla went up from \$10 to \$14, while the Dripped Fries went up from \$9 to \$14. Even the Elote went up from \$4 to \$5.

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/3054e3fe-d7d3-4117-bc47-ed4f0eef0309_1764x882.png)

Prices at Houston-based Dripped Birria food truck in 2022 and 2023.

My point is that as a lunch buyer, I no longer have a strong internal reference price to make my lunch-buying decisions. Everything seems expensive compared to my previous “\$5 for lunch” price point. And given that prices are still increasing, I haven’t yet settled on a higher reference point that I believe will stick for a while. (Should it be \$12, or [perhaps \$15 is more sustainable](https://www.nrn.com/fast-casual/are-consumers-hitting-price-increase-breaking-point)?) Many consumers are confused and don’t know how to use price to make smart lunch-buying decisions.

If and when inflation moderates and stabilizes, this confusion is not going to go away quickly. It won’t be quick or easy to replace the entrenched \$5 price point for lunch, the \$400 price point for a car payment, the \$1,200 price point for rent, and so on. None of these price points are ever coming back (except as Throwback Thursday promotions). It will take a long time, of the order of years rather than months, for new price points to replace the old ones, take hold, and influence habitual buying behaviors that we’d grown accustomed to.

#### Many sellers have shielded consumers from experiencing the effects of inflation directly.

Throughout the lengthy episode of higher inflation after the Covid pandemic, input costs have continued to increase for many products and services. Rather than simply pass on these costs to customers with across-the-board price increases, most sellers chose more subtle pricing strategies. They adopted a variety of methods to tweak [their price structures](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/pricing-concept-understanding-the)[^2] to buffer or hide price increases.

Take the case of restaurants. Ingredients soared in cost in 2022. The [price of snow crab leg increased](https://www.nrn.com/finance/when-are-price-increases-warranted) from \$17 in the fall of 2021 to \$79 per pound by the summer of 2022, while the Japanese amberjack (popularly called the Hamachi Yellowtail) went up in price from \$8.80 to \$17 per pound. These sorts of large cost increases occurred for virtually every ingredient used by restaurants, from [bread flour](https://sfstandard.com/2023/01/23/a-12-loaf-of-sourdough-blame-rising-flour-prices-and-the-cost-of-quality-assurance/) to [eggs](https://www.cnbc.com/2023/03/03/egg-prices-increased-70percent-over-the-last-yearheres-why-.html) to [beef](https://www.wsj.com/story/beef-prices-are-high-heres-why-a28ca6df) to [seasonal fruits and vegetables](https://www.thepacker.com/news/retail/retail-food-inflation-slowing-usda-reports-farm-prices-fruits-and-vegetables-still). Within a short period of time, ingredient costs went up from 25-30% of the price to 40% or more for many restaurants. Not to mention the sharp increase in labor costs to recruit replacement workers for [those who left the industry](https://skift.com/2020/05/08/u-s-leisure-and-hospitality-industy-lost-8-2-million-jobs-in-2-months/) during the pandemic [for greener pastures](https://www.washingtonpost.com/business/2023/02/03/worker-shortage-restaurants-hotels-economy/).

At first, viewing inflation as transitory, restaurant operators decided to [swallow the cost increases](https://www.codehospitality.co.uk/industry_news/cambridge-chef-on-why-hes-taken-the-difficult-decision-to-raise-menu-prices/), leaving prices unchanged. But as costs kept going up, this became infeasible. Restaurants used a range of indirect approaches to raise prices, from [reducing menu size](https://www.wsj.com/articles/restaurant-menu-food-costs-shortages-11639437261) (by taking lower-margin items out), [shrinking portion sizes](https://nypost.com/2022/06/15/shrinkflation-on-the-menu-restaurants-cut-portions-to-scrape-by/), [substituting high-cost ingredients](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2022-05-12/how-inflation-is-shrinking-food-portions-at-domino-s-burger-king-restaurants?sref=d3S20v77), reducing service levels, cutting down on promotions, reducing hours of operation, introducing lines of take-home products like [sauces, seasonings](https://shop.momofuku.com/), and [take-home meals](https://www.restaurantbusinessonline.com/marketing/cracker-barrel-intends-tout-value-5-go-meals) (with lower incremental costs).

[Embedded content: www.youtube-nocookie.com](https://www.youtube-nocookie.com/embed/XSla0ipV8rc?rel=0&autoplay=0&showinfo=0&enablejsapi=0)

Others left menu prices unchanged but added surcharges to their customers’ bills. The names for these usually mandatory surcharges varied, limited by the restaurant owner’s creativity: Covid surcharge, PPE surcharge, inflation surcharge, employee benefit charge, hospitality charge, [living wage charge](https://www.ohioemployerlawblog.com/2022/04/dont-confuse-tips-and-service-charges.html), and so on. And I didn’t even [mention tipping](https://thehill.com/business/4063179-is-restaurant-tipping-getting-out-of-hand/). From a discretionary and relatively modest “reward” given for receiving good service, it has become a [virtually extortionary](https://www.wsj.com/articles/tipping-self-checkout-restaurants-airports-c3e09f7?mod=article_inline) surcharge to be paid out in every service transaction, with [potentially violent ramifications](https://www.nbcnews.com/news/us-news/doordash-driver-curses-customer-tip-online-debate-rcna92596) when consumers don’t comply. (More about the vagaries of tipping in a future post).

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/1cdf6c69-f6ca-4d09-8316-f4477a35fe42_566x751.png)

This two-pronged restaurant industry approach of raising prices [indirectly](https://www.wsj.com/articles/how-companies-raise-prices-without-raising-prices-11637490602), and adding surcharges and fees that are seen as separate from the “main” prices has been common in many [other industries as well](https://www.wsj.com/articles/how-companies-raise-prices-without-raising-prices-11637490602). Words like *[shrinkflation](https://www.bloomberg.com/opinion/articles/2022-07-26/shrinkflation-is-back-with-new-ways-to-short-consumers?sref=d3S20v77)* and *[surcharge](https://www.bloomberg.com/opinion/articles/2022-06-16/inflation-is-making-drip-pricing-a-bigger-consumer-hazard?sref=d3S20v77)* that used to be relatively esoteric before the pandemic are now invoked routinely by everyone.

For consumers, the upshot is that it becomes really difficult to make sense of the current prices, the logic behind them, and how they got here from their previous lower levels. When I order Thai food through Doordash, for example, I might see that the final bill for my order is \$77, but because of all the aforementioned tricks, it is hard for me to learn the price of each individual menu item or figure out how their prices have changed in the last two or three years. Even more problematic is the fact that the effects of this partitioning or breaking up of the total inflated price on consumers can be insidious.

There are a [number of old](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S105774081500042X?casa_token=L163DqZdZ0QAAAAA:pFQa5Y9MKbMFrSflXe5cRnYgteH7OlPTgHtwH2hS5gTbLyj3HrcdtgdmvLA9w6e8lzQpE7xfiCHT) and [recent experimental](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4479218) consumer psychology studies that find that consumers actually react more favorably when the prices are partitioned or itemized as opposed to provided as a single aggregate amount, mainly because they underestimate the total amount when they have to add up its components. What’s more, itemized prices appear to be transparent and confer goodwill on the brand. In the words of [White et al. (2023)](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4479218), who conducted the recent interesting research on partitioned prices,

> “While preferring partitioned disclosures (without totals) may not reflect a general mistake, these results suggest that the preference is exploitable. Firms can use partitioning to shroud total prices while signaling transparency and trustworthiness.”

All of this is to say that sellers may have shielded consumers from the effects of inflation by avoiding straightforward inflation-congruent markups thus far, but their strategies have hindered consumers’ processes of sense-making and comprehension of what current prices are and what they should be.

#### The inflated prices of big-ticket categories like housing and cars are irrelevant to most consumers, but their effects are consequential and persistent for some consumers.

A third complicating factor is that while the reporting on inflation and consumer response usually occurs at an aggregate level, its effects are dispersed individually and unevenly. Thus, we while might say that average [home prices went up by more than 20%](https://www.reuters.com/markets/us/us-existing-home-sales-slide-again-june-2022-07-20/) in two years, that [used car prices went up by 40%](https://www.usatoday.com/story/money/cars/2022/02/13/used-cars-cost-more/6778705001/) year-over-year in January 2022, or that the inflation expectations of US consumers [went down to 3.5% in July 2023](https://www.newyorkfed.org/newsevents/news/research/2023/20230814#:~:text=Median%20inflation%20expectations%20declined%20across,reading%20is%20the%20lowest%20since), these numbers aren’t meaningful to consumers in any common way.

Someone who purchased a house a few years ago and is locked into a 15-year fixed mortgage at around 2.1% is not going to care that the 15-year mortgage rate has risen to 7.25% now. And someone who drives a paid-off car with no plans to replace it is not going to be affected by used-car inflation. Thus, the inflated prices of high-expense categories like housing and cars are irrelevant to most consumers. Whether inflation goes up or down in these categories really doesn’t matter to them. Even if inflation comes down, these consumers are unlikely to make any buying decisions, such as buying a new house or car, because they don’t need these things.

On the other hand, it matters a great deal to that small subset of consumers who are in the market and need to make a purchase in these categories. For these consumers who must buy a new house at an elevated price *and* pay the high mortgage rate, renew an apartment rental lease for 25% or 30% more than last year, or [pay \$30,000 for a used car](https://money.com/new-used-car-prices-december-2021/), the effects will be substantial. More importantly, they will be long-lasting, affecting their spending and financial situation for years.

They will have to keep making their large monthly payments month after painful month, perhaps long after the inflation rate comes down. Their current buying response is locked in by the inflation rate (and the prices that prevailed) when they made the buying decision. The same applies to those making other big purchases using installment loans like home improvement projects, vacations, and so on. They’ve already paid the higher prices and signed on to higher-interest loans and are not affected by the moderating inflation.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

#### Conclusion

In this post, I’ve argued that a number of factors have weakened the link between changing inflation rates and consumer buying response. I considered three in particular: (1) a distortion of reference prices after a lengthy period of stability that allowed consumers to form and use reference prices, (2) the exposure to indirect price increases and mandatory surcharges that hid the nature and logic of inflation-driven price increases, and (3) the susceptibility of a subset of consumers to financial ramifications of major purchase decisions that extend out for years into the future.

The bottom line is this. If and when inflation comes down and stays down, consumers’ buying response is likely to be tepid and indecisive because of these three factors, with “normalization,” in any behavioral sense of the word, likely to be drawn out over a lengthy period.

[^1]: A *price point* is the price level that is so well-known and well-accepted by consumers that they consider it to be the normal or usual price for the product. Consumers use the price point as a reference point for making buying decisions with little or no cognitive effort. \$5 for lunch is an example of a price point.
[^2]: A *price structure* is defined as the overall assortment of prices, price-benefit combinations, price variations, and price offers provided by the company to its customers. It covers all the different ways pricing is used strategically to align the company’s offering to customer beliefs and assessments, competitors’ offerings, and other contextual marketplace factors.
