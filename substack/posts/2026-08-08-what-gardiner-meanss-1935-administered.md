##### ***Summary:*** Gardiner Means’s 1935 idea of administered prices helps generate fresh insights into many current pricing issues. Prices often seem to be dictated by the marketplace, but in large organizations, they are usually managerial decisions based on costs, competitors, customers, channels, algorithms, brand meaning, and other factors. The administered prices lens helps to explain why price increases can be sticky, why algorithmic pricing can increase margins without collusion, why shrinkflation preserves symbolic price equity, and why greedflation is more complicated than businesses being greedy.

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/6c5027f7-418e-406d-8d2f-d89556cfc6c5_1672x941.png)

*“If greedy businesspeople were responsible for price increases, then slowing inflation would mean they are becoming less greedy.” – [Phillip S. Coles in the Wall Street Journal](https://www.wsj.com/opinion/greedflation-is-high-priced-baloney-economy-policy-9418cf4e).*

*“Companies, which in normal times are wary of angering customers with big price changes, seem to have seized on the excuse of generalized inflation to shield their margins, with drops in actual sales mostly offset by higher prices.” – [Jon Sindreu in the Wall Street Journal](https://www.wsj.com/finance/greedflation-is-realand-probably-good-for-the-economy-6c475b8e).*

On January 17, 1935, the U.S. Senate published a document titled “*[Industrial Prices and Their Relative Inflexibility](https://archive.org/details/CAT10505731/page/n3/mode/2up).”* Its author was Gardiner C. Means, then a 38-year-old economic adviser to the Secretary of Agriculture. The report’s thesis influenced the thinking behind New Deal industrial policy and sparked debate in academic circles over administered prices that went on for over 40 years[^1]. Over the years, as often happens with influential and clever ideas, Means’s work has faded from attention, and few of today’s pricing decision makers are familiar with the concept of an administered price.

In this post, I want to explore Means’s thesis in detail, focusing on the ideas that remain most applicable today. This discussion will reveal that his framework has much to say about many of the important issues in contemporary pricing, including the logic behind sticky high prices, why algorithmic pricing tends to push prices higher even when there is no collusion, why shrinkflation is effective even with vigilant consumers, why list prices function as psychological anchors that firms guard fiercely, and why the greedflation debate requires more nuance than the usual moral framing allows. Means considered the logic behind many of these questions all the way back in 1935 and came up with insights that pricing decision makers would benefit from engaging with.

### **The Core Findings of the Gardiner Means Report**

Means’s central question was why some prices fell sharply during the Great Depression while others remained relatively unchanged. To answer it, he used Bureau of Labor Statistics wholesale price data from 1926 to 1933 and compared the magnitude and frequency of price changes across product categories. He reported a number of interesting findings. Let’s start with the high-level descriptive finding, shown in the figure below (Chart 1 in the Means report, p. 2).

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/b6cd0e64-26e3-4ea7-8cb2-ea14851962d9_624x563.png)

Source: Industrial prices and their relative inflexibility. Letter from the Secretary of Agriculture, transmitting in response to Senate Resolution No. 17, a report relating to the subject of industrial prices and their relative inflexibility, Internet Archive.

In his own words:

##### *“Chart 1 indicates the very great importance of administered prices in the American economy. It shows all the commodities making up the Bureau of Labor Statistics’ wholesale price index (except railroad and utility rates and a few composite items) distributed according to frequency of price change. The chart covers the number of changes from month to month for each item during the 8-year period from 1926 to 1933…The U-shaped character of the distribution curve carries the usual suggestion that there are two quite different type of price. It is clear that the highly flexible prices of the right-hand group of items are for the most part made in the market, and are the type of prices around which traditional economic analysis has been built. The inflexible prices of the group of items at the left of the chart are established administratively and held for appreciable periods of time. More than half the items covered in the chart averaged less than three changes a year.”*

His second core finding was the relationship between the frequency and magnitude of price changes, shown in the next figure (Chart 3 in his report, p. 4).

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/a8fdc1d0-bfdc-490b-b579-d538ca9dddc1_624x587.png)

Source: Industrial prices and their relative inflexibility. Letter from the Secretary of Agriculture, transmitting in response to Senate Resolution No. 17, a report relating to the subject of industrial prices and their relative inflexibility, Internet Archive.

He concluded that “The items which changed frequently in price show a large drop during the depression while those having a low frequency of change tended to drop only a little in price.”

Means also found large differences across product categories. Prices for agricultural products and raw materials changed frequently in response to market conditions and, consequently, many of these prices fell sharply between 1926 and 1933, during the Depression. In contrast, prices for categories such as agricultural implements, iron and steel, automobiles, and anthracite showed far greater price stability, even as demand and output declined.

Another important finding in the Means report was that this pattern was related to industry structure. Prices tended to be more flexible in markets with many sellers and standardized commodities, and relatively rigid in concentrated industries that were dominated by a smaller number of firms. In sectors with flexible prices, prices responded quickly to falling demand, whereas in rigid-price sectors, falling demand showed up mainly in reduced production and job losses. In these industries, firms kept their posted prices relatively stable, instead preferring to produce less, lay off workers, and idle capacity when demand decreased.

Means interpreted this divergence between flexible and rigid prices as evidence that some prices were not being established in the same way as prices in commodity markets. In concentrated industries, prices were often set by firms through managerial decision making. These decisions reflected costs, competitors’ likely responses, long-run relationships, internal pricing policies, and how customers would perceive a change to the posted price. Means called these prices “administered prices” and contrasted them with prices determined more directly by the market. In his words,

##### **“An administered price… is a price which is set by administrative action and held constant for a period of time. We have an administered price when a company maintains a posted price at which it will make sales or simply has its own prices at which buyers may purchase or not as they wish. Thus, when the General Motors management sets its wholesale price for a particular model and holds that price for 6 months or a year the price is an administered price. Many wholesale and most retail prices are administered rather than market prices. For administered prices the price is rigid, at least for a period of time, and sales (and usually production) fluctuate with the demand at the rigid price.” (p.1).**

What his analysis showed was that a stable price did not necessarily mean that supply and demand were in balance. An alternative, equally viable interpretation of a stable price was that the firm had chosen not to change its posted price, instead adjusting another managerial decision variable such as production level, employment level, or its manufacturing capacity.

### **Means’s Perspective on Administered Pricing**

To appreciate why Means’s observations were so significant, it helps to contrast them with the classical view of how prices work. According to the classical view, the price is the level at which the market clears, where the supply and demand curves intersect. Thus, whenever demand changes, prices adjust to restore balance, meaning the broader economy self-corrects as prices fall, real wages adjust, and purchasing power recovers.

Means argued that in large parts of the modern economy, this mechanism simply does not work. In concentrated industries (he mentioned General Motors several times in his report), prices are set as a matter of policy by managers who weigh other considerations such as the goodwill of customers, the risk of price wars, the stability of relationships with distribution channels, the appearance of equanimity to investors, and the desire to signal strength to competitors, and to this list we can add any number of other factors that pricing strategists would consider today in setting prices. When demand falls, rather than lowering prices as theory suggests, companies choose to reduce production, resulting in unemployment, idle capacity, and, eventually, depending on how widely this approach is used and for how long, a recession.

The implications of this idea go far beyond the pricing decisions of individual firms. If prices are administered rather than market-clearing, as Means argued, then the classical adjustment mechanism doesn’t function in exactly those parts of the economy that matter most for employment and output. For example, during the Great Depression, as demand collapsed, prices for agricultural commodities fell by more than 60 percent while farms continued to produce. In contrast, prices for agricultural implements fell by roughly 6 percent over the same period, but here, it was production of agricultural implements that collapsed, and factory workers were laid off in large numbers.

The concept of “cost” also becomes harder to pin down because, in a large, multi-product firm with substantial fixed costs, marginal cost is at least partly a decision variable, reflecting managers’ preferences for allocation, sourcing, and capacity utilization. For example, consider an automaker that produces both sedans and trucks at the same plant. The marginal cost of a sedan depends on how the firm allocates shared overhead, which production line runs at full capacity, whether components are sourced internally or from suppliers, and how the firm depreciates its plants and tools. Each one is a managerial decision, and different decisions would yield very different marginal cost numbers for the same sedan. Given that cost is an outcome of subjective choices, the markup and the price are also the consequences of those choices and are subject to managerial discretion. The familiar equation, price – costs = profit, loses much of its explanatory power once costs themselves are determined by firms rather than discovered in the marketplace. (You can explore a 1950s take on this point by [Robert Lanzillotti in this post](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/the-lost-language-of-pricing-restraint).)

The upshot of this idea is significant for policymakers. Monetary policy, and demand management more broadly, are based on the assumption that prices will respond to changes in aggregate demand on a predictable timescale. Means argued that this assumption breaks down precisely where it matters most. If a large share of the economy is governed by administered prices that change in response to the firm’s decisions rather than market forces, then a central bank that tightens credit, expecting prices to fall, may instead see production cut and workers laid off, while prices barely budge.

This is what made Means controversial in his own day. He claimed that the New Deal’s economic recovery could not be engineered solely through demand-side tools. Before policymakers could fix the economy, they had to ask structural questions, such as which industries had concentrated pricing power, how those firms were using it, and what it would take to restore the kind of price flexibility that was central to the theory of market-based prices.

### **How Administered Prices Faded from View**

Means’s administered prices hypothesis generated decades of debate. The most influential challenge came in 1970, when George Stigler and James Kindahl published *The Behavior of Industrial Prices*. Their critique focused on a key observation about the administered prices as defined by Means: Did he mean list prices the firm posts publicly and quotes in its brochures, or those quoted by sales staff? Or did he mean realized prices (or what the authors called transaction prices), which is the price the customer actually pays after all the incentives, such as discounts, rebates, freight adjustments, etc., are deducted?

As we well know from the more recent extensive research by McKinsey and others on pocket prices and the pocket price waterfall, the gap between list prices and realized prices can be substantial, especially in concentrated industries selling to large industrial buyers. In his analysis and in the 1935 report, Means relied on BLS list price data, which would understate price flexibility. It could very well be that the firms were making far more adjustments in their price structure below the surface. To examine this issue more closely, Stigler and Kindahl collected data on realized prices (a.k.a. transaction prices) directly from industrial buyers and found that prices varied far more frequently and by larger amounts than was evident from list price data. Thus, they argued that the rigidity in administered prices was significantly overstated and, in reality, it was an artifact of how prices were measured rather than a structural characteristic of concentrated industries.

Although Stigler and Kindahl made a legitimate point, their critique missed the deeper argument Means was making. His core claim was that the nature of price setting in concentrated industries was fundamentally different from what classical theory described, oriented toward objectives unrelated to market clearing, such as maintaining customer and channel relationships, avoiding price wars, strengthening the brand, and signaling stability to investors and competitors. The debate continued for decades, with authors marshaling arguments for or against the administered pricing hypothesis, and eventually, mainstream economics moved on.

This was an intellectual loss, because all this while, the economy was running a natural experiment, the results of which favored Means’s hypothesis. The share of activity in concentrated industries grew, and firm-level markups rose steadily over the decades, as De Loecker and coauthors have estimated (although it is debated)[^2]. Pricing departments at major companies evolved from offshoots of accounting departments into strategic functions with their own software stacks, dashboards, and greater authority to manage prices. In the modern economy, most prices are “administered” in the sense that Means defined it, even though few pricing decision makers may be familiar with this term.

More recently, after the pandemic, people began to talk about greedflation or profit-led inflation, implicitly treating pricing power as binary, i.e., a firm either has it and uses it, or it does not. Back in 1935, a core contribution of Means’s report was to argue that a firm’s pricing power is a matter of degree and form, not to mention the ever-changing pricing context. Nearly every firm of meaningful size sets its prices through internal decision making, often using a combination of strategic thinking and powerful technology, rather than letting the market decide the price. The key questions are what their pricing decisions are designed to achieve, on what timescale, and through what mechanisms. Once we frame the impact of pricing strategy in this way, we can generate new, useful insights into current pricing controversies. Let’s look at four issues that have been in the public eye recently.

### **Examining Four Current Pricing Controversies Through the Lens of Administered Pricing**

#### *1. Algorithmic pricing, collusion, and higher prices and margins*

At first glance, when a company or an entire industry adopts algorithmic pricing (which could happen over years), it appears to be a move in the direction of flexible, market-responsive pricing, because pricing algorithms usually consider supply and demand (whether in real time or with a lag) to set prices. The frequency with which inputs are updated dictates how often prices change; in general, this results in far more frequent price changes than before, making algorithmic pricing appear antithetical to administered pricing.

The reality is that when a company uses algorithmic pricing, it is administering prices according to a specific pricing policy (defined by the algorithm’s design). This explains why algorithmic pricing in concentrated markets can raise prices rather than lower them, even in the absence of collusion. In one German study of retail gasoline markets, for example, researchers found that in local duopolies where both gas stations adopted pricing algorithms, margins rose by approximately 28% after adoption[^3]. In contrast, in markets with just one gas station (i.e., no competition), there wasn’t a change in margin. Each company’s algorithm was running an administrative policy on its behalf, optimizing for the company’s chosen objectives, which would typically be margin protection and matching competitor prices. When two algorithms operate in the same concentrated market, each one observes the other’s prices and adjusts its response. Over time, the structure of this interaction moves both algorithms toward an equilibrium in which neither firm cuts prices aggressively, because doing so would lead to retaliation and hurt both. This results in higher margins for both firms than direct price competition would generate. Essentially, the same dynamic that operated in the steel and automobile industries during Gardiner Means’s time is now powered by algorithms in fuel retailing, rental housing, and many other concentrated markets.

This discussion has significant ramifications for regulators. For example, the [Preventing Algorithmic Collusion Act of 2025](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/senate-bill/232), now pending before the Senate Judiciary Committee, is designed as antitrust legislation with the core thesis that when competing companies use shared pricing software and nonpublic competitor data to artificially inflate prices by coordinating prices through algorithms, creating what might be called “[collusion by code](https://www.reuters.com/legal/legalindustry/collusion-by-code-understanding-algorithmic-pricing-antitrust-enforcement--pracin-2026-03-20/),” consumers end up paying higher prices to their detriment. However, our discussion suggests that prices and margins may also drift higher when competing firms use algorithmic pricing with independent algorithms, software, and data in concentrated markets. The collusion may not just be in the code; it may be in the market structure.

#### *2. Sticky price increases, or why prices rise like rockets but fall like feathers*

Anyone who has worked on a company’s pricing strategy (or been a particularly perceptive, observant shopper) will have an instinctive understanding of the asymmetry in price changes, known as the “rockets and feathers” effect (“Prices rise like rockets but fall like feathers[^4]”). When costs rise, companies pass them on quickly. For instance, the recent rise in oil prices has led to a [spate of fuel surcharges](https://www.wsj.com/business/airlines/flight-jet-fuel-prices-airline-surcharges-9bb238d5). In contrast, when costs fall, companies delay in passing on cost savings to customers, enjoying higher margins for longer. In one large study covering 165 B2B and 77 consumer offerings, prices rose faster than they fell in approximately two-thirds of the cases, and the immediate response to a cost increase was at least twice the response to a cost decrease, with the gap sustained for five to eight months[^5].

There’s been a great deal of debate about the moral and political implications of this seemingly self-serving pricing strategy. However, looking at this issue through the lens of Gardiner Means’s administered pricing thesis offers a different perspective and yields new insights. It could be that prices raised during the cost increase are now the new administered prices, and their stickiness derives not only from the higher cost levels, but also from other considerations such as how the marketplace, especially customers and competitors will view a price cut, whether another input cost increase will occur soon only to result in a quick price increase, whether raising and lowering prices too frequently will impact the company’s brands negatively, and so on. In other words, it is all the baggage attached to the firm’s price structure beyond its relationship to costs that slows down the price cut.

#### *3. Shrinkflation as managerial protection of symbolic price equity*

[Shrinkflation](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/why-shrinkflation-works-so-well-and?utm_source=publication-search), defined as reducing the volume or quantity of a product while keeping the price unchanged, has received significant negative publicity over the last three or four years. Having taught MBA-level pricing strategies for close to two decades, I can vouch that far fewer people used the term shrinkflation before the pandemic, but everyone knows about it now and sees it as “[a sneaky way to price gouge](https://www.theguardian.com/australia-news/2023/sep/19/australian-supermarkets-should-use-shrinkflation-labels-to-warn-shoppers-and-shame-brands-advocate-says).” Again, Means’s perspective offers a different, fresh way to look at shrinkflation decisions by brands, as a virtually perfect application of administered price.

If prices simply reflected supply and demand, there would be no reason to hold the dollar amount constant while quietly changing the quantity; the relevant variable for shoppers’ decision making would be the price per unit. But administered pricing focuses on different objectives, and one that makes sense in this case is the potential symbolic value of price. Let’s imagine a product that has been selling for a well-known reference price, like a AriZona Iced tea Big Can at 99 cents, even while reducing volume from 23 oz. to 22 oz., or a [rotisserie chicken at Costco for \$4.99](https://www.tastingtable.com/2180013/facts-costco-rotisserie-chicken/). Arizona Beverage Company is explicit about the logic of its pricing. In a piece titled “[Why is AriZona Tea Still 99¢](https://drinkarizona.com/blogs/news/why-is-arizona-iced-tea-still-99?srsltid=AfmBOoqyVAqLD3JUKtgFpp0wM6nMLMF_UrdcRv4nZtensBmOLgFrJyhz),” the company notes that while coffee that once cost a dollar “now pushes five,” the AriZona Big Can still carries the same price it had decades ago, printing the price directly on the can. The price, the company says, has become part of the brand’s identity, “like a badge of honor.”

As this explanation clarifies, these and many less entrenched prices are embedded with significant [symbolic value](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/the-eight-psychological-affordances?utm_source=publication-search), which we can call “symbolic price equity,” and changing them would be tantamount to destroying brand equity. Thus, through Means’s lens, shrinkflation is a useful example of how brands administer the posted price as an asset.

#### *4. Greedflation as a consequence of an opportunistic reset of administered prices*

The current round of the greedflation debate has been raging since late 2021, with proponents arguing that supply chain disruptions during and after the pandemic created a conducive opportunity for large corporations to use cost increases as justification to hike prices and protect or even increase their margins[^6]. On the other side of the argument, many economists have rejected greedflation, instead suggesting that pandemic-era stimulus and low interest rates fueled inflation[^7]. From our perspective, there’s some merit on both sides of the argument. On the pro-greedflation side, research suggests that the late 2021 inflation surge coincided with an increase in the share of price growth attributable to corporate profits, which then faded in 2022[^8]. Companies using high markup factors behaved differently from those using low markup factors, capturing the benefits of subsequent cost declines as profit rather than passing them on to customers[^9]. On the anti-greedflation side, skeptics are correct in pointing out that greed is not an economic variable and that firms have [always sought to maximize profits](https://www.wsj.com/opinion/greedflation-is-high-priced-baloney-economy-policy-9418cf4e) when given the opportunity, and that there’s nothing unique about this particular period.

Here, too, as in the previous three cases, the administered prices concept gives us fresh insight. According to this perspective, we can say that what changed during the period from 2021 to 2023 was the opportunity for pricing decision makers to manage prices and exercise their pricing power. A real, clearly visible, and broad-based cost shock occurred due to explainable circumstances (i.e., supply chain disruptions because of COVID aftereffects and the Russia-Ukraine war). After a long stretch of relatively stable prices and low inflation, this gave pricing decision makers across industries permission to reset their list prices higher. The environment was conducive to raising prices, with less backlash, regulatory scrutiny, or competitive retaliation due to the circumstances. Once list prices were reset higher, the rockets-feathers asymmetry described earlier came into play, so that increases in costs began to slow and then reverse while prices held steady. We can conclude that rather than greed, it is the predictable inertia of administered prices that is at work here.

### **The Virtues of Administered Pricing**

In the four cases covered in this post, administered pricing has been discussed largely in terms of its problems. However, this misrepresents the concept, and to balance its examination, we need to consider its virtues, which boil down to the benefits of relatively stable (as opposed to volatile) prices for buyers and sellers. On the [customer side](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/why-shoppers-hate-frequent-price?), stable prices reduce search and transaction costs and increase price knowledge, enabling customers to make better buying decisions and use prices more effectively. On the seller side, stable prices allow firms to choose and implement a positioning and branding strategy that uses pricing strategy as a way to build the brand. Administered prices also support long-term contracts, channel relationships, and capacity planning, and reduce the risk of bruising price wars.

#### *How Administered Are Your Prices? And Is that Effective for Your Organization?*

If you make pricing decisions for an organization, as a consultant, manager, or entrepreneur, Gardiner Means’s administered price framework offers an opportunity to evaluate your pricing strategy and check the extent to which your prices are administered and, consequently, how effective your pricing strategy is. The following set of questions provides a brief audit for your consideration:

1\. ***When did your list price last change in response to a change in customer demand, rather than a change in your cost structure or your competitor’s prices?*** Administered prices change based on an internal orientation, largely ignoring what customers are willing to pay. If your recent price moves haven’t considered the customer value prong of the [pricing pillars](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/the-four-pillars-of-pricing-strategy?utm_source=publication-search), your pricing is administered.

2\. ***Are your actual realized-price adjustments happening somewhere beyond the list price level?*** When costs change or competitive pressure shifts, ask [how your pricing structure adjusts](https://www.wsj.com/business/retail/how-companies-raise-prices-without-raising-prices-11637490602). If your list price holds steady while your organization shrinks the package, tightens payment terms, adds fees or surcharges, scales back the standard discount, reduces trade allowances, or reduces service levels, then you are administering the list price as a brand asset**.**

3\. ***How does your organization react when costs fall?*** In a heavily competitive industry, a firm passes on cost declines to customers because it has little choice. In contrast, an administered-price firm seeks to capture most of these reductions through higher margins, since it has the autonomy to do so.

4\. ***How does your pricing algorithm (if you use one) respond to competitor price changes versus customer buying behavior?*** Algorithms that give greater weight to competitor prices and less weight to customer price response are administering rather than reacting to demand. When two or more firms in a concentrated market run such algorithms, they can track each other and converge on similar prices without ever communicating. We can think of this as administered pricing in a technology-enabled form.

5\. ***Could you defend your current price to a regulator using only your marginal costs and what your customers are willing to pay?*** A “yes” answer means that your organization’s prices are behaving like market prices. A “no” answer will imply that other forces are at work, such as protecting brand positioning, honoring channel commitments, supporting capacity decisions, signaling margin discipline to investors, and so on.

6\. ***How does your organization respond if your main competitor cuts prices by 10%?*** An immediate price cut would indicate a market response. In contrast, the administered-price firm would not match right away; instead, it would assess the possibility that the competitor might cut again, the impact on the brand, the effects on customer psychology, and so on.

As your answers to this short list of questions should make clear, chances are that your organization, like most organizations today, administers prices. In fact, every principle of designing and executing effective pricing strategies is based on administered prices. Obviously, the core question in pricing strategy is *how* prices are administered, i.e., the devil is in the details.

Gardiner Means’s key contribution to how we think about pricing strategy today is to explicitly frame prices as governance decisions, rather than market outcomes, and to explicitly frame price-setting as a complex set of questions about price structures. As we saw throughout this discussion, once we understand the administered price concept, we have a vocabulary and a framework to make sense of a wide range of current pricing issues, from algorithmic prices and collusion to sticky price increases to shrinkflation and greedflation. Each of these issues turns out to be a variation on the question of who administers the price, on what authority, with what tools (i.e., how), and to what end.
