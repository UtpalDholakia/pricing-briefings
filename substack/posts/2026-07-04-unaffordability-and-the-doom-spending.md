##### *Summary*: With homeownership and retirement security beyond their grasp, many younger consumers respond by placing a greater value on the present and doom spending. In this piece, I argue that doom spending reflects unaffordability of two kinds, economic and psychological. The affordability barrier establishes objective unaffordability when the consumer has zero likelihood of attaining homeownership, and distance from this barrier establishes a propensity to doom-spend. The result is a decoupling of price sensitivity, such that consumers subject to unaffordability refuse large commitments while willingly paying premium prices for smaller purchases that offer solace. For brands, doom spending offers premiumization opportunities that should be approached ethically.

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/453c515e-960a-436f-88c7-2149c346864f_1672x941.png)

Certain patterns of consumer behavior in the United States are puzzling many observers and should interest readers of this Substack. Prices remain well above their pre-pandemic levels even as inflation has moved off its 2022 peak, and similarly, [mortgage rates have remained elevated](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-05-21/mortagage-rates-hit-highest-since-august-as-war-fans-inflation?sref=d3S20v77) for a lengthy period. Housing affordability has declined significantly, with the [median home now costing close to six times](https://econofact.org/hitting-home-housing-affordability-in-the-u-s) the median household income, up from about 4.5 times in 2010 and 3.6 times in the mid-1980s.

The puzzle is this: despite this so-called [affordability crisis](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/may/31/young-adults-financial-independence-affordability-crisis), spending in discretionary categories, especially by younger consumers, has remained strong. For instance, major concert tours [sell out at record prices](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20250514994325/en/Sound-Investments-New-Survey-from-Cash-App-Shows-How-Gen-Z-Is-Turning-It-Upand-Shelling-Outfor-Live-Music-This-Summer), many young millennials and Gen-Z consumers spend [hundreds of dollars on food delivery](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/01/30/dining/food-delivery-apps-doordash-uber.html) every week, and splurge on an [ever-changing cast](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20260210084352/en/71-of-Consumers-Say-Viral-Trends-Drive-Purchases-Putting-New-Pressure-on-Retail-Fulfillment-Finds-Locus-Robotics) of [items that go viral](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/06/04/style/dot-cakes-tiktok.html) on TikTok and Instagram. Many younger consumers are trading up on [small, premium indulgences](https://www.cnbc.com/2025/08/09/from-lipsticks-to-concerts-the-treatonomics-trend-is-booming.html) such as specialty coffee or a customized facial treatment as a psychological remedy, a phenomenon [market researchers have called “treatonomics.](https://www.ibisworld.com/blog/treatonomics-retail-consumer-goods/1/1126/)“ Whereas a decline in consumer confidence and affordability would ordinarily lead consumers to batten down the hatches and increase their savings rate, the opposite is happening as younger consumers are ramping up their everyday spending.

One perspective with which treatonomics is viewed is that young consumers today (like young people in previous generations) are [simply careless with money](https://thefinancialbrand.com/news/gen-z-banking/in-credit-cards-gen-z-is-swapping-credit-scores-for-social-stardust-193126), an updated version of the decades-old “[avocado toast](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/the-science-behind-behavior/201705/why-eating-avocado-toast-can-set-you-back-financially)“ or “[latte factor](https://www.amazon.com/Latte-Factor-Dont-Have-Rich/dp/1982120231)” arguments, which maintain that consistently spending small amounts on frivolous purchases can add up over time to a meaningful shortfall for a big purchase like a home. In these discussions, the focus is on the consequences of frivolous spending, not the reasons behind it. It’s an intuitively appealing narrative, but the magnitudes involved don’t make sense, as summarized by this [2017 New York Times article](https://www.nytimes.com/2017/05/15/business/avocado-toast-millennials.html):

“The truth is, even if millennials assumed the eating-out habits of baby boomers, it would take around 113 years before they could afford a down payment on a home (assuming a 20 percent down payment on the median price for a home in the United States, [\$315,000 in March 2017](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/MSPNHSUS), and a 1 percent yearly yield rate).”

These numbers and the barrier to home purchase for Gen-Z and young millennial consumers are even higher today, nearly a decade later. A second explanation for treatonomics focuses on the “why,” or the psychological remedy aspect of spending. It argues that the anxiety and hopelessness about the future fuel these purchases. However, this explanation still treats consumer behavior as a purely emotional response to repair one’s mood.

A third, more balanced explanation, particularly from the perspective of pricing strategists, is that the current behavior of young consumers is sensible and supported by two different meanings of unaffordability. It says that younger consumers are paying premiums for current consumption because the traditional financial milestones of adulthood, such as buying a house or saving enough money for a comfortable retirement, have moved beyond their grasp, i.e., become objectively unaffordable. To unpack this explanation, we need to distinguish between the economic and psychological meanings of unaffordability. In this piece, I want to examine this explanation in greater detail and then consider its implications for pricing strategies.

### **The Economic Meaning of Unaffordability**

For a large and growing share of young consumers, buying a house is no longer economically feasible. The share of millennial renters who aspire to buy a home but have no down-payment savings rose from [48 percent in 2018 to 67 percent in 2023](https://www.apartmentlist.com/research/millennial-homeownership-2023). More than one in five adults now say they will never afford their dream home, and the figure is closer to [one in four among millennials](https://www.self.inc/info/millennial-homeownership-statistics/). (The numbers for Gen-Z consumers are even lower, but part of that is because many are still too young to buy a house by traditional standards.) At the same time, the personal savings rate fell from [above five percent in early 2025 to below three percent (in April 2026)](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/PSAVERT), while about a third of adults reported using savings to pay for ordinary expenses.

These ominous financial statistics help clarify something that is commonly misconstrued in discussions of doom spending by younger consumers. These consumers are not necessarily buying a small luxury as a substitute for a home purchase or for regular retirement saving, nor do they see these things as interchangeable or related. They are consuming this way because the goal of home ownership is no longer attainable, and buying smaller, more affordable items that bring them pleasure in the present is a reasonable thing to do.

We can call this the “affordability barrier,” which we can define as the price level of an asset, in this case a house, above which purchasing the asset is economically infeasible, i.e., objectively unaffordable, for a particular consumer, given their income trajectory, savings, and net worth. In this sense, it is a binding constraint for the consumer; there is little they can do on their current trajectory (barring winning a Mega Millions lottery) that will allow them to afford that asset. However, unaffordability also has a second, distinct meaning that is psychological, which needs to be unpacked to fully understand doom spending.

### **The Psychological Meaning of Unaffordability**

Most popular accounts of doom spending by younger consumers lean on the hyperbolic discounting explanation[^1], defined as the tendency of individuals to overvalue immediate rewards relative to future ones. The logic is that younger consumers are present-biased and therefore devalue future rewards. However, a closer consideration suggests that, for hyperbolic discounting to apply, the person must be able to believe that future rewards will accrue when evaluating the present decision. As discussed in the previous section, if future rewards are authentically seen as unattainable, discounting them does not make sense.

A more relevant concept that applies to doom spending is probability discounting, defined as the devaluation of uncertain rewards. We can surmise that as consumers consider the likelihood of future rewards (e.g., the likelihood that I will be able to buy a house in the future), and discount them given the low probability of their attainment[^2].

This psychological affordability comes into play once the economic aspect has played out. For some consumers, the affordability barrier may be absolute, in the sense that they will never be able to save enough to buy a house, and they know it. For others, there may be uncertainty about whether purchasing a house is just barely affordable. As the consumer moves further towards the affordability barrier and beyond into the domain of absolute unaffordability , they are likely to concern themselves less and less with saving money or thinking about the future, and more and more with seeking pleasurable consumption in the present[^3]. See the figure below for a graphical depiction of this discussion.

Two other well-established consumer behavior mechanisms are also consistent with this explanation. The first is compensatory consumption, defined as consumers’ tendency to engage in buying activities to repair a threatened sense of status, control, or self-worth[^4]. Specifically, when people feel a loss of control or status (as will be the case when they are stranded beyond the affordability barrier), they will often gravitate toward products or brands that are visible, symbolic, or emotionally rewarding, rather than merely inexpensive. This helps explain why doom spending often involves the purchase of conspicuous or experiential items.

Second, the so-called “lipstick effect,” according to which the purchase of small indulgences remains resilient during economic downturns, is also relevant here. When much of one’s life seems to be devoid of the meaning that selecting and striving towards challenging, longer-term life goals provides, the consumer turns to diverting their goal pursuit towards more attainable, pleasure-oriented short-term goals as a coping mechanism[^5]. Enjoying a good dinner, a short trip, or a wellness service provides relief from economic conditions the consumer cannot otherwise change, much like fiddling on the Titanic to have one last good time before the curtain descends.

### **How Unaffordability Influences the Consumer’s Price Sensitivity**

What, precisely, is this consumer buying? They are not buying expensive caffeine offered at a high markup, nor are they buying the ability to record a billionaire singer belting out songs in a crowded arena with their iPhone to post on TikTok. What the consumer is buying when they purchase these products and experiences is a brief respite from a permanent stressor, a “this will never happen” hole in their future, that is beyond resolution. In such purchases, the hedonic value derives from the experience of solace rather than a functional or even an emotional benefit. In such situations, charging normal prices would be setting them too low, because what is sought and delivered to the customer is so valuable. The price is a signal of solace, and higher prices equate to greater solace.

For such consumers, doom spending is a marker of decoupled price sensitivity across time. The same consumer can be frantically searching for an eighth point off a mortgage, trying their best to jump over the affordability barrier, while at the same time being insensitive to a twelve-dollar matcha latte or a nineteen-dollar cocktail. Refusing a mortgage whose interest rate is deemed even a little too high is a rational response to a large financial commitment toward a goal that intimidates and seems unreachable. In contrast, happily shelling out the premium price for a drink also makes sense because it is something they can control, afford, and experience now to feel a semblance of solace.

This creates a pricing opportunity that works through two mechanisms that may reinforce each other. The first is the purchase price threshold. Specifically, consumers judge small discretionary purchases by absolute dollars, rather than with percentage markups. A coffee that costs the seller little can earn a very high margin, because the buyer registers the seven-dollar price, not the markup behind it, and seven dollars sits comfortably below the point at which a purchase starts to require justification. The brand can capture a large percentage margin as long as the absolute price stays under that threshold. For businesses that cater to doom spenders, the prices must signal a response to doom; if they are too low, they won’t work. A \$3 coffee soothes nothing and nobody, whereas a \$12 matcha provides comfort and solace. See the figure below for a current example.

The second is the benchmark (or the lack thereof) against which the price is judged. The consumer’s discipline that once restrained their purchase of small indulgences like avocado toast and lattes was based on paying attention to their opportunity cost, so that every dollar they spent was a dollar they were not saving toward the house. But once that goal has been abandoned because they had no choice, the comparison (and therefore the benchmark) is no longer meaningful. The seven dollars spent on a coffee drink is no longer measured against a house down payment. These two effects may reinforce each other, as the loss of the benchmark raises the threshold for justifying spending. Both mechanisms support high prices with high margins and explicitly disfavor lower prices that provide greater economic value as conventionally defined. Here are three specific ways brands can implement this idea through deliberate, value-adding premiumization pricing strategies.

#### **1) An intentional “trading up” pricing strategy**

This sounds counterintuitive in an environment with persistent inflation and nervous, pessimistic consumers, but marketing experts have found that customers across all income levels will pay a steep premium in a few categories that matter to them and economize in others[^6]. Doom spenders fit this characterization perfectly, because they are no longer allocating their income to a future home purchase; they are ripe to trade up.

The way to do this is to intentionally add higher-priced tiers (keeping incremental costs as low as possible) alongside standard offerings (also known by brand managers as “upward line”). Depending on the category and brand, this may be an ultra-premium, premium, or masstige option. We see this phenomenon play out in skincare at the brand level, where many consumers are shifting from drugstore brands to higher-priced, higher-quality brands like The Ordinary and [La Roche-Posay](https://cosmeticsbusiness.com/la-roche-posay-to-rule-dermo-cosmetics-and-make-3-billion-this-year) that offer the formulation and feel of luxury brands at lower price points. In 2024, while both prestige and mass facial skincare sales declined, the [masstige segment grew by double digits](https://www.gcimagazine.com/brands-products/skin-care/news/22933514/why-shoppers-are-choosing-masstige-skin-care-over-prestige-and-mass-2024-sales-2025-trends), the strongest performer in the category. This type of trading-up shift is happening in other doom-spending-prone categories, like fragrance and live entertainment. In fragrance, for example, consumers are buying more expensive, higher-concentrated scents (extraits), luxury designer brands (e.g., Chanel, YSL, Dior), discovery sets, body sprays, and layering products. In live entertainment, offerings have become [far more complex](https://culturaleconomics.org/trends-in-concert-ticket-pricing-and-consumer-preferences-whats-driving-the-changes/) (and expensive) with more price tiers, VIP packages, premium lounges, exclusive merchandise, and higher on-site spending.

#### **2) Occasion-based framing of experiential offerings**

The second approach focuses on how the purchase is framed for the consumer. A well-established finding in consumer psychology is that people evaluate experiences less comparatively than they evaluate objects. Specifically, a tangible product encourages comparisons with other options and with previous and competitive prices, whereas an experience is judged largely on its own terms because it is difficult to establish comparable equivalents[^7].

For a brand, this means folding a product or a set of complementary offerings into an experience. For instance, a bottle of wine can be evaluated by a shopper based on market prices and prices of previous vintages, but when that bottle becomes part of a wine tasting that includes other wines, premium cheeses, and a compelling narrative tying them all together, the shopper finds it harder to make that comparison. The concreteness, immediacy, and certainty of the experience, and the opportunity for immediate pleasure at an affordable (though higher than the unbundled product) price make this offer compelling to those in a doom-spending mindset. The popularity of experiential offerings like a guided, focused flight of cold brews or lattes at a coffee shop or a tasting tour at a distillery attests to this pricing principle. Many of these experiential services are limited time in the sense of being pop-ups or seasonal offerings, further benefiting from the present-orientation associated with doom spending.

#### **3) Adding (and pricing) features to support the customer’s need for belonging**

The third approach is to price for belonging. When the future is uncertain, the markers of a person’s current identity are emphasized. Some of the roles that once defined adult life, especially that of a homeowner, are now foreclosed, and need to be replaced with other valued, substantial identities, usually through consumption.

Consumer researchers have studied the significance of brand community, whereby group membership in communities built around a shared attachment to a brand and marked by kinship among members, common rituals, and a feeling of mutual obligation[^8]. Such communities are even more powerful for today’s consumers, with significant pricing implications: adding community elements such as rituals, recognition, and a shared sense of belonging (at low incremental cost) will make an offering more attractive and increase the ability to charge a price premium.

Take the example of a conventional gym versus a boutique fitness studio. In the latter case, comparable equipment and workouts are packaged with regular classes, the ability to form teams, instructors who know members by name, and a recognizable brand to belong to. These additions can support a far higher price, for example, from \$30 per month to \$30 per session. The community elements justify the premium price. Another recently popular service in the same domain is run clubs, which often begin as free group runs, then add paid memberships, branded apparel, race entries, training programs, and ticketed events to build on the developing community.

Belonging-based premiumization aligns with the doom-spender ethos because the identity of being a community member serves as a substitute for being a homeowner and a responsible member of one’s community. While these traditional markers of one’s place in society took years or even decades of saving and progression to earn, a studio membership, club affiliation, or branded community offers a more immediate and accessible identity marker with similar characteristics.

### **Cautionary Boundaries for Doom Spending Premiumization**

Pricing strategists considering these and other ideas to capitalize on the doom spending premium discussed here should be cautious about two things. First, doom spending is a coping response by unfulfilled consumers; as such, it is contingent on the concreteness of the affordability barrier. If economic conditions improve either in the aggregate, say housing prices fall significantly, or for specific younger consumers, the doom spending could reverse quickly as consumers change their orientation back to the future and quickly adopt frugal practices. Contrarily, if economic conditions worsen, say due to an AI jobpocalypse, the discretionary funds available for doom spending will dry up. Either way, doom spending premiumization is very much a tactical pricing strategy at the margin and should be treated as a way to earn high-margin revenue at low incremental cost, and assessed periodically.

The second concern with the discussion in this piece is ethical. There is a fine line between premium pricing in lieu of the amplified emotional value delivered to consumers by giving them solace and pricing to take advantage of the consumer’s despondency about what they may never attain. A core best practice of any premiumization pricing strategy is that a premium-priced product must have commensurate high quality; few things can provoke a sense of betrayal or a sharper backlash or a question about ethical pricing than discovering that one’s own hopelessness is being used to extract higher prices.
