> “Earlier, we would consume about two or three kilos of tomatoes a week in our family of five. Now we are only consuming half a kilo per week.” - [Neeta Agarwal, Software Developer, East Delhi](https://www.nytimes.com/2023/07/21/world/asia/india-tomatoes-shortages-climate-change.html)

There are endless ways to raise prices indirectly without ever changing the asking or visible prices. Here’s an interesting case study of indirect price increases by Burger King and Subway stores in India in response to steeply increasing tomato prices. This case has some common aspects to other forms of hidden price increases that I’ve covered here on [the Pricing Conundrum](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/), but it also has one or two significant wrinkles worth exploring further.

### The Tomato Vacation Approach to Raising Prices

This month (August 2023), [Burger King](https://www.bbc.com/news/world-asia-india-66519700) and [Subway stopped](https://www.reuters.com/world/india/some-subway-india-outlets-drop-tomatoes-citing-poor-quality-amid-price-surge-2023-07-22/) adding tomatoes to their sandwiches, wraps, and other menu offerings in many of their Indian stores. This came on the heels of [McDonald’s withdrawing tomatoes](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2023-07-08/mcdonald-s-limits-tomato-usage-in-india-amid-price-surge?sref=d3S20v77) a few weeks earlier. This “tomato vacation,” as Burger King called it, was in response to nationwide shortages and a five-fold increase in the price of tomatoes. This move by the fast food chains is noteworthy for three reasons: First, tomato slices are a significant component of menu offerings in India, where many of the items tend to be vegetarian. Second, tomato prices have been in the spotlight for the past several months, even having [political repercussions](https://thewire.in/economy/tomatoes-the-modi-government-has-made-a-pr-stunt-out-of-a-real-issue).

Third and from a pricing perspective, the most interesting and uncharacteristic aspect of Burger King’s and Subway’s tomato vacation approach was that they actively advertised the fact that they had withdrawn tomatoes from their menu offerings by posting notices in their stores.

In this case study, I want to explore the reasons behind this move and draw some lessons for managers interested in using this so-called tomato vacation approach to raise prices.

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/7aa62dd2-97ed-4b70-ab3f-3843b4801789_1435x2064.jpeg)

First of all, why is this an indirect price increase?

Burger King’s and Subway’s sandwiches and wraps contained tomatoes before, with a certain cost to the operators and a certain benefit to the consumers (juicy goodness plus nutritional value). Now, at least temporarily, they don’t, but the prices of these items remain unchanged. In other words:

Degraded Product + Same Price = Indirect Price Increase

Here is the backstory for the tomato vacation approach that Burger King and Subway used. Tomatoes have become a focal food item in India this summer with the “[red gold rush](https://www.israelhayom.com/opinions/will-indias-red-gold-rush-bring-down-modi/)” in full swing. In the first half of the year, key tomato-producing regions of India experienced unexpected and extreme weather patterns, including [excessive and persistent temperatures](https://www.nibsm.org.in/tamatar-ka-rate-kya-hai/) in some areas and [heavy unseasonal rains](https://www.hindustantimes.com/cities/pune-news/unseasonal-rains-wreak-havoc-in-maharashtra-affecting-crops-and-causing-damage-to-property-and-life-relief-measures-announced-by-state-government-maharashtrarains-cropdamage-naturalcalamity-101680974052256.html) in others. The upshot was that tomato crops were affected significantly, leading to a [nationwide shortage and rising prices](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2023/jul/10/tomato-crisis-hits-india-as-rain-ravages-crops-and-prices-rise-400-percent) by as much as 500 percent compared to last year, as explained in this video.

[Embedded content: www.youtube-nocookie.com](https://www.youtube-nocookie.com/embed/SEJ7UsGeJuE?rel=0&autoplay=0&showinfo=0&enablejsapi=0)

So it makes sense that restaurants across India have curtailed the use of tomatoes this summer, effectively raising prices for customers buying their food. This, by the way, is the [standard recommended practice](https://www.touchbistro.com/blog/how-to-navigate-restaurant-menu-price-inflation/) for restaurant operators when prices of inputs go up. Substituting cheaper ingredients for more expensive ones and reducing portion sizes are two of the most widely used remedies when restaurant operators want to leave menu prices unchanged.

### Publicizing the removal of tomatoes from menu offerings

The most interesting aspect of this tomato vacation case study is the fact that both Burger King and Subway chose to publicize that they had removed tomatoes from their menu offerings and degraded them by posting explanations in their stores.

Subway provided an explanation that sounded like it was supply-based:

> “We are ever committed to serve you the best food with the best of ingredients. Despite our best efforts, we are not able to get adequate quantities of tomatoes which pass our world class stringent quality checks. Hence for the time being we are forced to serve you products without tomatoes.”

Burger King’s explanation had some overlap but was a lot more ambiguous:

> “Due to unpredictable conditions on the quality and supply of tomato, we are unable to add tomatoes in our food. Rest assured, we are doing our best to get back tomatoes in our burgers. Till then, we thank you for your patience and understanding.”

First, it’s worth reiterating that these types of explanations are by no means common. Restaurants delete, add, and modify ingredients in their offerings all the time, and often for the same reasons as Burger King and Subway. But they hardly ever draw attention to the fact. And advertising an ingredient withdrawal is even more unusual.

Then, why have these two operators gone against conventional wisdom, and what can we learn from this form of publicized indirect price increase?

### Providing explanations is a good idea when raising prices, especially when customers are likely to notice

I’ve written before about the virtues of [providing an explanation](https://hbr.org/2021/06/if-youre-going-to-raise-prices-tell-customers-why) when raising prices. Specifically, I pointed out that:

> “Research shows that after the size of the price increase, the [perceived fairness of the motive](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0092070304269953?casa_token=Ii58e-DWFo0AAAAA:8PL-bW8aQ7SGUONxdRbe3MaUSdfTTJLgCOy8FsZxubzbhD0lBj3VBY-bEmIS8vNpZh73ueVLz4_7Xw) for it is the second-biggest driver of how customers react. The [prospect of inflation](https://www.wsj.com/articles/us-inflation-consumer-price-index-may-2021-11623288303), widespread shortages, rising input costs, and [the return to normalcy](https://www.nytimes.com/2021/02/21/business/economy/pandemic-economic-boom.html) after the pandemic are on everyone’s mind these days. Under such circumstances, when customers receive word that the brand’s price is increasing, it simply confirms what they’ve been expecting. It is worth making the effort to craft a short and forthright explanation for why the brand is raising prices.”

This point is especially relevant in the case of fast food restaurants in India at the moment. Fueled by [incessant media discussions](https://time.com/6298825/india-tomato-crisis/), Indian consumers have been paying attention to tomato availability and prices all summer. They will notice if tomatoes are missing from their sandwiches. It, therefore, makes sense to point out that tomatoes are missing and provide a reasonable explanation. Calling the withdrawal a vacation is an advertiser’s attempt at humor that may or may not jive with the target audience. (It didn’t with me, I thought it was hokey!)

### The lack of ingredient availability is a legitimate explanation for a price increase and has significant ancillary benefits

Concerns about the state of the national tomato crop and stories of reduced availability and escalating wholesale and retail prices have [been circulating](https://www.nytimes.com/2023/07/21/world/asia/india-tomatoes-shortages-climate-change.html) in the Indian media for months. Against the backdrop of this heightened public awareness, explaining that high-quality tomatoes are not consistently available right now makes sense for the fast food brands. What’s more, the narrative that tomatoes have been removed because they are not available (whether fully or partially true) diverts customer attention from the fact that removing tomatoes should make the resulting menu items less costly. In other words, even with the seeming transparency of the notices, the fact that this is an indirect price increase remains hidden.

To those noticing, there’s an undercurrent in this explanation that says, “If we didn’t stop using tomatoes at the moment, we would have to increase prices because they are so expensive right now.” In other words, the explanation allows the action to be framed as an implicit tradeoff that the seller is making on behalf of consumers and for their welfare: “More expensive sandwich with tomatoes, or same-priced sandwich without tomatoes.” For a consumer base and a product category (fast food) where budget constraints reign, and even a small direct price increase is intolerable to many patrons, the “same price - no tomatoes” option appears attractive. The underlying subtle narrative explaining the tomato vacation works.

And finally, the supply-based explanations provided by Burger King and Subway indicate that the removal of tomatoes is temporary, subject to supply constraints. When supply resumes, possibly with the new harvest (and prices moderate), there’s the implicit promise that tomatoes will return to the menu offerings. It is only a tomato vacation, not a tomato retirement. Using the lack of availability of an ingredient as an explanation for a price increase is a useful lesson from this case study for pricing decisions. If there is indeed a supply issue, explaining that it is a supply issue is ethical and sincere, and consumers see it as such.

The bottom line is that trying to stay under the radar and hoping that Indian fast food buyers won’t notice the missing tomatoes is a risky proposition in this situation. It is not likely to work because of the prominence of tomatoes in the offerings and the social attention being given to tomatoes. On the other hand, generating and communicating a coherent, authentic narrative that is sensitive to the context of the price increase is an effective way to communicate the price increase.
