***“You’ll get much further if you assume the price is NOT set in stone—it will also show up in your tone and the confidence of your question.” - [Advice from the Krazy Coupon Lady on how to haggle at Home Depot](https://thekrazycouponlady.com/tips/money/7-tips-to-negotiating-your-way-to-extra-savings-at-home-depot).***

In the B2B world, prices have always been negotiable. Any significant contract for purchasing machinery, chemicals, or software is only signed after [intense negotiation](https://hbr.org/2010/02/how-to-size-up-a-negotiation.html).

In contrast, for over a hundred and fifty years, most everyday consumer packaged goods, restaurant meals, apparel, and appliances have been sold using posted prices in the United States with no room for negotiation.

How many of us will walk into our favorite Japanese restaurant and start haggling about the price of the bento box lunch? Or put a 92 oz. container of Tide detergent in our shopping cart and then offer the cashier \$10 in cash for it as “my best and final offer”? It’s just not done, right?

### **The Rise of Posted Fixed Prices**

In a delightful article in the journal *[Business History Review](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/business-history-review)* titled “**[One-Price Policy among Antebellum Country Stores](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/business-history-review/article/abs/oneprice-policy-among-antebellum-country-stores/F4EB7C8A05216E8BF1383405F120FF69)”,** business historian [James Morris](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/business-history-review/article/oneprice-policy-among-antebellum-country-stores/F4EB7C8A05216E8BF1383405F120FF69) provides an illuminating introduction to bargaining in the retailing realm and its decline. He attributes the popularity of bargaining to two reasons: (1) differences in the quality of the consumer goods on sale, and (2) differences in the bargaining power of retail shoppers, closely linked to their social status in their community. According [to Morris](https://www.jstor.org/stable/pdf/3111918.pdf?casa_token=fsGXd217ri4AAAAA:1uBcn58MuyEZvzComCMdy4ClihHYyvbVgh4HN5W-FCsPhlNhGJ7ZGg7yJwCgnbPnSG0QiEIwluYcB6BhCwX1VazslXEN6Fy7LOwmHZHEEvEaNTxdxV9m):

> “Certainly ample evidence exists that higgling \[as haggling was called then\] or pricing according to the relative bargaining power of individual customers occurred to some degree in most country stores, and especially when products of varying quality were involved. In domestic and imported cloth, shoes, boots, men's clothing, dry goods, dishes, hardware, and other commodities the quality varied widely among individual items of the same product. This quality variation permitted a greater range of bargaining between the storekeeper and the customer .”

By the early nineteenth century, these differences were starting to diminish, as American manufacturers adopted industrial processes, and social hierarchies ruptured, at least within commercial spaces, and status differences were obscured by buying rituals and scripts. Industrial production meant that a certain level of quality could be reliably achieved, and one unit of the good would be more or less the same as another one. As far back as 1830, country store proprietors in the United States were adopting a single-price system for selling basic commodities such as flour and sugar.

The period between 1860 to 1920 was [one of transition](https://scholarcommons.scu.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1118&context=history) from negotiated prices to set prices across the United States. Bargaining for food items faded away first, followed by other types of consumer goods. Posted fixed prices became standard practice in stores, and new generations of consumers accepted it as the norm. For shoppers, posted fixed prices removed the stress of bargaining, and made the shopping process faster, smoother, and therefore more convenient. (It also changed the nature of the shopper-service employee interaction dramatically, in ways good and bad, but that’s a story for another day).

Fixed prices sped up the purchase transaction in a country store by an order of magnitude or more (e.g., from fifteen or twenty minutes of back-and-forth “higgling” to a minute or two for the shopkeeper to find the prices, tally the bill, and for the customer to pay it). As branded standardized products became common, so did posted fixed prices.

### **The Decline of Posted Fixed Prices**

The wheel is turning again. Today, in more or less every shopping situation, fixed posted prices are an illusion, surviving mostly because of consumer ignorance, inertia, and reticence. The [price of virtually every retail product](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/the-science-behind-behavior/201706/why-posted-prices-are-often-meaningless), even a package of ham and a single banana in a supermarket, or a double espresso in a coffee shop, is more often than not, negotiable. This is the case even when prices are listed clearly for these items. The only hurdle to turn the fixed price into a fluid price is that the [retail shopper must unilaterally initiate](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/the-science-behind-behavior/201701/how-shop-smartly-when-prices-are-changing-constantly) the negotiation process by asking to pay a lower amount than the posted price. While many consumers know that haggling is on the table at your neighborhood grocery store or [home improvement store](https://thekrazycouponlady.com/tips/money/7-tips-to-negotiating-your-way-to-extra-savings-at-home-depot), [few actually bother or dare](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/the-science-behind-behavior/201612/why-shoppers-hate-frequent-price-changes) to do so.

What’s more, technology-driven pricing processes and changing social norms both favor the abandonment of stable, fixed prices, and the adoption of negotiated fluid prices. On the technology front, there is a strong push for customized prices because of the widespread adoption of [price optimization software](https://pros.com/products/price-optimization-software/). Consider the similarities between today’s algorithm-based customized prices and prices established through bargaining a century and a half ago. According to [historian Steven Gelber](https://scholarcommons.scu.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1118&context=history):

> “Prior to the 20th century, consumption was almost always a personal transaction between a vendor (who was often also the producer) and a buyer, both of whom were likely to know each other as individuals in a unique interpersonal relationship. That broader knowledge of the other complexified the commercial relationship because it triggered feelings that transcended the simple exchange of value. Knowing that the other person was, for example, wealthy or strapped for cash, had a large family or was living alone, was a member of one or another socio-ethnic subculture, and so forth all altered the power relationship in the deal, and virtually every purchase was indeed a deal. Because the price for any item was established at the time of its sale through negotiation, that price often reflected not only the strict exchange value of the item to each party, but possibly also the constraints of ingroup sympathy, the opportunism of out-group antipathy, or particular knowledge of the other person's strengths or vulnerabilities.”

The same type of information the seller and the buyer learned about each other through a lifelong interpersonal relationship in the 1800s is gathered today via the [intense collection](https://www.theguardian.com/commentisfree/2018/mar/28/all-the-data-facebook-google-has-on-you-privacy) of attitudinal and behavioral consumer data by the company, and through intensive online research by the customer. We are reverting to the “[deep knowledge](https://hbr.org/2015/06/focus-on-the-customers-you-want-not-the-ones-you-have)” of the pre-industrial era, insofar as understanding the momentary value of the transaction is concerned. For example, when consumers conduct repeated searches for a particular airfare within a short window of time, they are [likely to encounter increased prices](http://time.com/4899508/flight-search-history-price/) on the sites of online travel agencies.

Separately, spurred on by the Great Recession of 2008-2009, cultural trends favoring mindful purchase and consumption, and encouraging frugality and [minimalism](https://www.becomingminimalist.com/consumer-dont/) have become popular over the past decade. These movements are based on the principle that consumers should make purchase decisions thoughtfully and use every avenue at their disposal to pay the least amount and [get the most value for their money](http://www.mrmoneymustache.com/2015/07/02/if-you-wouldnt-buy-it-you-should-probably-sell-it/) when making purchases. Such extreme value mindsets mean that consumers want to play an active role in establishing prices and engage in vigorous bargaining, even in product categories where everyone viewed prices as fixed until recently. For example, one woman recounted her surprise on the [Make Love, Not Debt blog](http://www.makelovenotdebt.com/2006/06/you_can_negotiate_at_the_supermarket.php) as follows:

> “I was in line at the deli counter behind a tiny, sweet old lady \[at a large chain grocery store\]. She pointed at a small end of a ham in the display and asked the clerk to weight it for her. It was about half a pound. What happened next floored me. “Will you sell it to me for half price?” she asked the clerk. “Yes,” he said, and wrapped it up for her.”

Personal finance bloggers have embraced this idea further by trying different haggling methods and then sharing those that work. For example, on the personal finance blog Wisebread, [Kyle James provides suggestions](https://www.wisebread.com/negotiate-better-prices-on-these-6-grocery-store-buys) for grocery store haggling:

> “The keys to negotiating at the grocery store are always to be polite and have a friendly conversation rather than be demanding. Throw in a legitimate reason to ask for that discount, and you stand a great chance of saving money on your next trip.”

Each year, more and more consumers haggle when shopping for groceries, buying a cup of coffee, booking a hotel room, and shopping for jewelry. They treat every purchase, big or small, as an opportunity to get the best price.

### What Does the Popularity of Negotiated Prices Mean for Managers?

For retail managers, here’s the challenge and the opportunity in a nutshell:

*Fixed prices are receding, fluid negotiated prices are entering more retail spaces in more ways, and more shoppers are embracing the idea of haggling.*

#### 1. Most omnichannel retailers already use pricing strategies with aspects of negotiation embedded in them. But they don’t go all the way.

***Frequent price changes.*** Every time a retailer changes prices (which is more and more frequent these days), it’s a shift away from a fixed, posted price strategy that relies on stable prices. When prices change often, they act as a form of one-sided negotiation. While shoppers can’t initiate a price offer, they can certainly react as if they are negotiating, but either purchasing at a price that they consider to be acceptable or holding off if the price is deemed too high.

***Frequent and complex discounting schedules.*** When a retailer uses a complex and quick-changing discounting schedule (e.g., discounts [every week](https://www.businessinsider.com/kohls-to-offer-more-sales-2019-5)), it’s a signal to its customers that it is open to negotiating prices. If a product was selling for a 20% discount yesterday and it is being offered for a 10% discount today, then many shoppers will naturally feel it is reasonable to ask for yesterday’s “20% off” price.

***Price match policies***. Many retailers like Walmart and Target have price-matching guarantees in some shape or form. (Best Buy [credits price matching](https://www.nytimes.com/2017/09/18/business/best-buy-amazon.html) as one of the key reasons for its turnaround). Walmart, for example, currently [matches prices found](https://corporate.walmart.com/policies#price-match-policy) on Walmart.com in its stores, and prices [found at any online retailer](https://www.walmart.com/help/article/walmart-com-s-price-match-policy/9b1a48788b0045a8a784d34aa566627f) on purchases made on Walmart.com. The rules of the policy are often too complicated for many customers to understand, as this exchange on Twitter illustrates:

![](https://bucketeer-e05bbc84-baa3-437e-9518-adb32be77984.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/d57729b7-64d4-4419-882a-c5906b096ed2_587x741.png)

Price-matching guarantees give an excuse to shoppers that are interested in bargaining. They now have the ability to search for and find lower prices elsewhere and haggle the price down at the retailer’s cash register or online shopping cart.

#### 2. Frontline retail employees need training in how to bargain with customers.

Yes, it seems too much to ask on top of all the other challenges involved in providing high-quality frontline service in today’s retailing environment. But frontline employees need training in how to negotiate with customers who want to haggle. Training is needed on two fronts.

First, frontline retail employees need to know their employer’s price negotiation policies well. This is a particular challenge when the policy is complicated by various clauses and contingencies. For example, price match guarantees can have ambiguous wording about such things as which competitors are acceptable, which products are eligible, and so on. The employee needs to have an understanding of how to enforce the policy when the customer asks for a comparison and a price reduction.

Second, to the extent that the retailer is enthusiastic about incorporating bargaining activities by its shoppers into its pricing structure, frontline workers will need training in negotiation skills. Such skills are useful not just for haggling, but also for [defusing situations involving difficult customers](https://www.retailcustomerexperience.com/blogs/4-negotiation-strategies-for-managing-difficult-retail-customers/). Relatedly, frontline employees also need to be empowered to make offers and counter-offers during the haggling process. And finally, they need to know whether they can initiate the bargaining process or only react when a customer does so.

#### 3. Retailers need to adopt negotiated pricing mechanisms, aka participative pricing, as part of their pricing strategy.

The core idea behind participative pricing is to have customers play an active role in setting the price. The retailer gets to decide how much of a role they want their customers to pay, and have a portfolio of pricing mechanisms to choose from - auctions, pay what you want pricing, name your own price, and even BDM-style bid once only. With a few exceptions like the online fashion retailer Everlane, very few retailers are using participative pricing mechanisms in any systematic or strategic way. Here are some examples of participative pricing, mostly from smaller businesses.

- Everlane routinely has “Choose What You Pay” events where customer can choose what they pay from three or four different prices.

  ![](https://bucketeer-e05bbc84-baa3-437e-9518-adb32be77984.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/7b89b05b-0722-415d-a8e9-b680b739e65c_1191x769.png)

  Restaurants commonly have Pay What You Want promotions on all or part of their menu. They are often combined with giving to a charity or raising funds for a specific cause.

  [Embedded post: https://twitter.com/TrishaHendricks/status/1395481811426283523](https://twitter.com/TrishaHendricks/status/1395481811426283523)

  Personal services like yoga studios, salons, gyms, etc. also tend to use *Pay What You Want*, or its sibling, *Pay What You Can* for such things as launching a new offering, a substitute for a free trial for new customers, or as a fun promotion for existing clients.

  ![](https://bucketeer-e05bbc84-baa3-437e-9518-adb32be77984.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/bdced016-5364-4c7d-a355-ac5556fa1c0a_1153x742.png)

Participative pricing has a lot of potential and retailers are only beginning to scratch the surface of its potential. As retailers move away from stable posted prices, we are going to see lots of new pricing mechanisms pop up to allow customers to participate more actively in establishing prices of consumer products.
