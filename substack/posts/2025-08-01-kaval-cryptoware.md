### **Introduction[^1]**

It was a warm November afternoon in Miami in 2022. The weekend was here, and the city’s crypto crowd prepared to break loose and party. The times were good. However, Ethan Martinez’s mind was elsewhere. A bona fide member of the crypto crowd, Ethan was preoccupied with a significant pricing challenge. He was tasked with pricing Kaval Cryptoware’s new offering, the Tuli + TUSA cryptomining rig. Tuli was Kaval’s new cryptomining rig, and TUSA was the software tool that ran on it to optimize mining activities.

Ethan was full of anticipation and anxiety. As a new product manager at Kaval Cryptoware, this would be his first real project with real responsibility and consequences. Ethan, a computer science engineer by training, had worked as a software developer at a large cryptocurrency trading firm for three years. Then, he returned to graduate school and completed his MBA earlier that year. With the COVID tailwinds behind him, recruiting was a breeze, and Ethan had accepted Kaval’s product manager job offer six months earlier.

**The Pre-Launch Meet.** The pre-launch meeting in Kaval’s thirty-fourth-floor conference room had four participants, led by Rob Agosti, Kaval’s hardware division head and Ethan’s boss. He had called the meeting. Ethan was there, of course, and Amber Thomas and Feng Li were there. Amber Thomas was Kaval’s chief scientific officer. Her team had developed and tested the TUSA software. Feng Li was the company’s marketing director, responsible for designing and implementing product launch marketing programs. Ethan noted that Kaval’s sales director, Tim Broward, was absent and wondered if his absence meant something.

Agosti began by giving the group a brief overview of the new Tuli rig. Tuli was developed to target the growing small mom-and-pop (or, more accurately, “bro-and-bro”) cryptominer segment. “The key to Tuli’s success will hinge on how effectively we can bundle the TUSA software with the Tuli rig and sell the bundle’s advantage to our target customers,” Agosti said. The “Turbo Speed Application Software,” or TUSA for short, leveraged the latest high-performance chip’s design to pare down processing times for cryptomining tasks significantly. Thomas’ team had developed it, especially for cryptomining applications and it worked effectively. Running TUSA could make the Tuli rig up to four times faster, something that many cryptominers would value immensely because of their fluctuating and overall tight profit margins. Agosti said, “Make no mistake, we want to aggressively and confidently push the Tuli + TUSA bundle into the market.” “Is he challenging me, or is this some kind of a coded threat?” Ethan wondered about his boss.

Feng Li was the next to speak. He presented key characteristics of the cryptomining rig industry and shared his team’s uptake forecasts for the Tuli rig (Exhibit 1 provides his slide). He said, “As you can see in this Exhibit, the Tuli rig is targeted towards what we are calling our “core rig” segment. This segment is small but growing quickly. This segment is comprised mostly of individual crypto enthusiasts who set up mining rigs in their homes. Traditionally, we have ignored this segment and focused only on data centers, where we are market leaders with a 23% market share. Our forecast is the core rig segment will consist of about 55,000 rigs in 2023 and grow quickly with a 43% CAGR through the end of 2025, almost double the growth rate of the data center segment.

At this point, Agosti jumped in assertively and said, “Thank you, Feng Li. This is super helpful background information. Ethan, as you can see from these numbers, you have a massive opportunity here to make your mark. Our Tuli rig, equipped with the TUSA, is our lead into a brand-new segment, where we haven’t really paid much attention thus far. It’s all yours to conquer and win over.”

“As you know, MBE has dominated the core rig segment with its Brom rig from the beginning. But with the TUSA, we now have a solid competitive advantage. Correctly pricing the Tuli + TUSA will be crucial for leveraging this advantage. As you work on your pricing recommendation, be sure to consider not only how our potential target customers will respond but also how our competitors, particularly MBE, may react to your price. We need to be cautious.”

The meeting ended soon after that. Ethan was tasked with providing his recommendations for the Tuli + TUSA pricing strategy to the group in about ten days’ time. CRYPTOXPO, a major cryptocurrency trade show, was a month away, and it would be in Miami. It would be the perfect spot to launch Kaval’s new rig bundle to the crypto-enthusiast community.

### **Understanding the Pricing Context**

Over the next few days, Ethan compiled relevant information from internal company reports and various external databases to help him better understand the pricing context in which he was working. He remembered his pricing professor’s sage words from his business school days: “Ethan, unless you fully understand the contextual factors surrounding the pricing decision and account for them, you will miss the boat, ending up with a broken price and a pricing strategy flop.”

#### **The Company**

Ethan first reviewed a summary of Kaval Cryptoware’s history. Kaval was currently the largest player in the relatively new cryptorig industry, which itself had been carved out as a distinct niche from the much larger server hardware industry. Kaval had been the earliest dedicated cryptorig maker, selling its first rig dedicated to mining Bitcoin in the early 2010s. Because of its early start and strong reputation, Kaval was now considered the premium cryptorig brand of choice by major data center operators like Marathon Digital and Riot Blockchain, who mined cryptocurrencies on a large scale using thousands of rigs. The company had the highest market share in the so-called *high-performance cryptorig category,* where its Verkko rig was the established gold standard.

#### **The Customers**

In his research, Ethan learned that about a year earlier, Rob Agosti had pushed to extend Kaval’s cryptorig line at the low end to serve the growing market for individual cryptocurrency enthusiasts. These customers were vastly different from the large miners. They were mostly individuals (not businesses), mostly male, of varying ages, with a majority between 13 and 45 years old, running a handful of rigs, or even a single rig, in their bedrooms or basements. Many were hobbyists; a few were “get-rich-quick” dreamers, but virtually all of them were ideologically invested in the idea behind cryptocurrency. A substantial proportion, about 40%, viewed their initial cryptorig purchase as a stepping stone towards expanding their operation, aspiring to someday run dozens of rigs mining different cryptocurrencies.

Virtually everyone in this target segment was a cryptocurrency apostle[^2], in it for the long haul through booms and busts of crypto valuations. Ethan also knew these customers were knowledgeable and technologically minded. A majority of them were self-taught, relying on YouTube and Discord channels, Subreddits, WhatsApp and Telegram groups, and other more private means of learning about new technologies and sharing their experiences and opinions. They would not only appreciate the significant speed-boosting value of the TUSA software right away, but chances were high that they would disseminate their (hopefully positive) opinions through venues like the r/Cryptocurrency Subreddit. Whether these customers would be willing to pay more for the bundle, and if so, how much, was a separate challenge.

#### **The Competition**

As Rob Agosti had pointed out in the kick-off meeting, MBE was a formidable competitor in the low-end rig market that Tuli + TUSA would enter. Started around seven years ago by three ex-Goldman Sachs investment bankers turned crypto-bros, MBE had literally taken over the cryptorig market for enthusiasts. MBE’s Brom rig currently held about 60% of the market share in the core rig segment. In a head-to-head comparison conducted by Amber Thomas’ staff using a battery of technical tests, the Brom rig had performed at about the same level as the standalone Tuli rig (without TUSA).

From the outset, MBE had chosen operational efficiency as its core differentiator and built a lean operation from the ground up with every extra cost wrung out. Consequently, the Brom rig was priced at a lower price point in the marketplace than Kaval’s Tuli rig.

The rest of the competitors in this market were small operators in a fragmented space, selling a few dozen rigs annually, many of which were built from scratch or custom-assembled by the vendor. It was clear to Ethan that Agosti and Li considered MBE to be the primary or even the sole competitive threat to Tuli’s launch.;

#### **The TUSA Advantage**

It was clear to Ethan he needed to know the ins and outs of the two competing rigs. After reading and digesting numerous technical reports, Ethan learned that depending on the type of cryptocurrency being mined, the TUSA software boosted Tuli’s performance by up to four times relative to the rig’s baseline performance. This meant that the customer could achieve the same mining capability by buying one Tuli rig loaded with TUSA as buying four Brom rigs.

This high-level comparison was complicated by the fact that the 4X performance applied only to Bitcoin mining. For mining cryptocurrencies on the Ethereum blockchain, the performance upgrade was more like 2X (i.e., one Tuli + TUSA was equivalent to two Brom rigs), and for other esoteric cryptocurrencies known as altcoins, the performance improvement factor was even lower, roughly 1.5X. Based on secondary research, Ethan knew that a vast majority of crypto-enthusiasts mined Bitcoin and Ethereum, with considerable overlap: they would use their rig setup to mine both currencies, switching to one depending on prevailing prices. In contrast, less than 15% of the target segment mined altcoins.

Based on this assessment, Ethan was confident that his target customers would immediately recognize the economic value of the Tuli + TUSA bundle. The difficult challenge was how to quote a price that factored in the customers’ consideration of short- and long-term benefit mindset. What pricing strategy would work best?

### **“Free the Software”**

As part of its premium branding strategy, Agosti’s hardware division followed the approach of the parent server industry. Kaval focused on designing and making the best Cryptorig hardware, while the software loaded on them was seen as a distant secondary consideration or even someone else’s problem. “Our customers pay for the quality of our hardware, they expect not to get nickel-and-dimed by us,” Agosti had said. He meant that the software installed on Kaval’s cryptorigs would usually be offered to customers for free as a perk when buying the rig. After all, the entire server industry had embraced this pricing principle of “free the software” for the last two decades. In the past, however, the software installed on servers was relatively undifferentiated, and a lot of it was built on open-source platforms at relatively low cost. Ethan wondered if this principle applied to something as powerful, costly, and advantageous as the TUSA software.

***Cost-plus pricing.*** Even though it sold cutting-edge technology, the cryptorig industry had adopted rather traditional cost-plus pricing methods to set hardware prices in the past. Both Tuli & Verkko were priced using this approach. Agosti had already established a round price point of \$2,000 for the Tuli rig before Ethan came on board.

According to research collated from external consulting firms, Ethan learned that MBE had also consistently used cost-plus pricing for its products. Exhibit 2 provides relevant information about costs and prices for the Verkko, Tuli, and Brom rigs sourced from consultants.

“There are some obvious weaknesses with using cost-plus pricing here, aren’t there?” Ethan said to himself. “This seems like a perfect situation to use value-based pricing, but how will Agosti react? What will Feng Li and Tim Broward think?”

***Customer benefits.*** To Ethan, the benefits of bundling TUSA with the Tuli rig were obvious after that first meeting. However, there was a major complicating factor to consider, as TUSA’s benefits varied significantly depending on the type of cryptocurrency mining. Internal performance tests suggested that those mining Bitcoin and Ethereum saw the biggest jump in performance, others not so much (See Exhibit 3 for the results of these tests). Ethan recalled Feng Li’s observation that “our research shows that most of the customers buying the Tuli rig will use it for mining a single cryptocurrency, or at most two cryptocurrencies.”

Furthermore, Ethan realized that regardless of the pricing approach he recommended, potential customers would have to be educated about the direct and indirect savings they would enjoy from buying the Tuli + TUSA bundle: direct savings from the fact that they would have to buy fewer cryptorigs to achieve the same results, and indirect savings from the reduced costs of operation that would occur over the course of using the cryptorig with the enhanced software.

***The sales process.*** Although he was absent at the launch meeting, Ethan knew that Tim Broward, Kaval’s sales director, would play a significant role in price execution. Broward had been eagerly asking Ethan for updates about his pricing work regularly, and even offered to be a sounding board as Ethan fleshed out his thinking while developing the pricing strategy.

Traditionally, Kaval had used a relatively high-touch consultative sales approach when selling rigs to legacy cryptomining companies. Broward’s sales team was competent and experienced by any industry measure. Before joining the cryptocurrency industry, many of them had a decade or more of experience selling hardware servers for companies like HP and Dell. They were compensated with a roughly 65% salary and a 35% commission structure. Ethan’s pricing recommendation would matter to Kaval’s sales organization.

Ethan was certain he needed buy-in from Broward and his team for his pricing recommendation to be considered a success internally. They would be responsible for the entire sales process, from generating demand, communicating TUSA’s economic value to the market and positioning it relative to competition, dealing with customers’ objections to a potentially higher price, and following up on after-sales issues.

At the upcoming CRYPTOXPO trade show, Ethan would be the point person tasked with talking to prospects and demonstrating the Tuli + TUSA rig in Kaval’s booth. What he learned there would help Broward develop sales scripts and help Feng Li generate marketing collateral when approaching target customers. Therefore, it was imperative to have a clear picture of the bundle’s pricing approach beforehand.

As Ethan worked on the pricing strategy project, he was surprised that everyone seemed to want to chime in. Rob Agosti stopped by his desk more than once to check up on his progress and offer advice. He underscored how successful Kaval’s current pricing strategy with the Verkko rig has been each time. (“Is he pushing me to stick to the free software approach?” Ethan wondered. “And should I?”). Feng Li, Amber Thomas, and Tim Broward emailed him or talked to him at various times, trying to find out which way Ethan was leaning in his thinking about pricing the Tuli + TUSA offering. “This decision is clearly important to them, and it seems each of them has their own preferred ideas on what to do,” Ethan said to himself. “I need to make it clear to all of them that I have considered different pricing options carefully before landing on my recommendation.”

As a starting point, Ethan developed a detailed customer persona that he called “Alex Johnson,” along with a detailed needs specification document of what the target customer would look for when purchasing a cryptorig. This information is provided in Exhibit 3.

For his pricing calculations, Ethan assumed that Alex Johnson would purchase four core rigs and would be interested in minimizing his initial purchase costs (which would be financed through a loan) and the overall operating costs of his rig setup. Thus, a relevant consideration for the Alex Johnson persona would be the total first-order and second-order savings derived from purchasing a single Tuli rig (enhanced with TUSA) versus buying four Brom rigs. He also determined that, given the conservative nature of many of the senior managers involved in the decision making process, he needed to compare two Tuli+TUSA rigs to four core rigs.

Next, he made a list of four pricing approaches he would pursue in detail:

1.  **Legacy pricing approach**: Use the same approach as that used by Agosti and his predecessors; charge only for the hardware and give the TUSA software away to customers, eating its costs. In this case, TUSA’s role would be to work as a brand-builder and loyalty-enhancer.

2.  **Competition-based pricing**: Price the Tuli+TUSA rig at the same price as the “equivalent” competitor price: what the customer would pay for four Brom rigs.

3.  **Cost-based pricing**: Apply a cost-based approach to pricing the Tuli+TUSA rig by marking up the relevant costs. (Ethan’s assumptions for this approach are provided in Exhibit 4).

4.  **Value-based pricing**: Charge a price based on the differentiated economic value of the Tuli+TUSA rig to the target customer.

Finally, he considered what he would need for his upcoming pricing strategy meeting with the executive team. First, he needed a crystal-clear set of prices derived from the four methods. Second, he needed to go a step further and provide a clear recommendation for a single price he thought would be the best price to charge for the Tuli + TUSA rig, along with a rationale behind his recommendation. Third, he felt he should provide some ideas and specific guidance to the sales team to support executing his recommended price. Fourth, he needed to think through the rig bundle’s launch price strategy, considering whether to offer price promotions or use other approaches to introduce the product to the marketplace. And finally, he needed to consider the competitive ramifications of his price recommendation. Specifically, how would MBE react to the clear and present threat represented by Tuli + TUSA’s launch? And could something be done at the outset to minimize their aggressive response?

There was a lot of work to be done. Ethan knew that the product’s success and his career trajectory within Kaval rested on his pricing decision. He rolled up his sleeves and got to work, putting all thoughts of partying with the crypto crowd aside.

**Exhibit 4. Assumptions for Cost-Based Pricing Approach**

Ethan assumed that Kaval would want to recover the development costs of TUSA, \$6,000,000, over a period of three years. He also made a number of other assumptions to prepare his cost-based pricing model. They were as follows:

1.  The company would assemble a limited number of core cryptorigs due to manufacturing capacity constraints and corporate strategy. It would be able to sell all the rigs it could produce over the 2023-2025 time period. This assumption would need to be stress-tested for 2026 on.

2.  Kaval Cryptorig’s market share in the core rig category would be 4% in 2023, 9% in 2024, and 14% in 2025.

3.  TUSA would be attached to half the Tuli rigs shipped. The remaining half would be shipped as-is, without TUSA installed on them, for customers mining esoteric cryptocurrencies on other blockchains or using rigs for other computing purposes like running AI deep learning models.

4.  Kaval would want to apply a 30% markup rate on its costs, consistent with its business strategy.
