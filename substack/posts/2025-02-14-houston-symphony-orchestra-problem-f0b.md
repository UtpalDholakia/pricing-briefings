### **Case Purpose and Overview**

Broadly speaking, this case provides a practical application of incremental cost analysis for pricing decisions in a service industry setting. It uses the difficulty faced by the Houston Symphony Orchestra’s marketing manager to illustrate the nuances associated with using incremental costing for developing pricing structures. After analyzing this case, participants will be able to distinguish between costs that are incremental to the pricing decision and those that are irrelevant vs. average costs, learn how excess capacity, especially when it is perishable, creates compelling pricing opportunities, why prices below the *average cost* and sometimes even far below this value, can still be profitable, and how customer segmentation allows more complex pricing structures to be implemented and enables greater economic value to be extracted from the customer base.

**Learning Objectives**

By the end of the case discussion, participants should understand:

- The concept of incremental or relevant vs. non-incremental or irrelevant costs

- How to think through incremental analysis for pricing decisions

- How the economics of capacity utilization in service industries influences the efficacy of pricing strategies

- The counterintuitive relation between profit and price in such situations, a.k.a., very low prices can be very profitable prices.

### **Case Background & Current Situation**

The Houston Symphony Orchestra faces a common challenge in the performing arts that is quite common not just in this industry but in many other services settings as well. It carries the unenviable dual burden of high fixed costs and underutilized capacity. Let's first verify the financial baseline shown in the next table.

### **Financial Analysis Framework**

#### **Step 1: Current Situation Analysis**

In the case, we are told that the marketing manager would ideally like to sell out her entire theater capacity. If this happens, the symphony will make \$1,200 per performance as shown below.

**Capacity Performance (for 1,200 seats sold out):**

- Revenue: 1,200 × \$15 = \$18,000

- Total Costs: \$16,200

- Profit: \$1,800

However, the manager is only able to sell an average of 900 tickets per performance. As a result, as shown below, she actually loses \$2,175 per performance.

**Actual Performance (900 average attendance):**

- Revenue: 900 × \$15 = \$13,500

- Total Costs: \$15,675

- Loss: \$2,175 per performance

What’s more, because the capacity is perishable, these losses can’t be recouped after the fact, like product settings where price promotions, sales through second-tier channels, etc., could be used to recover costs if a SKU doesn’t perform according to expectations. A pair of shoes is available to sell until it is sold, a seat at a symphony performance is gone forever after the event. Next, let’s consider the costs given in the case and classify them into fixed and variable.

#### **Classifying costs for incremental analysis**

**Fixed Costs (per performance):**

- Fixed Overhead: \$1,500

- Rehearsal Costs: \$4,500

- Performance Costs: \$2,000

- Administrative Staff: \$800

- Marketing: \$1,200

- Conductor Fee: \$1,800

- Guest Soloist fee (when used): \$3,000 – here we’ve assumed no soloist.

- Sheet Music Licensing: \$500

- Instrument Maintenance: \$300

- Stage Setup/Teardown: \$400

- Utilities: \$500

- Venue Insurance: \$600

- **Total Fixed Costs: \$14,100**

**Variable Costs:**

- Per patron: \$1.00

- Program printing: \$0.75 per program

For 900 patrons: Variable costs = (900 × \$1.00) + (900 × \$0.75) = \$1,575 **Total costs for 900 patrons: \$14,100 + \$1,575 = \$15,675**

As you can see, classifying costs is tricky, and chances are, you may have classified some of them differently. An interesting question that often arises during the classification of costs for incremental analysis is whether a cost is fixed or variable, or more importantly, whether it is incremental or non-incremental to the pricing decision. Note that only incremental costs count and are to be included in the analysis.

As an example, if the conductor is paid a salary, irrespective of the number of performances, then their fee would be non-incremental to marketing programs involving adding more performances; however, if the conductor is paid on a per-performance basis, then their fee would be incremental to such decisions. The same thing applies to the soloist fee. Here, without additional information, I assume that the conductor is paid on a per-performance basis, and therefore, the conductor’s fee is an incremental cost for programs involving an additional performance. With different assumptions (or a different understanding of the conductor’s compensation), this categorization could very well change.

The same logic applies to other costs as well. Utilities can be treated as entirely fixed if they are paid as a part of a rental or leasing fee on a per-month basis without regard for the number of performances, whereas they will be treated as variable and therefore incremental if they are incurred on a per-performance or per-patron basis. Here, I assume that because additional performances will cost more to air condition, more water will be used in the restrooms, etc., I treat utilities as incremental to programs that involve adding performances but not to programs that maintain the number of performances.

As this discussion suggests, the key question to ask when considering whether any cost is incremental is whether it will be incurred if there is an additional performance. #### **Step 2: Program Assessment Using Incremental Analysis**

Next, let’s consider each program in a little detail, noting the inclusion and exclusion of different costs in the analysis.

***Program 1 – Student Ticket***

***Program Details:*** This program involves selling discounted \$8 student tickets an hour before the concert. The program expects no cannibalization to occur since it targets a different price-sensitive segment clearly.

Incremental revenue: 200 × \$8 = \$1,600

Incremental costs: 200 × (\$1.00 + \$0.75) = \$350

Incremental profit: \$1,600 - \$350 = \$1,250

The only incremental costs to be added here are the variable costs per patron and the costs of program printing. No other costs are incurred from this offer. Although presumably some outreach may be needed to publicize this promotion, no details are given, so we assume that the cost of marketing this offer is zero.

***Decision:*** This program brings in an extra \$1,250 in profit with very little additional expenditure, so it’s an easy yes. The key insight here is that even though the \$8 discount ticket price is less than half the average cost of \$17.42 per seat (\$15,675 ÷ 900), it is still highly profitable in incremental terms. The actual cost that matters is the incremental cost, which is only \$1.75 per ticket. Thus, a \$6, \$5, or, in theory, even a \$2 student discount ticket would be profitable in this scenario.

***Program 2 – Sunday Matinee***

***Program Details:*** This program involves putting on an additional Sunday matinee show and therefore incurs all the costs associated with an additional performance of the same program. It also generates a significantly higher number of ticket sales than the student ticket (700 vs. 200), but also leads to cannibalization (100 vs. 0).

Lost Saturday revenue from cannibalization: 150 × \$15 = \$2,250

New Sunday revenue from matinee: 700 × \$10 = \$7,000

Incremental revenue: \$7,000 - \$2,250 = \$4,750

The following incremental costs will be incurred from implementing this program

Additional performance costs: \$2,000

Stage setup/teardown: \$400

Conductor fee: \$1,800

Utilities for performance space: \$500

Variable costs for 700 patrons: 700 × \$1.75 = \$1,225

Total Incremental Costs: \$5,925

Note that in this calculation, we have to be judicious in including only costs that vary on a per-performance basis (e.g., stage setup/ teardown, conductor fee) but not costs that vary on a per-program basis, even if they look like per-performance costs (e.g., sheet music licensing). Also, some costs will simply not materialize because of infeasibility (e.g., there is no time to rehearse between a Saturday night show and the Sunday matinee show, nor is there time to work on maintaining instruments). Therefore,

Incremental Profit: \$4,750 - \$5,925 = -\$1,175

***Decision:*** Although it seems like a good idea to open up a new “market” in terms of Sunday mornings, the math doesn’t work here as given. The significant additional incremental costs make this idea unprofitable. Now, if the ticket prices were not reduced, but instead maintained at \$15, the story would be different. With everything else remaining unchanged, the \$15 matinee show would be profitable, earning \$2,325.

***Program 3 – New Series***

***Program Details:*** This idea involves adding an entire series of new programs, turning the Symphony from a biweekly to a weekly offering. This is clearly the most ambitious of the four programs, with the highest possibility of growing revenue and increasing the Symphony’s profile and influence among both patrons and the music community. On the negative side, this is also the most expensive of the programs in terms of additional costs. Let’s work out the financial impact of this program addition.

Price: \$15 per ticket

Expected sales: 800 tickets

Cannibalization: 100 would have attended the previous series shift to this one

Net new patrons: 700

Incremental Revenue: (800 – 100) × \$15 = \$10,500

Incremental Costs:

Performance costs excluding Fixed Overhead, Administrative, and Venue Insurance: \$11,200

Rehearsal Costs: \$4,500

Performance Costs: \$2,000

Marketing: \$1,200

Conductor Fee: \$1,800

Sheet Music Licensing: \$500

Instrument Maintenance: \$300

Stage Setup/Teardown: \$400

Utilities: \$500

Variable costs for 800 patrons: 800 × \$1.75 = \$1,400

Total Incremental Costs: \$12,600

Incremental Loss: \$10,500 - \$12,600 = -\$2,100

***Decision:*** Note that in this calculation, the only costs excluded are the fixed overhead costs, the administrative staff costs, and the venue insurance costs (assuming that the insurance is time-based, not number of performance-based). Every other performance cost needs to be added in because it is incremental to adding a brand new series. The result is that even though the revenue gain is substantial, the cost increase is even higher, resulting in a loss.

There is a lot of really powerful learning here. Marketers are always pursuing significant new opportunities like launching new products or brands, creating significant variations for new markets, all with the aim of making a big splash and fueling growth. The reality is that such endeavors are not only risky, but they are costly. If the focus is on the bottom line, the smartest marketing actions are often the low-cost, small actions that specifically pay attention to using up all available spare resources and generating additional value to customers. Even when the impact on revenue of such actions is relatively small, it can still have a major impact on profitability.

***Program 4 – VIP ticket***

***Program Details:*** This final program in the case has a similar orientation to the student ticket idea, with the difference that this is targeting the upper end of the customer base, whereas the student ticket targeted the lower end. In this case:

VIP ticket price: \$25 per ticket

Expected sales: 90 tickets

Cannibalization: 80 would have bought regular tickets

New patrons: 10

Additional cost per VIP patron: \$8

Lost Regular Revenue: 80 × \$15 = \$1,200

VIP Revenue: 90 × \$25 = \$2,250

Incremental Revenue: \$2,250 - \$1,200 = \$1,050

Incremental Costs:

VIP services for 90 patrons: 90 × \$8 = \$720

Variable costs for 90 patrons: 90 × \$1.75 = \$157.50

Total Incremental Costs: \$877.50

Incremental Profit: \$1,050 - \$877.50 = \$172.50

***Decision:*** This program is not as profitable as the student ticket, but it still is modestly profitable. Here, a new version of the experience is created at relatively low added cost, all of it variable, and pitched as a “VIP experience.” Sure, it doesn’t generate that much new interest, but what it does is extract more economic value from the Symphony’s current customers who are willing and able to spend more but haven’t previously had the opportunity to do so. Again, this fits our theme of finding low-incremental cost opportunities to grow and increase profitability.

#### **Key Learning Points**

This case is rather simple in working out the math, but it provides a number of extremely powerful lessons about pricing, marketing, and customer relationships. We will end this teaching note with five of them:

***1) Shifting to contribution margin thinking from average cost thinking.***

This case illustrates the importance of contribution margin thinking in pricing decisions. Traditional “average cost” pricing that is embraced by many managers would suggest that pricing any ticket below \$17.42 (the current average cost per patron of \$15,675 divided by 900 attendees) would be unprofitable and a bad thing to do. However, incremental analysis actually reveals that any price above the variable cost of \$1.75 per patron contributes to covering fixed costs and improving overall profitability. This is why the student discount program at \$8 per ticket generates \$1,250 in incremental profit despite being priced at less than half the average cost and is the most profitable of the four programs considered.

The key insight is that for businesses that have proportionally high fixed costs and low variable costs, volume and capacity utilization become crucial drivers of profitability. In such cases, we are always trying at the periphery to sell every ounce of excess capacity, even if that involves very low prices. And we are always concerned about minimizing cannibalization so that the core paying regular prices doesn’t migrate to the periphery’s low prices. Each additional patron above the variable cost threshold helps absorb the substantial fixed costs that must be incurred for the performance.

***2) Segmenting customers and extracting the most value***

The case also provides a classic example of how different customer segments exhibit varying price sensitivities, value perceptions, and willingness to pay, creating opportunities for price discrimination. Students represent a price-sensitive segment willing to attend at \$8 but unlikely to pay \$15, while VIP customers demonstrate a willingness to pay premium prices for a special experience. This point gets at the heart of why relatively complex pricing structures are always more effective than too-simple ones.

Such segmentation allows the organization to capture more consumer surplus than would be possible with uniform pricing. The student program taps into an entirely new market segment without cannibalizing existing revenue, while the VIP experience successfully “trades up” existing customers to higher-value offerings. Effective segmentation requires understanding not just price sensitivity, but also different value propositions that resonate with each segment, timing of purchase decisions, and the ability to prevent arbitrage between segments.

***3) Paying attention to cannibalization***

When we use this core-periphery approach to incremental costs, we must pay attention to cannibalization effects when making any marketing move, especially when it is periphery-focused. In this case, the Sunday matinee program provided an excellent illustration of how cannibalization can significantly impact the profitability of seemingly attractive initiatives. While the program would generate 700 ticket sales, 150 of these represented customers switching from the higher-priced Saturday performances, reducing the net revenue gain.

However, not all cannibalization is detrimental, as shown in the VIP ticket case, where trading up existing customers from \$15 to \$25 tickets generated positive incremental revenue despite serving very few new customers. The key learning is that the pricing strategist should focus on calculating the net incremental impact rather than focusing solely on gross revenue generation. While this case didn’t really cover this issue, organizations must also consider whether the cannibalization will be a one-time deal or long-term and whether new offerings might permanently shift patterns of customer behavior.

***4) The economics of capacity utilization***

Service industries with perishable inventory face unique economic pressures that provide both challenges and opportunities for pricing strategy. At every performance, the Symphony’s empty seats represent lost revenue that will never be recovered. Consequently, capacity utilization is a critical performance driver. A lot of marketing activity, and by implication, pricing strategy, is geared towards maximizing capacity utilization smartly. In this case, the Sunday matinee program sought to utilize unused capacity in one way by adding performances when the venue would otherwise sit empty.

This is similar to an upscale restaurant opening for happy hour or offering lunch on certain days of the week. However, we found that the additional Sunday performance still required significant incremental fixed costs such as conductor fees and utilities, which eroded the benefits of increased capacity utilization. This highlights the importance of distinguishing between truly variable costs and costs that step up with additional service delivery. Organizations must carefully evaluate whether excess capacity can be monetized profitably or whether the incremental costs of utilizing capacity exceed the additional revenue earned.

***5) Need for strategic considerations beyond financial metrics***

While incremental analysis provides a clear guide to the profitability (or lack thereof) of new programs, successful pricing strategies must also consider broader strategic implications. For instance, in this case, the VIP ticket, beyond generating incremental profit, can enhance the symphony's brand positioning and create stronger emotional connections with high-value patrons. Similarly, student pricing locks in customers with high future potential, contributing to future audiences, and also demonstrates community service, potentially generating longer-term loyalty and positive word-of-mouth marketing. These programs also require careful operational consideration, as multiple pricing tiers can increase complexity in marketing, sales processes, and customer service delivery, and open the doors for potential mistakes. Organizations must consider their capability to execute sophisticated pricing strategies effectively while maintaining service quality across their diverse customer base.
