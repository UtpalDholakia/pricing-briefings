##### ***Summary:*** In August 2024, Crumbl replaced its simpler loyalty program, which returned 10% of a customer’s spending as store credit, with a more complex tiered points-based program. On its face, the new program appeared more generous because each dollar spent earned customers 10 times as many points. However, the brand also revamped its point-redemption menu, changing the discounts Crumbl’s loyalty program members received at redemption. In this post, I argue that the result was a *regressive redemption ladder*, whereby customers received less economic value per point as they saved more points to attain larger rewards. Analogous changes in loyalty programs at Dunkin’ and Starbucks hint at this pricing strategy’s efficacy. By converting straightforward percentage rebates into complex proprietary currencies, brands can manage earning rates, redemption ratios, customer tiers, expiration duration, and product eligibility, using these multiple levers to raise prices in precise ways that remain hidden from customers.

“The real price of every thing, what every thing really costs to the man who wants to acquire it, is the toil and trouble of acquiring it.” - Adam Smith, *Wealth of Nations*, 1776.

When Hannah Dorman was in high school, she and her friends drove 45 minutes each way to the nearest Crumbl store nearly every week, sometimes even in poor winter weather. These cookie quests began in their junior year, and Crumbl became so closely associated with Hannah that two boys asked her out using Crumbl cookies, once for junior prom and once for senior homecoming. A Spanish exchange student who joined her friend group sent Hannah Crumbl’s weekly announcements years later after returning home. Checking the new lineup was one of her favorite parts of Mondays (when new cookie line-ups are released), and the weekly drive gave Hannah and her friends a regular reason to spend time together. They have since moved away from their hometown to go to different colleges, but they still reminisce about their various Crumbl experiences[^1].

### **Crumbl and its Loyalty Program**

Crumbl is a privately-owned dessert chain founded in Logan, Utah, in 2017. It currently operates approximately 1,100 stores, almost all of them owned by franchisees. Its cookie-centered menu features six desserts that change weekly (plus several that don’t), and they’re announced on social media every Sunday evening. The weekly rotation began as a practical way to manage a small store kitchen, but it soon became the company’s primary differentiator and an important reason customers returned regularly. Because the company’s business depends heavily on repeat purchases, its loyalty program is critical to its success.

Until August 2024, Crumbl’s loyalty program was straightforward, very similar to those of many other QSR operators. Program members received 1 Loyalty Crumb for every dollar they spent online or in a Crumbl store. When they accumulated 100 Crumbs, they could redeem them for \$10 in store credit, a.k.a. Crumbl Cash. Thus, the old version of the program essentially offered a 10% rebate to loyalty program members[^2].

Crumbl’s loyalty program, pre-August 2024

### The August 2024 Redesign

In August 2024, the company revamped its program, introducing a tiered version with four status levels, named Bronze, Silver, Gold, and Pink. As customers spend more, they progress through these levels[^3]. Since the revamp and as of this writing, Bronze customers earn ten Crumbs for every dollar spent, Silver customers earn eleven, Gold customers twelve, and Pink customers thirteen (see the image below). Furthermore, attaining higher levels provides additional benefits such as a free cookie on one’s birthday, the ability to vote for flavors, gift packaging, and early ordering with a discount. Accrued Crumbs expire a year after they are earned, while tier status is evaluated separately and resets at the beginning of the year.[^4]

Compared with the previous rate of one Crumb per dollar, the new program appears to offer a substantial increase in rewards. The customer can exchange 500 Crumbs for a single cookie, 2,000 Crumbs for a 4-pack, 3,000 for a 6-pack, and 6,000 Crumbs for a 12-pack. At first glance, these requirements seem reasonable when viewed in terms of cookie count, indicating that each additional cookie is worth an even 500 Crumbs.[^5]

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/760026bd-50a4-4777-b6f8-2f99798098df_1280x645.png)

Crumbl’s loyalty program, August 2026

Now consider a customer who has accumulated 6,000 Crumbs. To redeem that entire balance, the customer can either get a 12-pack @ 6,000 Crumbs or redeem 12 single cookies (over time or all at once) @ 500 Crumbs per cookie, or buy other combinations of offerings. What is important here is that the customer does not receive any quantity discount; both methods yield 12 “free” cookies and cost the same number of Crumbs. This is not the case for purchases. If this same customer were to purchase the cookies instead of redeeming Crumbs, they would pay different prices, as shown below. For purchases, the customer, not surprisingly, gets a significant discount of just over 20% when buying in quantity.[^6]

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/26f571b8-9451-4ccf-aa6b-0b2799c2efe1_1520x353.png)

Crumbs required and retail price of a single cookie and a 12-pack at my local Houston Crumbl store, August 2026.

Thus, purchasing Crumbl with money provides a quantity discount, but redeeming accrued Crumbs yields no quantity discount. A customer who saves up Crumbs to get the larger reward of a 12-pack gives up just over 20 percent of the value that the same accumulated Crumbs would have given them with incremental one-cookie-at-a-time redemption. Note that the specific values will depend on store-level prices, as individual stores have some discretion in setting them. However, the larger point applies everywhere: when a 12-pack costs less than 12 individual cookies when purchased with dollars, but exactly the same when purchased with Crumbs, the larger buy loses value when redeeming Crumbs.

Even many regular, smart Crumbl customers may miss this significant devaluation because it is so subtle, not to mention counterintuitive. The reward earning rate (11 Crumbs per dollar, 12 Crumbs per dollar, etc.) is far more visible to customers. Not only was it the focus of publicity when Crumbl revamped the program, but customers have also been receiving frequent emails and in-app notifications since then, showing the Crumbs they’ve earned with each transaction and their running balance. However, the more consequential change of the revamp was somewhat hidden from customers’ view and occurred on the redemption side. By replacing a straightforward Crumb-to-dollar conversion (100 Crumbs = \$10) with a more complex conversion ratio (each menu item = a specific number of Crumbs), Crumbl essentially created a proprietary, flexible, and relatively opaque currency within its rewards program. It could now change the redemption rates for each menu item separately and increase or decrease the value of its proprietary currency with this new complex loyalty program.

When launching the revamped program, Crumbl touted it as a way to offer more rewards and recognize its most dedicated customers. That characterization was partially accurate, in that some redemptions were indeed more favorable to customers. The next table compares the spending required to earn a single cookie and a 12-pack under the two programs. In the first row, the old program values are economic spend equivalents based on its 10% rebate, and the prices used are again those at my local Houston store. The figures for the revamped program show the customer spending needed to accumulate the required Crumbs at each status level.

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/9787b473-438f-4b50-b9d8-e70fd1ce5226_1831x815.png)

As the table shows, the revamped program generally required customers to spend more to earn rewards, indicative of a devaluation of Crumbs. The exceptions were Gold and Pink status customers for single-cookie redemptions, which required lower spend levels than before. For the 12-pack reward, customers at every tier had to spend significantly more than before. The graduated structure itself is reasonable, since a program of this kind is expected to deliver greater rewards at higher status levels.

This is best understood as a segment-specific (or really, tier-specific) price increase. The original loyalty program did not distinguish customers by how much they spent at Crumbl. In the revamped version, Crumbl effectively raised prices selectively by using two dimensions: customer tier and reward size. It targeted price increases so that its best customers saw the smallest increases (and even price decreases in the two instances described above). In terms of reward size, counterintuitively, greater percentage price increases were observed for the 12-pack than for the single-cookie reward. The loyalty program moved from a simple to a complex pricing structure, making it much harder to figure out the economic value of a Crumb, as well as the conversion ratio of exactly how much of a rebate they were getting from Crumbl.

### Two concurrent price lists and the regressive redemption ladder

Under the new program, Crumbl’s loyalty members were given two related but different price schedules. The first was the regular menu expressed in dollars and cents, which included prices for individual cookies and boxes, lower per-cookie prices for larger quantities, local price variations, promotions, and product upcharges. The second was the rewards menu, which specified how many Crumbs members needed to redeem for each eligible product.

The rewards menu was not a one-to-one alternative to the regular menu. Only loyalty members could use it, they needed to have sufficient Crumbs, and only selected products could be purchased with Crumbs. However, the rewards menu did perform the primary function of a price list by assigning separate point-denominated redemption prices to a single cookie, a four-pack, a six-pack, and a dozen. Members with sufficient Crumbs, therefore, had to compare the Crumb price of each reward with its corresponding dollar price to determine what their accumulated points were worth.

This was a significant departure from the old program. Previously, Crumbl Cash had a fixed dollar value and could be applied to products priced on the regular menu, so it automatically preserved the quantity discounts and price relationships already built into that menu. In this case, there was a single price list, and Crumbs simply applied a fixed 10% discount to any purchase.

When Crumbl redesigned the program, it replaced store credit awards with product-specific redemption offers. This led to a decoupling of prices between the regular and rewards menus and essentially gave Crumbl double the degrees of freedom to change prices as before. The regular menu had a quantity discount built into its pricing (buying larger packs led to a lower price per cookie), but on the rewards menu, every incremental cookie cost the same 500 Crumbs. This change resulted in a rewards program with a “regressive redemption ladder,” in which the retail value received by the loyalty program member per Crumb declines as more Crumbs are redeemed for a single purchase.

Although there may be a natural impulse to say that Crumbl is taking advantage of its customers, this may not be the case. Rather, it is very likely that the choice to set a flat 500 Crumb price for a cookie is driven more by a need to keep things simple for communication and accounting purposes than a deliberate attempt to exploit heavy buyers. However, the problem is that loyalty programs, by and large, encourage customers to accumulate points toward larger rewards, and for customers, saving points creates a sense of progress and gives them a reason to return. In Crumbl’s revamped program, instead of being rewarded, such behavior is penalized. Research on loyalty programs suggests this is a common issue. A study comparing close to 7,000 rewards across six programs found that customers were effectively charged more when paying with the loyalty currency than when paying for the same items with cash.[^7]

### Other features of the revamped Crumbl loyalty program

Two other features of the redesigned Crumbl loyalty program deserve attention: the larger number of Crumbs earned per dollar spent and the introduction of status levels. Under the old program, a customer earned one Crumb for every dollar spent, whereas with the new program, the customer earns between ten and thirteen Crumbs per dollar, depending on status. Point balances therefore grow more than 10X faster, even as redemption rates are stingier than 1/10X, resulting in substantially less generous benefits than before.

This change in the scale of points earned can affect how customers evaluate the program. Research on “illusionary progress” has studied what can happen when a loyalty program increases both the number of points customers earn and the number required for a reward (just like Crumbl). In such cases, consumers do not fully adjust for both changes, particularly when the relationship between earning rewards and redeeming them is difficult to work out.[^8] The higher earning rate is immediately visible, while the increase in the number of points required for redemption may receive less attention (and be harder to calculate). Consequently, 13 Crumbs per dollar sounds more generous than one Crumb per dollar, even though the customer cannot assess the actual value of the change without also knowing that a single cookie now requires 500 Crumbs, where it only required 45 Crumbs before, or that each menu item (e.g., 4-pack, 12-pack) has its own redemption requirement, or that the rate of earning Crumbs varies by one’s status.

Research on medium maximization is also relevant here. This research has found that consumers may start focusing on collecting the intermediate currency itself, even when accumulating more of it does not actually improve the underlying results that the currency is intended to provide them.[^9] Thus, a growing point balance gives the customer tangible evidence that they are making progress and results in a sense of accomplishment, regardless of whether the balance has more purchasing power than it did under the previous program.

In the Crumbl revamp, the introduction of Bronze, Silver, Gold, and Pink status levels gave its program members two measures of progress to track. They can watch their Crumb balance grow (and are frequently reminded via emails and in-app notifications of their running balance) while also tracking their progress toward reaching the next tier, which offers a higher earning rate and additional benefits. Holding amount spent constant, a Silver customer earns Crumbs more quickly than a Bronze customer, a Gold customer even faster with more benefits, and a Pink customer earns the largest number of Crumbs per dollar. Even though these benefits are clearly spelled out, it’s still not clear to customers whether the new program is more or less generous than the previous one.

Status levels also make it much harder to determine the aggregate economic effect of the program’s redesign. Where the old program had a single earnings rate, the revamped one has 4 tier-based rates and also introduces product-specific redemption requirements, a 1-year expiration rule for accrued credits, and other status benefits whose economic value is hard for customers to figure out. Again, I must reiterate that there’s no evidence that Crumbl introduced tiers or other program features to make it harder for its customers to use the program or to harm them in any way. In fact, this program revamp is entirely consistent with my assertion about pricing best practice: *moderately complex price structures are far more effective for the business than simple ones.*

### Multiple exchange rates affect the redemption behavior of loyalty program members

The structure of the redemption menu may also influence whether customers use their Crumbs at all. When the conversion ratio is fixed, customers have little incentive to delay redeeming rewards. For instance, if 100 points always convert to \$10 in store credit, the points will have the same value today as next month. A complex redemption menu replaces the single conversion rate with several item-specific conversion rates. Now, the customer must do the additional cognitive work of comparing the number of points required for each item with its regular dollar price to determine which redemption offers the best value. Given what is known about consumers’ [cognitive miser mindset](https://psycnet.apa.org/record/2021-35834-003), most will be unlikely to make these calculations across the entire menu. Consistent with this idea, recent research has found that customers are less likely to redeem points when exchange rates vary across different rewards in a set, even when the average dollar value per point is held constant. This is because exchange rate volatility leads many consumers to believe that a better use for their points may become available, prompting them to retain their balances and pay with cash instead.[^10]

The managerial implication is that different exchange rates for different menu items can lower the company’s near-term reward redemption costs by delaying customers’ redemption activity and increasing the likelihood that more points will expire unused than before. This does not necessarily mean that every delayed customer redemption benefits the company; after all, the customer may redeem later, well before their points expire. The key insight is that compared to a fixed rate, a variable exchange rate gives the issuer greater influence over both the value of the reward and the timing of its use.

This issue is especially relevant in Crumbl’s, because the larger rewards on its rewards menu have a worse exchange rate than smaller rewards. For instance, a customer who postpones redeeming 500 Crumbs for a single cookie to accumulate enough for a 12-pack will receive a lower dollar value per Crumb. Thus, even though common sense suggests that the customer should wait and accumulate more Crumbs because the resulting larger reward will be more valuable, Crumbl’s rewards menu does the exact opposite. It penalizes the customer for delaying temptation and saving their Crumbs until they have enough to earn a 6-pack or a 12-pack of cookies. Furthermore, the fact that accumulated Crumbs expire in a year after they are earned poses additional risk to customers; those who wait too long may see their unused Crumbs expire. To summarize, Crumbl’s redesigned loyalty program encourages members to accumulate Crumbs by spending more at its stores, but it isn’t structured to make that accumulation lead to better rewards for them.

### The same pattern is reprised in revamped loyalty programs at Dunkin’ and Starbucks

Other QRS programs have followed a similar trend in recent years. For instance, Dunkin’s old DD Perks program awarded 5 points per dollar spent. A free beverage was 200 points, implying a drink earned after \$40 of spending.[^11] When Dunkin’ Rewards replaced DD Perks in October 2022, its members now earned 10 points per dollar. Dunkin’ emphasized that rewards began at 150 points, which customers could reach after spending only \$15. However, the 150-point reward only provided smaller items, such as 3-count Munchkins or a Cold Foam drink topping. Beverages required between 600 and 950 points, depending on the category. The effective spending requirement for a drink has risen from \$40 to \$60-\$95 as of this writing.[^12]

As shown in the photos above, Dunkin’s redesigned loyalty program was very similar to Crumbl. On the plus side, customers could now redeem points for food and get something for even small balances. On the negative side, though, customers who had previously used their points for higher-priced beverages had to spend significantly more at Dunkin’ to earn the same reward. Also like Crumbl, Dunkin’ introduced “Boosted Status,” which raised the rate of earning points from 10 to 12 points per dollar for customers who completed 12 qualifying visits in a calendar month. Here again, we see the concept of rewarding the brand’s best customers. Dunkin’ also added a 12-month expiration period for points earned, further reducing the overall value of the rewards to customers.

Starbucks provides yet another QSR example of a loyalty program redesign. In February 2023, the company doubled the number of stars needed for a brewed coffee, tea, and small bakery items in its loyalty program from 50 to 100 Stars. The stars needed for handcrafted beverages and hot breakfast rose from 150 to 200 Stars, while lunch items rose from 200 to 300 stars. At the same time, iced coffee and tea fell from 150 to 100 Stars, and packaged coffee fell from 400 to 300.[^13]

This was another targeted price change. In Crumbl’s case, the price change targeted segments or tiers; in Starbucks’ case, it targeted menu items. The most popular rewards became more expensive, while some low-marginal-cost menu items became cheaper. Because of this variation, no one could claim that Stars had devalued across the board, even though most members experienced devaluation most of the time. Like Crumbl, Starbucks added tiers to its loyalty program in March 2026. It now has Green, Gold, and Reserve levels, with more Stars earned at higher status levels, and places explicit dollar caps on product rewards. One hundred Stars cover eligible products worth up to \$6, 200 Stars cover up to \$10, 300 Stars cover up to \$16, and 400 Stars cover up to \$20.[^14]

The upshot is that the dollar caps generate different exchange rates. At the maximum permitted value, the 100-Star reward translates into a conversion ratio of 16.7 Stars per dollar, the 200-Star reward to 20 stars per dollar, the 300-Star reward to 18.75 stars per dollar, and the 400-Star reward 20 stars per dollar, resulting in an uneven rather than a monotonically regressive structure. Just like Crumbl and Dunkin’, with its revamped loyalty program, Starbucks controls the earning rate, status threshold, eligible product categories, point requirements, and maximum dollar value thresholds, providing it numerous levers to manage price and target its incentives finely.

### Tiering in loyalty programs does not require a variable, opaque currency

Subway’s 2023 loyalty program redesign provides a useful contrast with those of Crumbl, Dunkin’, and Starbucks. With its redesign, Subway introduced status levels without making the value of its points depend on the menu item selected for redemption. Specifically, when Subway replaced its MyWay Rewards program with a new loyalty program called MVP Rewards in September 2023, it created three tiers: Pro, Captain, and All-Star. Analogous to Crumbl’s rewards structure, Subway’s Pro members earned 10 points per dollar, Captain members earned 11 points per dollar, and All-Star members earned 12 points per dollar. Customers in higher tiers also received some additional benefits, as shown in the figure below.[^15]

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/87050656-d84c-4e4f-b71f-4e3a064ab789_554x523.png)

Subway MVP Rewards, earning details by tier

In this program, the redemption still remained straightforward. 400 points could be exchanged for \$2 in Subway Cash (implying a .5 ₵/ point conversion rate), which could then be applied as store credit to purchase anything from the restaurant’s menu. Such a structure makes the incentives earned by each tier relatively easy to calculate. At 10 points per dollar, a Pro member earned a base rebate of 5%, while the Captains earned 5.5% and All-Stars earned 6%. Unlike the other three programs considered here, in Subway’s program, tiers changed the rate at which customers accumulated value, while the redemption rate of points to store credit remained constant.

Based on the discussion so far, we can distinguish between two design factors of QSR loyalty programs that are often conflated or ignored completely. We will call these factors “status complexity” and “redemption complexity.” A loyalty program’s status complexity refers to the idea that customers earn rewards or receive benefits at different rates or levels depending on their tier or status within the program. A program with low status complexity gives the same rewards to everyone, whereas one with higher complexity introduces more tiers, with different reward rates for each tier. Greater status complexity implies that the brand pays closer attention to customers’ behavior and distinguishes them by how much they spend.

The second factor, redemption complexity, refers to how straightforward or complicated it is for a program member to redeem rewards. To the extent that redemption values vary across products or when other conditions are attached to redemption, the program has greater redemption complexity. When Subway revamped its loyalty program in 2023, it increased the status complexity of its new program but maintained low redemption complexity. On the other hand, Crumbl, Dunkin’, and Starbucks raised the complexity of their respective revamped programs by introducing status-based earning rates and product-specific reward menus.

One important takeaway from understanding these two factors is that tiers or status levels, in and of themselves, do not create a second distinct price schedule, separate from menu prices. The second schedule can only be created when the loyalty program adopts greater redemption complexity. Neither type of complexity is good in and of itself; the levels of both status and redemption complexity need to align with the goals the brand’s managers want to achieve through their loyalty program.

Recently, Subway has further simplified its loyalty program in many U.S. regions. It currently offers a Sub Club program that provides all members with rewards at a rate of 10 points per dollar while preserving the conversion of 400 points to \$2 of Subway Cash. This change removed the status levels that the MyWay program had, while retaining a common redemption rate. In its current form, Subway’s program resembles Crumbl’s original program with low status complexity and low redemption complexity.

### **From regressive redemption to dynamic redemption**

As discussed earlier, Crumbl’s reward redemption rates are regressive, but they remain fixed until the company changes them. In fact, in the two years since the program was revamped, they haven’t changed at my local store. A single cookie has a published Crumb price, as do its 4-pack, 6-pack, and 12-pack, allowing program members to compare the rewards menu with the dollar menu when deciding whether to pay with money or Crumbs. Notably, the redemption rates for these products haven’t changed from the time Crumbl revamped its loyalty program to the time of this writing. But we only need to look beyond QSR, to travel loyalty programs, to see how a proprietary currency can be structured to add an additional layer of complexity to the loyalty program and make it even harder for members to make buying decisions.

Consider the case of Marriott Bonvoy, which uses what it calls “[flexible point redemption rates](https://help.marriott.com/s/article/flexible-point-redemption-rates)” for hotel stays. The number of points required to book a hotel stay corresponds to the hotel’s nighly rate and varies with availability and seasonality. Stays in hotels with greater room availability will require fewer points, while stays in busy destinations and on busy dates will require more. What’s more, the point requirements for booking a stay can fluctuate on a daily basis.

This approach of loyalty rewards redemption is different from Crumbl’s rewards catalog because a particular hotel stay does not have a single, fixed price in rewards points. The number of points required depends on the property, travel date, availability, and the rate in effect at the time the member books. On its website, Marriott displays both the price in dollars and cents and the price in points as payment options. Unlike Crumbl, there is no stable exchange rate between points and dollars.

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/e629f257-edeb-4cf6-8662-4718aae30f4a_1318x1038.png)

Marriott Bonvoy’s redemption & dollar rates for a September 2026 booking in New York City’s Lexington Hote (Marriott.com)

Note that this so-called dynamic redemption policy is not devaluation. Point requirements can and do decline during periods of lower demand, or for less popular hotels, giving members who are flexible access to better values. But it does make it a much more challenging and effortful decision making process when redeeming Marriott Bonvoy points when compared to redeeming Crumbs or Subway points. A Subway member with 4,000 points can easily figure out that their point balance represents \$20 in Subway Cash. A Crumbl member with 4,000 Crumbs faces a more difficult calculation. They must decide whether to buy a single cookie or a larger pack, and compare the Crumb requirement with the corresponding menu price. However, a Marriott Bonvoy member has to go much further than either one: identifying the location and date first, then considering different properties and the point and dollar prices for available rooms across those properties. Even then, after a lot of back-and-forth and calculations, they may still not be able to tell whether they are getting the best value for their points with their decision.

To my knowledge, Crumbl or other QSR operators, such as Dunkin’ and Starbucks, have not adopted any form of dynamic redemption pricing. However, if a QSR operator were to consider adopting dynamic pricing as a pricing strategy anywhere in its business, a natural place to begin would be with its rewards program. From being able to change the redemption ratios for various menu items for its loyalty program members, dynamic pricing would be a logical extension of that discretion. It would only be a small additional step, for example, to set reward redemption at 10% or 20% lower on weekends (for Crumbl) or during rush hours (for Dunkin’ and Starbucks), and to raise it back to baseline levels at other times.

### Four factors to evaluate loyalty program changes

As I’ve argued in this post, when evaluating a redesigned loyalty program, customers and industry observers often compare the old and new rates at which rewards are earned while paying less attention to changes in redemption rates. However, a complete assessment of a loyalty program’s redesign must take the entire customer journey into account, including how rewards are earned, where they can be used, how long before they expire, and their redemption rates. As we’ve seen, a redesigned loyalty program often increases the number of points awarded per dollar to create the illusion of generosity while simultaneously raising the redemption bar, removing quantity discounts, or shifting value away from the rewards customers use most often and toward those with more attractive cost structures for the business. These effects don’t necessarily harm customers, but usually favor one group over others. In evaluating a loyalty program’s redesign and understanding its repercussions for customers, the following four factors should be considered.

**1) The reward’s denomination and conversion methods.** Like cash, fixed-value store credit (e.g., 100 Crumbs = \$10) can be converted back and forth easily. A 10% rebate means that every dollar spent gives the customer 10 cents to spend later on anything from the company’s menu. However, a proprietary loyalty program currency that converts spending to points at purchase, and then separately converts points to purchases at redemption, does not have such a referent. The company has complete discretion in determining both conversion rates and can use them to its advantage.

**2) The net incentive given for using rewards.** Here, the customer must do some math (reward’s dollar value/amount spent to earn it) to figure out exactly how much of a discount they are getting when using their rewards points, and consider whether this depends on their prior spending with the brand. Such a calculation will prevent a higher spending-to-points rate from distorting an assessment of the program’s generosity and reveal potential regression in the redemption ladder, if any. This is not necessarily a bad thing, but from the company’s perspective, having a regressive redemption ladder increases the risk of customer backlash, for example, if it is noticed and publicized on social media.

**3) Comparison of the rewards redemption prices to regular prices.** Here, the key question is whether the loyalty program currency preserves the quantity discounts, bundles, promotions, and relative product prices available to customers paying with cash. As discussed earlier, as of this writing, Crumbl’s rewards schedule fails this parity test because a 12-pack costs 12 times as much as a single cookie in Crumbs, while the regular menu gives customers a 20% discount. This comparison will indicate that in Crumbl’s case, the points menu has an independent price structure; it is not a simple translation of the regular menu prices into Crumbs.

**4) Which customers win and which customers lose?** With all four QSR redesigns I have covered here (although Subway has recently reverted to a non-tiered program), introducing tiered programs privileges the operator’s heaviest spenders while making it less generous to occasional customers. Another related issue is changes in specific redemption rates. In many loyalty program redesigns, as complexity is introduced on the redemption side, smaller redemptions, or redemptions for lower-marginal-cost items, are more attractive while increasing the effective price of larger ones. Finally, the analysis should identify which variables of the loyalty program the company can now alter. This may include earning rates (e.g., points per dollar spent), status thresholds (e.g., spending required to move to a higher tier), the conversion rate of points to dollars, which products can be purchased with points, how long points last before expiring, and maximum reward thresholds. Depending on how these variables are set, the program can become more or less attractive for customers, perceptually and economically.

Furthermore, considering these factors allows us to distinguish between three issues that are often conflated by loyalty program designers: (1) the program’s overall generosity[^16] before and after its redesign; (2) the distribution of program generosity across customers; and (3) the authority and flexibility retained by the company to modify the program in the future. In a nutshell, there are trade-offs associated with making a loyalty program more complex. Adding different earning and redemption rates for different items naturally allows the company to provide more precise incentives to individual customers and manage the cost of offering these rewards more effectively. On the flip side, the individual’s customer’s economic return becomes much harder to calculate, and the future (or even current) value of their accumulated points becomes more opaque to translate into dollars and cents. What this means is that a loyalty program should be assessed as a complete price structure, in and of itself, rather than as a collection of promotional benefits.

### **Conclusion**

Many managers treat loyalty programs as promotional programs, viewing them as a way to maintain relationships with existing customers by incurring the costs of operating them and offering incentives. But in reality, they are an integral part of a company’s pricing strategy, determining the effective price paid by a significant fraction of its customers across a sequence of purchases. They establish the incentives given to customers through rules governing earning, redemption, eligibility, and expiration, and are an effective way to customize and manage realized prices in consumer settings.

As we’ve seen through the various, mostly QSR examples in this piece, the company decides how simple or complex to make the loyalty program. Like other areas of pricing strategy, QSR operators seem to favor greater complexity (although, as it stands, Subway is an exception here), giving them the leeway to offer more targeted incentives to specific customer groups, and the flexibility to raise or lower prices selectively and indirectly without changing prices on their menus. This flexibility can also be used for other related goals, such as rewarding valuable customers, encouraging particular purchases, and managing redemption costs. The core pricing challenge in designing effective loyalty programs is to preserve a reasonably clear connection between the customer’s spending and the value they eventually receive, even as the company uses the loyalty program to influence their future behavior.
