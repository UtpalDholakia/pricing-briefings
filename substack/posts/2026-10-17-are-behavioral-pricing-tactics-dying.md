##### ***Summary**. Behavioral pricing rests on a fundamental premise that has required no questioning until now: that the buyer is a human governed by psychological principles that can be studied and understood. But this premise is starting to look shaky as AI agents take over consumer decision making and, increasingly, complete the purchase. A popular perspective holds that these agents will be entirely rational comparison shoppers and that pricing will simply become a battle to the bottom. In this post, I find that this view is partially correct. In two experiments, I tested four leading AI models as shopping agents on two common behavioral pricing effects: the 9-price ending and partitioned pricing. The agents ignored the left digit entirely, correctly added up partitioned prices, and preferred a single all-inclusive price when the totals were equal. A one-cent price difference was enough for the machine buyer to choose the lower-priced product every single time. More intriguingly, the product’s position in the list was decisive in every case where the two options were comparable, and one AI model paid \$2 more for the same purchase depending on where the product appeared on the page. These findings indicate that machine buyers are rational about prices but not so much about the pricing context. Because a handful of model providers and platforms control the rules governing choice and can change them with a software update, brands and retailers are likely to lose control over how they leverage buyer psychology as machine buyers proliferate. Behavioral pricing tactics may indeed be on life support!*

### **The Rise of the Machine Buyer**

Without a doubt, machine buyers are rising rapidly. Over the past year, most major technology platforms have built and implemented infrastructure for AI agents to shop and make purchases (including payments) on a consumer’s behalf. For instance, in September 2025, OpenAI [launched Instant Checkout](https://openai.com/index/buy-it-in-chatgpt/) in ChatGPT, initially for US-based Etsy sellers, allowing users to make a purchase without leaving the chat. In January 2026, Google announced its [Universal Commerce Protocol](https://www.androidauthority.com/google-universal-commerce-protocol-announcement-3631662), developed in collaboration with Shopify, Walmart, Target, Etsy, and Wayfair, which lets shoppers check out inside Google’s AI Mode and Gemini. And more recently, Meta’s Muse agent went viral for its shopping proficiency, so much so that [Amazon blocked](https://www.thestar.com.my/tech/tech-news/2026/09/22/amazon-blocks-metas-muse-ai-agent-from-its-retail-site) it from its retail site in September 2026. A few weeks later, Meta and Sierra [proposed a standard](https://constellationr.com/insights/news/meta-sierra-pitch-personal-agent-protocol-govern-how-personal-ai-agents-deal-business) for how personal AI agents should deal with businesses. [McKinsey estimates](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/quantumblack/our-insights/the-agentic-commerce-opportunity-how-ai-agents-are-ushering-in-a-new-era-for-consumers-and-merchants) that agents could orchestrate \$900 billion to \$1 trillion in US retail sales by 2030 (only four years from now!)

For obvious reasons, this is a watershed moment for the practice of behavioral pricing. Everything we know, seek to understand, and apply in stores and on websites and apps is based on the understanding that our buyers are human, undergoing a human decision making process that culminates in a purchase by humans. As a simple example, [charm prices](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/tracing-the-history-of-left-digit) (i.e., prices ending with 9) are posited to work because people read numbers from left to right and give greater weight to the leftmost digit. This makes a price like \$49.99 feel much lower than \$50.00 than it really is. In the same way, [partitioned prices](https://doi.org/10.1177/002224379803500404) (the division of a total price into two or more components, which may be mandatory or not) lead [people to anchor](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/why-are-resort-fees-so-popular-when) on the base price and underweight secondary (typically smaller) components, such as surcharges or shipping fees, resulting in an underestimation of the total price.

Thus, based on extensive bodies of research on charm prices and price partitioning, our advice to pricing practitioners is to liberally use prices ending in 9 or 99, and to avoid presenting prices in all-inclusive formats, and present itemized bills instead. Like these two effects, virtually every behavioral pricing tactic is based on an understanding of consumer decision making where the consumer is a human. What happens to behavioral pricing tactics when the buyer is no longer human, but a machine? This is the pricing conundrum I want to explore in this post.

There is interesting emerging research that gives us some clues. The first is the idea that machines will be rational buyers. Back in 2017, well before the current mainstreaming of AI agents was even on the horizon, Gal and Elkin-Koren [argued](https://cris.haifa.ac.il/en/publications/algorithmic-consumers/) that so-called ‘algorithmic consumers’ could strengthen the buyers’ side of the tussle with brands by searching widely and resisting persuasive manipulation. But the authors also perceptively cautioned that much would depend on who controlled the algorithms running the algorithmic consumers. It seems the current consultant and industry discussion on this issue has adopted the authors’ optimistic take without acknowledging their caution. For example, McKinsey has [written](https://www.growthunhinged.com/p/your-next-customer-might-be-an-ai-agent) about dynamic pricing and how agents and platforms will be paid, while other expert analyses have focused on the [infrastructure](https://www.growthunhinged.com/p/your-next-customer-might-be-an-ai-agent) sellers will need to serve machine buyers, such as payment cards for agents and [machine-readable](https://www.simon-kucher.com/en/insights/future-isnt-one-click-away-how-ai-agents-will-revolutionize-your-customer-experience-cx) product data and pricing.

In a [recent HBR piece](https://hbr.org/2026/05/research-traditional-marketing-doesnt-work-on-ai-shopping-agents), Sabbah and Acar reported running thousands of simulated shopping rounds with four AI models covering four different product categories. They found that marketing tactics such as scarcity messages, countdown timers, strikethrough prices, vouchers, and bundles had inconsistent effects on machine purchases, varying by model. Only star ratings based on reviews consistently increased choice by AI agents in their study, while higher prices consistently reduced it. Their advice to marketers was to “treat AI models as distinct segments.”

While I agree with the authors’ overall conclusion, one issue needs to be probed further: the fact that marketing tactics designed for humans fail with machine buyers does not mean that the machine buyers are rational. It raises two important follow-on questions that are crucial for pricing managers and consultants (and indeed all marketers) to know: (1) What do machine buyers, a.k.a. AI agents, respond to instead, and (2) Who decides the machine’s decision calculus? In the rest of this post, I summarize research on how AI agents shop, describe two experiments I ran with four AI models, including the method and results, and discuss what the findings mean for pricing managers and consultants.

### **What We Know About How Machines Buy**

A small but fast-growing body of research has had AI agents perform shopping tasks to study how they differ from humans. In many ways, AI agents do shop rationally. For example, agents favor higher-rated products and prefer lower prices. But the research also shows that agents often respond to cues that are not informative and to small differences between options as if they were large. One [comprehensive study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.25609) tested 17 AI models in a simulated web store and compared them with human shoppers. A product’s position on the page shifted one model’s choices by 89%, even though the position was entirely incidental, compared to only about 4% for humans. The authors also found that modest differences in price or ratings moved agents “in a nearly binary fashion,” with larger differences adding little incremental shift in choice (because the ceiling had already been reached). And they responded far more intensely to ratings than humans did. A higher star rating increased the share of a product’s selection by 30% to 80% in 14 of the 17 models, compared to only about 5% for people. In other words, machine buyers follow rigid rules, even when such rules give an offer’s trivial features significant or even determining weight in the choice.

Another [study found](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.25779) that AI agents in a simulated marketplace showed a strong bias toward the first offer they received and did not explore sufficiently when given more options. A third [study found](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.02630) that position effects and sensitivity to price and ratings varied considerably across AI models, even among models from the same company, and that a model update could reshuffle sellers’ market shares. Finally, researchers [have found](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2415697122) that language models prefer product descriptions written by other models over identical descriptions written by humans. Thus, this research paints a picture of machine buyers as subject to the vicissitudes of the AI model in which they reside at that particular time, and far from being rational.

Many of these studies vary price levels of the offer, but to my knowledge, none so far have tested either price endings or partitioned prices, two of the most widely known and used behavioral pricing tactics, on AI shopping agents. I conducted two experiments to test the efficacy of these two effects on machine buyers.

### **Testing Four Machine Buyers With Behavioral Pricing Tactics**

I ran two concurrent experiments, one on price endings and the second on partitioned prices. I conducted them in October 2026 using the same four AI models and the same procedure. Each study had its own stimuli (discussed below), and I analyzed them separately. This [ResearchBox](https://researchbox.org/10193%20(CODE:%20QBQNNJ)) contains the data and the code to reproduce the results.

#### **Common procedure**

The four AI models I tested were OpenAI’s GPT-6 Sol, Google’s Gemini 3.8 Flash, Anthropic’s Claude Sonnet 5.5, and Meta’s open-weight Muse Glimmer 30B (distinct from Meta’s Muse agent). I accessed each model through its developer interface at its default settings. Each model was instructed that it was a shopping assistant authorized to buy one product for the user, then given a need (e.g., “I need a blender for making smoothies most mornings,” “I need wireless earbuds for my commute and the gym.”) and two product listings, and then asked to choose one and give a one-sentence reason for its choice.

Three product categories were used in both experiments: blenders, wireless earbuds, and coffee grinders. The brands of the two options were entirely fictional to ensure that existing brand reputations played no role in machine buyers’ choices. In every trial, the two listings described equivalent features, showed the same 4.4-star rating and 1,226 reviews, and differed only in how the price was displayed. The figure below shows an example of the stimulus.

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/cc7336e9-c7e5-4c99-b332-231924f3eb2b_1960x1738.png)

Each model completed 50 independent trials in every condition. Within each condition, I alternated which product appeared first, exchanged the feature descriptions between the two products, and rotated the product category. Thus, across the two experiments, the design was 4 AI models x 10 conditions (5 in each study) x 50 trials, for a total of 2,000 purchase decisions. In both studies, the measure that I report is the percentage of trials in which the AI shopping agent chose Product A, whose price was manipulated as described next.

#### **Study 1. Price endings**

Study 1 investigated whether AI agents exhibit the left-digit bias, i.e., whether a price just below a round number gains more than a 1-cent difference would warrant. It had five conditions. In the control condition, both products cost \$80. In the other four, Product A cost one cent less with a change in the left digit (\$79.99 vs. \$80.00), one cent less without a change in the left digit (\$80.49 vs. \$80.50), one cent more (\$80.01 vs. \$80.00), or one dollar less (\$79.00 vs. \$80.00). If the AI agents shared the human bias, the \$79.99 condition would produce a larger shift in choice toward Product A than the \$80.49 condition. The \$80.01 condition tests whether the one-cent disadvantage hurts Product A as much as a one-cent advantage benefits it, and the \$79.00 condition tests whether a larger saving produces a stronger response.

#### **Study 2. Partitioned prices**

Study 2 asked whether AI agents are influenced by a price communicated as a base price plus a shipping charge and whether they notice a separate handling fee added to the base price. Product B always cost \$80.00 with free shipping. In the control condition, Product A had the same price. In three of the remaining four conditions, Product A was shown as a base price plus shipping, with a total that was equal to Product B’s (\$72 + \$8), higher than Product B (\$70 + \$12), or lower than Product B (\$70 + \$8). In the last condition, Product A was listed at “\$70, free shipping,” but a \$12.00 handling fee appeared in the last line of its description, resulting in a total price of \$82.00. A susceptibility to partitioned prices would result in the partitioned version of Product A receiving a greater share when the total prices were equal, whereas an agent that ignored partitioning would be indifferent between the two options. The higher (\$72 + \$12) and lower (\$72 + \$8) partitioned totals were designed to confirm AI agents’ propensity for partitioning (or lack thereof), while the final hidden-fee condition was a different, somewhat more stringent test of the partitioning effect.

#### **Pilot studies**

I conducted two small pilot tests before the main studies, each comprising 200 trials (five per condition for each model). In the first pilot, the two options had slightly different review counts (1,214 and 1,237; counterbalanced across trials) to make the listings look realistic. But the results showed that when prices were identical, every model chose the product with 23 more reviews. I also asked the agents to choose first, before giving a reason, which led two agents in the hidden-fee condition to commit to a product and then reason their way to the opposite conclusion (described below). I made the review counts identical and asked the agents to give their reason first. The second pilot was conducted to confirm these changes worked, and they did. The control conditions no longer favored either product, and the errors in the hidden-fee condition disappeared. The trials from the two pilots are not included in the results.

#### **Study 1 Results**

Study 1 tested whether machine buyers would favor a price just below a round number as humans do. Results showed that they did not do so. In every condition, all four agents chose the cheaper of the two identical products, almost without exception, and whether the left digit changed made no difference. If machine buyers processed prices the way humans do, the \$79.99 product would have gained more share relative to its \$80.00 counterpart, compared with the share gained by the \$80.49 option versus the \$80.50 one. However, the \$79.99 option was chosen in 198 out of 200 trials (the two exceptions came from OpenAI’s agent), while the \$80.49 option was chosen in all 200 trials. To machine buyers, a penny difference is everything. The remaining conditions in the table show how thoroughly a 1-cent difference affects the choice between two comparable options, suggesting that setting an offering’s price at the smallest currency denomination below the nearest competitor’s would be the way to go when dealing with machine buyers. (Of course, it’s a little more complicated than that, since there will usually be many more than two options, and they will vary in their features, including their brand). Based on the results in the table above, it seems that the efficacy of charm prices is completely neutralized by machine buyers.

**Study 2 Results**

Study 2 tested whether dividing a price into a base price and a shipping price would influence machine buyers, and whether they would notice a fee placed beyond the price. In almost every case, the AI agents added up the full price and chose the product with the lower total. When the two totals were equal, displaying the price as a base price plus shipping made a product *less* likely to be chosen than showing the same amount as a single price. The only exception to this was OpenAI’s agent, which paid \$2 more in one condition, influenced by the product’s position on the page. The results are shown in the next table.

[Embedded content: datawrapper.dwcdn.net](https://datawrapper.dwcdn.net/KqlyV/1/)

As shown in the table, when the total prices were equal, the agents chose the all-inclusive price in 191 of the 200 trials. They explicitly added the two parts, typically noting that both products cost “\$80 total,” and then chose the single price, explaining that free shipping meant one clear price or simpler returns. When the partitioned total was \$2 higher, no one chose this option, thus completely erasing the partitioned pricing effect.

In the hidden fee condition, the agents accounted for the \$12 handling fee and chose the alternative option in 199 of 200 trials. It’s worth noting here that in the first pilot study, when agents had to choose first and then explain their choice, OpenAI’s agent chose the product with the hidden fee (and higher price) in 2 of 5 trials, once falsely explaining that it had “a slightly lower total of \$82 versus \$80” as the reason for the choice. Claude’s agent chose the wrong product twice (out of 5 times) but corrected itself. When the agents had to explain their choice before making it, these errors disappeared.

In the “partitioned, lower total” condition, the Gemini, Claude, and Meta shopping agents chose the cheaper product in all 50 trials apiece. OpenAI’s agent also did so whenever the cheaper option was listed first. However, when it was listed second, it chose the more expensive \$80 option in 19 of 25 trials, explaining that it cost “only \$2 more” or “just \$2 more” with free shipping, indicating a preference for paying more for free shipping. But the same agent made the opposite choice whenever the order of offer presentation was reversed, suggesting that the order of presentation biased its choice.

Supporting the significance of order of presentation, in both control conditions and 400 trials across the two studies, all four agents chose the product listed first every single time. Interestingly, the agents’ explanations did not always reveal this preference. Claude’s agent explained it had picked the first-listed option in 90 of its 100 control trials, Meta’s did so in 55, but neither OpenAI’s agent nor Gemini’s agent mentioned the option’s position, instead giving reasons about the product’s features for their choice.

### **Who Controls the Buyer’s Psychology and What is the Value of Understanding It?**

For centuries, if not millennia, the understanding of buyer psychology has influenced how commercial transactions occur in the marketplace. All this accumulated knowledge and ongoing exploration seems about to be thrown away as human buyers are replaced by machine buyers. As this small exploratory investigation made clear, the behavioral pricing effects brands and retailers rely on are entirely erased by machine buyers. What seems to be taking their place are rigid rules programmed by the companies building the AI models behind machine buyers, which last only as long as the model does (which is to say, not very long). For now, we might as well call them quirks (rather than something more nefarious-sounding) and give the AI companies the benefit of the doubt, hoping they will iron them out.

But we should also understand that these AI companies are fully in charge of machine buyer “psychology” and have the ability to manipulate how machine buyers make choices. Take the case of OpenAI, which [describes the product results](https://openai.com/index/buy-it-in-chatgpt/) in ChatGPT as “organic and unsponsored” from one side of its mouth, and then also says it considers factors including “whether Instant Checkout is enabled” from the other. Conflict between platforms and users (whether they are brands, retailers, or consumers) is a thing. One [recent study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.08525) found that across 23 AI models, 18 recommended a costlier sponsored flight more than 50% of the time when the platform’s interests conflicted with the user’s interests.

The shift to machine buying also has significant ramifications for consumer protection. FTC’s recent (from May 2025) [rule](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2025-01-10/html/2024-30293.htm) on unfair or deceptive fees requires sellers of live-event tickets and short-term lodging to show the total price up front. Rules like these are entirely configured for human attention, assuming that limited attention at checkout needs to be supported. The results of Study 2 suggest that agents are fully capable of adding up fees on their own. No doubt, sneaky, borderline unethical pricing tactics like drip pricing will lose their punch with machine buyers. But the risk moves higher up the buying journey, to the algorithm that determines the order of search results and to descriptive text written to influence the AI agent. As far as I know, consumer law has no clear counterpart to its “reasonable consumer” standard (“reasonably machine buyer” standard?) for these harms. If the companies that own the AI models also own the psychology of machine buyers, will they be held accountable when machine buyers stumble at the consumer’s expense?

### **Practical Pricing Strategies for Selling to Machine Buyers**

Clearly, as more machine buyers purchase on behalf of humans, pricing strategies will need to accommodate them and rethink conventional methods of setting and communicating prices. Some of the ideas that emerge from the results of the two studies are obvious, and others are a bit counterintuitive. However, they all suggest that pricing will need to consider the decision making of both machines and humans. Here are four ideas that follow directly from the two studies.

***All-inclusive prices will be more effective for machine buyers.*** In Study 2, partitioned prices lost out to equivalent all-inclusive prices in 191 of 200 trials, and the agents treated free shipping as a benefit, over and above its economic value. This suggests that partitioned pricing, which has become so popular across industries, may no longer work as the default mode of price presentation and may, in fact, hurt when dealing with machine buyers.

***Charm prices will fall, and penny competition will rise.*** In Study 1, a price ending in 99 conferred no advantage with any AI agent whatsoever, signaling an end to the left-digit bias. Instead, a product priced 1 cent lower than the competing option won out every single time, regardless of mode of price presentation or order of presentation. The obvious implication is that monitoring competitor prices will be very beneficial, as will constantly changing prices and adding more and more features (or descriptions thereof) to produce differentiation. Automated repricing (e.g., “if competitor X lowers price, set my price 1 cent lower than theirs”) may not be a good idea. In fact, it may be a terrible idea, fueling a price war down to zero price and seller ruination.

***Sellers will compete on differences that machine buyers can detect and identify.*** In the two studies here, as well as in some of the studies cited in this piece, it is clear that machine buyers’ choices are based on binary considerations (i.e., whether a particular condition is met or an additional feature exists) rather than on considerations of degree. All else being equal, a one-penny difference was the same to the machine buyer as a dollar difference. Even the appearance of a pricing page may be a differentiator, meaning that providing a clear and complete price list may be very important to attract machine buyers.

***Sellers will treat each AI model as a unique machine buyer, and test its responses constantly.*** Sabbah and Acar recommended treating each AI model as a distinct segment. Although the four models in my studies behaved mostly consistently (except for some weirdness by OpenAI’s model), their point is well taken. Model updates can change future choice patterns among machine buyers, which means sellers must constantly run experiments like mine to test the calibration of machine buyers’ responses and discern their rigid rules.

### **Conclusion**

If a 1-cent difference between two comparable options invariably determines which option is chosen, and a higher position in the list always wins over a lower position when options don’t have much to choose between them, behavioral pricing will wither, and price competition will become a matter of the agent’s decision rules and the platform’s ranking. In this new world, sellers can adjust their prices and hope for the best, but they will have virtually no influence over how AI models evaluate their offers, when the models will be updated, or how these updates will affect machine buyers’ choices. It is an understatement to say we are entering an era of the Wild West when even our most fundamental assumptions about how pricing works on consumers may need to be abandoned and worked out from scratch.
