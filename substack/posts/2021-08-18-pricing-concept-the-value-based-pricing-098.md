During my two-plus decades of teaching and consulting work on pricing strategies, if I had to name one concept that is discussed, idolized, revered, seen as a magic bullet, reviled, abandoned, and misunderstood more than any other, it is **value-based pricing** (also commonly known as “value pricing”). The method creates more anticipation, confusion, and angst among managers, and even many pricing experts, than virtually any other pricing concept.

In my experience, widely-held misconceptions about exactly how the value-based pricing method works lead managers to first embrace, and then later, prematurely abandon the method for the wrong reasons. Then they settle for what they’ve always done which is either use some variant of cost-based pricing or another ad hoc method.

To clear up the confusion and misunderstanding, I will describe the value-based pricing method clearly and completely in this post. In a companion post, I will write in detail about the different misconceptions surrounding value-based pricing, dispel them, and set out its strengths and limitations in B2B contexts.

### **Defining Value-based Pricing**

My definition of value-based pricing is this:

#### **“Value-based pricing is the method of establishing a price by which the manager calculates and seeks to earn the differentiated economic value of their offering to a particular targeted customer.”**

In most settings where it is used effectively, value-based pricing provides one, but not the only, input into the company’s pricing decision. A useful side effect of employing value-based pricing is to learn your offering’s economic value to a particular customer of interest and then be able to exploit the knowledge during pricing and sales negotiations.

Value-based pricing is a B2B pricing method. Although it is widely used in some seemingly consumer categories like over-the-counter medications or prescription drugs, its main use lies in pricing business products and services.
