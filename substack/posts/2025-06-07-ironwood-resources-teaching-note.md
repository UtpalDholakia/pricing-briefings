The Ironwood Resources case introduces students to the complexities of pricing strategy in the upstream oil and gas industry. It is about Ironwood Resources, a mid-sized producer of oil and gas in the Permian Basin. Its newly hired Pricing Director, James Wilson, finds margin erosion due to inconsistent discounting and bundled unpriced services. His research and analysis find that the average pocket price is only 78% of the list price, and 20% of the transactions across Ironwood’s customer base are unprofitable.

Through detailed transaction data analysis, including the use of pocket price and pocket margin waterfalls, the case challenges students to develop data-driven pricing strategies, address price dispersion, and align stakeholders in a relationship-driven industry. While oil and gas products are often seen as pure commodities with prices set by the market, this case demonstrates how various adjustments and customer-specific costs can dramatically impact profitability even when base prices appear to be similar or even the same. This teaching note provides a comprehensive guide to facilitate class discussions, analyze pricing calculations, and contextualize industry dynamics.

### **Learning Objectives**

The case will help students to:

1.  Apply pocket price and margin waterfall analyses to quantify the impact of discounts and hidden costs on profitability. Distinguish between list price, invoice price, on-invoice and off-invoice adjustments, pocket price, and pocket margin.

2.  Understand how seemingly small discounts and adjustments can compound to create significant price erosion, and the importance of analyzing profitability at the customer and transaction level.

3.  Design value-based pricing strategies, including tiered pricing and service unbundling, to improve margins in a volatile commodity market.

4.  Address resistance from sales teams and customers to implement pricing reforms while maintaining relationships.

5.  Identify and mitigate unjustified price variations across customer segments using data-driven insights.

Instructors can use this case to facilitate discussions about strategic pricing in B2B contexts, the balance between sales volume and profitability, and organizational culture and change management considerations when implementing new pricing approaches. It is suitable for use in second-year MBA or EMBA electives on pricing strategy, marketing, especially courses focused on B2B marketing and the oil and gas industry, as well as for executive education and training programs. It assumes familiarity with basic financial concepts (e.g., margins, cost structures) and is ideal for students exploring the intersection of pricing, business strategy, and operational efficiency.

### **Teaching Approach**

The case can be taught in a 90-minute session using a combination of lecture, group discussion, and breakout activities. The following is a suggested lesson plan:

***Pre-Class Preparation***

**Assigned reading**: Students should read the Ironwood Resources case and review Appendices A–E, focusing on the pocket price and margin waterfalls and transaction case studies.

**Potential preparation questions**:

1.  Calculate the pocket price and margin for a hypothetical transaction based on Appendix D’s structure, adjusting one discount variable (e.g., eliminate the competitive situation discount).

2.  Propose one strategic option from James Wilson’s list and justify its prioritization.

***Class Session Outline***

**Introduction (10 minutes)**:

- Briefly review the oil and gas industry’s upstream sector, emphasizing its market volatility (e.g., WTI price swings from negative to \$100+ per barrel), perceived commoditization, and competitive pressures in the Permian Basin.

- Introduce James Wilson’s challenge: improving margins in a relationship-driven industry for an organization that employs inconsistent, ad-hoc pricing practices.

- Introduce and test understanding of the key concepts: pocket price, pocket price waterfall, pocket margin, pocket margin waterfall, and price dispersion.

**Pricing Analysis Discussion (20 minutes)**:

- Use Appendix A (Pocket Price Waterfall) and Appendix B (Pocket Margin Waterfall) to walk through the erosion from list price (\$75.00) to pocket price (\$58.49) and pocket margin (\$12.36).

- Discuss key findings: 78% pocket price ratio, 20% unprofitable transactions, and high price dispersion (65–87% of list price in the refinery segment).

- Ask: *What surprises you about the gap between list price and pocket price? How does this reflect Ironwood’s pricing challenges?*

**Breakout Activity: Transaction Analysis (20 minutes)**:

- Divide students into small groups and assign each to analyze either the “Bad Deal” (Appendix D) or “Good Deal” (Appendix E) transaction.

- Task: Calculate the impact of removing one discount (e.g., competitive situation discount in Appendix D) or adding a premium (e.g., rush delivery fee in Appendix E) on pocket price and margin.

- Example calculation for Appendix D (GulfStream Refining):

  - Remove competitive situation discount (\$3.17/barrel):

  - Invoice Price: \$68.54 + \$3.17 = \$71.71 (90.5% of list price)

  - Pocket Price: \$60.45 + \$3.17 = \$63.62 (80.3% of list price)

  - Pocket Margin: \$18.49 + \$3.17 = \$21.66 (27.3% of list price)

- Groups report back, comparing how pricing adjustments affect profitability.

**Strategic Options Discussion (25 minutes)**:

- Present James’ six strategic options (Price Structure Reform, Discount Governance, Unbundling Services, Customer Segmentation, Performance Metrics, Digital Tools).

- Lead a debate between students on Question 1: *Which option should Ironwood prioritize, and why?* Use a whiteboard to list criteria (e.g., ROI, customer impact, implementation feasibility).

- Example: Unbundling services (e.g., charging for rush delivery, saving \$2.3 million annually) offers quick ROI but risks customer pushback.

- Discuss Question 3: *How can James Wilson align the sales team with the new pricing strategy?* Highlight Robert Chen’s resistance and the need for revised incentives.

**Industry and Implementation Context (15 minutes)**:

- Discuss Questions 2 and 5: *How can Ironwood minimize customer pushback and anticipate competitor responses?* Reference Permian market dynamics (e.g., ExxonMobil’s consolidation) and regulatory pressures (e.g., methane emissions penalties).

- Highlight the role of digital tools in providing real-time pricing analytics to counter competitive offers.

- Pose Question 4: *What KPIs should Ironwood track?* Suggest metrics like pocket margin percentage, price dispersion range, and customer retention rates by segment, as described later in this teaching note.

**Wrap-Up (5 minutes)**

- Summarize key takeaways: Pricing strategy requires balancing profitability, customer relationships, and market dynamics. Data-driven tools and stakeholder alignment are critical for success.

- Conclusion: Discuss how pricing decisions integrate with the organization’s broader business strategy.

### **Prioritizing the strategic options**

In the case, James Wilson identified six key strategic options for Ironwood: Price Structure Reform, Discount Governance, Unbundling Services, Customer Segmentation, Performance Metrics revision, and Digital Tools implementation. Prioritizing these initiatives should be guided by a balanced approach considering both the impact and the feasibility of each option.

Ironwood should prioritize its strategic options based on a matrix that evaluates both financial impact and implementation feasibility. The immediate priority should be initiatives that offer significant margin improvement with relatively straightforward implementation.

***Short-term priorities (0-6 months):***

- The implementation of discount governance should come first, as it addresses a clear source of price leakage (ad-hoc discounting by the sales team members) without requiring significant system changes or investments. By establishing clear approval thresholds and documenting the rationale behind discounts through the sales organization, Ironwood can quickly stem further margin erosion while building momentum for the broader pricing transformation that is James’ ultimate goal.

- The revision of performance metrics should follow closely, or be conducted in parallel as a first order of business, because changing what gets measured will begin shifting the behaviors of Ironwood team members toward profitability. Initially, this could involve adding simple pocket margin reports to existing sales performance dashboards, with more sophisticated metrics based on price dispersion analysis introduced over time.

***Medium-term priorities (6-12 months):***

- The reform of price structure will require more preparation and effort, but is likely to deliver substantial benefits. Ironwood should segment this work, perhaps starting with its most profitable business segment (e.g., NGLs) to demonstrate the impact before expanding to other segments and customer accounts.

- The implementation of digital tools could proceed in parallel with the Price Structure Reform initiative, as adopting the technology will enable the sales team to execute the new pricing approach consistently. The focus should be on practical tools that give frontline sales staff immediate visibility into the profitability of their transactions and customer accounts.

***Longer-term priorities (12+ months):***

- Customer segmentation represents a more fundamental strategic shift in how Ironwood views and prioritizes its customers and relationships with them. While valuable, this initiative is likely to require extensive market analysis and internal alignment, and is likely to have a broader focus beyond pricing strategy to span innovation, differentiation, and positioning.

- Unbundling of the services has high potential but is likely to be disruptive to customer relationships, evoking a negative response. It may be best to implement it last of the initiative set, after Ironwood has established its capabilities to quantify service costs and communicate value effectively to its customer base.

The prioritization also needs to consider interdependencies between the initiatives. For example, Digital Tools enable effective Discount Governance, while Performance Metrics Changes support all the other initiatives by aligning incentives with new pricing approaches.

### **Balancing profit, customer pushback, and competitive risks**

Ironwood should adopt a phased, segmented implementation approach that balances profit capture with relationship preservation:

***Graduated customer involvement***

- Begin with relatively low-risk customer relationships, focusing on ones that are new, less price-sensitive, or where Ironwood has unique value advantages.

- Implement changes at natural transition points or triggers in customer relationships (e.g., contract renewals, product specification changes) rather than mid-contract.

- Temporarily exempt strategic accounts that will require more careful transition planning while the new approach proves successful elsewhere.

***Sequencing of price structure improvement***

- Start by addressing the relatively low-profile or low-visibility elements first. One example is components of price that customers don’t directly see on invoices, such as free technical services or extended payment terms.

- For each customer, start with the single largest source of price leakage rather than trying multiple or comprehensive changes simultaneously.

- Gradually introduce value-based premiums by differentiating services before implementing any changes perceived by customers as “price increases.”

***Dealing with change management issues***

- Develop and implement tools for articulating value for the sales team that highlight the total customer benefits beyond price, and make them an integral part of the sales process.

- Create customer-specific transition plans for key accounts that might include phase-in periods, grandfathering certain elements, or offsetting adjustments.

- Establish a dedicated “SWAT team” of cross-functional experts to support account managers during difficult customer conversations.

- · Create a feedback loop to quickly address implementation issues and adjust the approach as needed.

An effective approach to implement the initiatives will rely on a surgical mindset instead of a broad-brush one. The idea is to identify the specific pricing elements that drive the greatest profit leakage for each customer relationship (or segment) and address those first while maintaining some flexibility for relationship-critical accommodations.

The company should also reserve some of the captured margin improvement to fund strategic investments in service enhancements or product quality improvements that provide tangible customer benefits, which helps to create a “win-win” narrative around the pricing changes. In a nutshell, better price execution means a stronger and more sustainable business.

### **Aligning the sales team with the new pricing strategy**

Securing buy-in from the well-entrenched and skeptical sales team requires addressing both the rational and the emotional aspects of the shift. The sales team’s concerns will typically center on customer relationships, the impact on their compensation, negotiation flexibility, and also perceived complexity.

***Education and context***

- Share the comprehensive analysis with appropriate detail so sales staff understand the patterns of margin erosion and their impact on Ironwood (and ultimately on their own jobs).

- Demonstrate how unprofitable transactions affect the company’s ability to be sustainable, and invest in innovations that ultimately help both customers and the sales team.

- Provide evidence from reputable external benchmarking studies or sources showing how competitors are shifting toward similar value-based approaches, countering the “we’ll lose customers” concern.

***Involvement and ownership***

- Include Robert Chen and his designated team leaders in the design process for the new pricing approach, particularly in defining the appropriate ranges of discretion.

- Create pilot teams of respected sales veterans to test and refine approaches before broader rollout.

- Establish a “pricing council” with rotating sales representation to evaluate ongoing decisions and adjustments.

***Incentive alignment***

- Consider designing a transitional compensation model that prevents significant decreases in compensation during the learning period.

- Consider implementing a “price realization bonus” that directly rewards improvement in pocket margin percentage, shifting from sales volume or revenue.

- Create recognition programs for deals that exemplify the new value-based approach.

***Tools and support***

- Develop simple, intuitive tools that make pricing decisions easier, thus reducing the administrative burden while improving decisions.

- Create *deal simulators* that can be used in Excel to allow sales team members to test different pricing scenarios and understand impacts before customer discussions.

- Provide ready-to-use value messaging and objection-handling guides for common customer reactions.

- Assign dedicated pricing specialists to support high-priority negotiations during the transition period.

***Manage flexibility***

- Establish guardrails rather than rigid rules or policies, preserving negotiation flexibility within defined boundaries.

- Create a streamlined exception process for unique situations that preserves agility while ensuring appropriate oversight.

- Allow account managers to retain certain relationship-building tools even as others are standardized.

***Share success stories***

- Quickly identify and publicize early wins where the new approach secured profitable business without damaging relationships.

- Calculate and communicate the personal impact, i.e., how improved margins translate to enhanced commission opportunities.

The most important factor is positioning the change as enhancing the sales team’s abilities rather than restricting them, thereby shifting from pure price negotiations to value-based selling that benefits both Ironwood and its customers.

### **Pricing strategy KPIs**

Ironwood should establish a balanced scorecard of metrics that capture both financial outcomes and the health of the underlying pricing approach. These should include:

***Primary Financial Metrics***

- **Pocket Margin Percentage**: The most comprehensive measure of pricing effectiveness, showing what percentage of the list price ultimately flows to the bottom line after all adjustments and costs.

- **Pocket Price Waterfall Leakage**: The gap between list price and pocket price, measured both in absolute dollars and as a percentage of list price.

- **Price Yield**: The realized price versus the relevant market benchmark indices over time, measuring Ironwood’s ability to command premium pricing.

- **Customer Profitability Distribution**: The percentage of customers in each profitability quartile, tracking migration toward more profitable relationships.

***Pricing Practice Metrics***

- **Discount Variation**: Standard deviation of discount percentages within customer segments, and across segments, measuring consistency of pricing decisions.

- **Exception Frequency**: Percentage of transactions requiring pricing exceptions, indicating whether standard pricing structures are adequate.

- **Approval Efficiency**: Average time required for discount approvals, ensuring the process enhances rather than hinders deal velocity.

- **Price Realization Gap**: Difference between quoted prices and actually realized prices, identifying post-sale leakage.

***Customer and Market Metrics***

- **Customer Retention Rate**: Overall, and specifically for high-value customers, ensuring pricing changes don’t damage key relationships. (May need consensus on how to define retention/ defection, but that’s the topic for another case study on another day).

- **Customer Satisfaction**: Targeted surveys assessing value perception and price fairness.

- **Win/Loss Rate**: Analysis of deals won and lost, with specific attention to price as a factor.

- **Competitive Price Position**: Regular assessment of Ironwood’s pricing relative to key competitors across product lines.

***Sales Capability Metrics***

- **Value Conversation Effectiveness**: Assessment of how effectively sales staff articulate value beyond price.

- **Pricing Tool Adoption**: Usage rates of new pricing tools and resources.

- **Profitability Improvement by Sales Representative**: Individual contribution to margin enhancement.

***Implementation Metrics***

- **Initiative Completion Percentage**: Tracking of project milestones against timeline.

- **Organization Readiness**: Surveys measuring understanding and support for the new approach across functions.

These metrics could be introduced and reported in a tiered fashion, with executives seeing comprehensive dashboards while sales managers and representatives receive metrics directly relevant to their sphere of influence. Importantly, Ironwood should establish clear baselines before implementation and set realistic improvement targets for each metric as the different initiatives come online and its pricing capabilities mature.

### **Potential competitive responses**

Ironwood’s competitors will likely respond in different ways, depending on their market position, financial health, and strategic priorities. Understanding these potential responses will guide the implementation approach.

***The Integrated Majors (XOMs and CVXs of the world)***

- They may initially tolerate Ironwood’s improved margins, especially if they are focused on their own cost efficiency programs.

- They could eventually leverage their scale advantages to target Ironwood’s most profitable customers with aggressive pricing if they perceive a market share threat.

- Another possibility is they might accelerate their own value-based pricing initiatives if Ironwood demonstrates success.

- There’s also a small chance that Ironwood’s actions fail to register or take a long time to register.

***Similar-sized Independents***

- They are the ones most likely to respond aggressively as they compete in the same customer segments with similar cost structures, and are likely watching Ironwood like a hawk.

- They may attempt to poach Ironwood’s customers by maintaining lower prices and turning on (or amplifying) “we-won’t-nickel-and-dime-you” messaging.

- If financially stressed, they might not have the capability to match Ironwood’s service quality even at lower prices.

***Private equity-backed competitors***

- These companies are often on a short leash and have explicit volume targets tied to their financing, making them particularly aggressive in price competition.

- They may also lack the operational sophistication to match Ironwood’s service quality or technical support.

- They could target specific high-volume customers with unsustainable pricing to achieve short-term growth metrics.

**Implications for Ironwood’s Implementation Strategy**

***Segmented rollout***

- Begin with customer segments where Ironwood has the strongest differentiation and competitive insulation.

- Initially, focus on product lines where competitors are known to have limited capacity to increase volume without significant investment.

- Consider geographical sequencing to avoid creating regional competitive hotspots.

***Competitor-specific contingency plans***

- Develop specific response protocols for predicted competitive reactions, including selective price-matching authority.

- Create “defense packages” for the most vulnerable high-value customers that could be quickly deployed if competitors target them.

- Establish intelligence gathering mechanisms to provide early warning of competitive responses.

***Value differentiation acceleration***

- Accelerate investments in capabilities that competitors cannot easily replicate, such as proprietary technologies that enhance the functional value of Ironwood’s offerings. This is likely to be more R&D and longer-term, and also outside the purview of James Wilson and the pricing strategy function.

- Enhance technical service capabilities that create switching costs for customers considering competitive offers.

- Develop bundled offerings in areas where Ironwood has unique strengths, making price comparisons more difficult.

***Strategic communication***

- Carefully manage external messaging to avoid telegraphing comprehensive pricing changes to competitors.

- Frame price changes in market-acceptable terms (e.g., “value-based pricing” rather than “margin improvement”). (Note that I’ve written about communicating price increases extensively on this blog and elsewhere).

- Prepare account teams with competitive differentiation talking points specific to each major competitor.

***Competitive opportunity identification***

- Use the newly implemented pricing analysis infrastructure to identify competitors’ customers who may be unprofitable for them but profitable for Ironwood.

- Consider creating targeted acquisition offers for these customers that improve margins while still appearing price-competitive.

The most effective approach will be one that balances competitive awareness with a firm conviction in Ironwood’s value proposition. Rather than trying to predict and counter every possible competitive move, Ironwood should focus on building a pricing structure that is resilient to competitive pressures through strong value differentiation and strategic flexibility.

### **Conclusion**

The Ironwood Resources case provides a robust framework for exploring pricing strategy challenges in the upstream oil and gas industry, emphasizing the critical role of data-driven decision-making in addressing margin erosion. Through James Wilson’s analysis, students uncover how inconsistent discounting and unpriced services lead to a significant gap between list and pocket prices, with 20% of transactions unprofitable. By engaging with pocket price and margin waterfall analyses, students learn to quantify the impact of pricing adjustments and propose strategic solutions like discount governance, service unbundling, and customer segmentation. The case underscores the need to balance profitability with customer relationships in a volatile, relationship-driven market, offering practical insights into implementing pricing reforms while navigating sales team resistance and competitive pressures.

To effectively implement these strategies, Ironwood must prioritize initiatives with high financial impact and manageable execution, such as discount governance and performance metric revisions, while phasing in more complex changes like price structure reform and digital tools. By adopting a phased, segmented approach, Ironwood can minimize customer pushback and competitive risks, using clear value articulation and tailored transition plans to maintain relationships. The recommended KPIs, including pocket margin percentage and price dispersion, provide a measurable framework for tracking progress. Ultimately, the case equips students with the tools to design resilient pricing strategies that enhance profitability while aligning stakeholders in a competitive commodity market.
