##### ***Summary:*** This post traces the communication of price changes into the industrial age, from 1800 to 1914. Industrialization turned the price from a privately negotiated, hidden value into a public commitment. Telegraphy, commodity grading, trade journals, and posted price tags made prices easier to observe and compare across markets. But this visibility created a new conundrum. Sellers could preserve the appearance of a public price while privately offering selective discounts or rebates. The resulting loss of credibility produced the era’s defining innovation, the price increase as a public proceeding. Railroad rate increases had to be announced in advance and filed with regulators. They could later be suspended until the seller justified them. This process also created a third audience empowered to judge the legitimacy of a price change.

For the first time, the dominant price communicators were neither rulers nor individual merchants but large private corporations. We also have many sources for understanding exactly how price communication evolved during this period, including numerous printed tariffs, circulars, hearing transcripts, and trade journals, which make it possible to study the nuances of price communication.

#### **Introduction**

Imagine you are a flour miller in Minneapolis on a cold spring morning in 1888[^1]. Your day begins with a look at yesterday’s closing grain prices from Chicago and New York in the morning paper. (These prices were telegraphed overnight to your local newspaper.) Once a week, you get *the Northwestern Miller*, the weekly trade paper, in the mail, with its price tables and an editorial column explaining why patent flour is weak while wheat holds firm. And at the freight office, you find something brand new that did not exist a couple of years earlier. Posted next to the railroad’s printed tariff schedule is a public notice stating that the rate for transporting flour to New York will rise, what the new charge will be, and the date on which the price increase will take effect. There’s a new federal statute that makes such notices mandatory. The Interstate Commerce Act, passed the previous February, reads:

“No advance shall be made in the rates, fares, and charges which have been established and published as aforesaid by any common carrier in compliance with the requirements of this section, except after ten days’ public notice, which shall plainly state the changes proposed to be made in the schedule then in force, and the time when the increased rates, fares, or charges will go into effect… Reductions in such published rates, fares, or charges may be made without previous public notice.[^2]”

Consider what this seemingly innocuous notice represents in the progression of our series on price change communication through the ages. For the previous 17 centuries, the announcement of a price had been an instrument of the seller’s or the authority’s power, whether it was through an edict, an assize, a narh register, or an auction catalog. But now, the authorities made a price change communication into something that the seller was obligated to provide to protect the buyer. This was a fundamental shift in the core function of price change communication, and one we still see in common use today, which provides advance notice of the new price and when it will go into effect.

So far as I have been able to discover, this is one of the first large-scale, governmentally mandated requirements for private entities to publicly announce a price increase in advance using a standardized format. Furthermore, note the deliberate asymmetry added to the requirement. While a price increase requires ten days’ public notice, a price decrease may take effect immediately. The law codified a distinction that seems obvious to us today. When a company raises prices, it has a duty to inform its customers beforehand. But cutting a price does not entail a corresponding duty. Sellers are eager to announce price cuts as advertisements to attract customers rather than as an obligation. That is why, to this day, the price increase announcement is drafted by companies with much trepidation, invoking euphemisms and getting approval from lawyers, whereas the price decrease announcement is produced by copywriters and embellished with sale cues and exclamation points.

#### **When the price became a public commitment**

How did we go from the authority of the early modern age to the obligation of the industrial age? During the early modern period, price communication went private. The price current made the market the author of prices, and the accompanying merchant’s private letter supplied the context. In a real sense, the industrial age “industrialized” price communication by mechanizing and standardizing it, and then using the apparatus of mass production and distribution to make it widely available. By industrializing prices, it changed the nature of the price itself.

A haggled price is a private, fragile agreement between two entities. A telegraphed quotation, a printed list price, a tagged shelf price, and a filed tariff, all artifacts of price communication from this era, are public, reproducible *commitments* that are fundamentally different. These prices can be checked and compared across sellers and across time, creating an expectation that the stated price is the authentic price. Deviations from it, whether through a secret rebate or an unannounced advance, constitute breaches of this unspoken understanding, leaving audiences violated and demanding rules regarding how the commitments may be changed.

As we will see, this progression unfolded in a series of steps. A price must first become visible and standardized, and it did. This visibility created the expectation that the stated price is authentic and universal, which in turn made secret deviations from this stated baseline both valuable to some and harmful to others. The harm produced demands that price changes, especially increases, be announced and explained. The explanations, once required, needed institutions with the power and expertise to judge them.

In this period, the five vocabularies of our price legitimacy framework were all transformed along the way: authority migrated from setting prices to setting the rules of price communication; virtue was recast on the floor of the retail store in the moral crusade for a one-price system; necessity, born in the merchant’s letter, was mechanized and standardized into the cost justification; mechanism was electrified into the ticker, also widely available and public; and future value made its first recognizable appearance in the promises made and kept by the department store.

#### **Publicizing the price**

The telegraph was introduced in the United States in 1844, and merchants immediately adopted it for commercial purposes. Specifically, commercial intelligence, mainly in the form of prices, dominated telegraph traffic from its earliest years, and the network’s expansion in the 1840s and 1850s was largely supported by mercantile demand for market information[^3]. Studies have found measurable effects of this spread of price information during this period. Generations of merchants had built successful businesses by leveraging price differentials between distant markets for the same commodity (including our Dutch letter-writer from the previous post). However, these differentials narrowed sharply after the telegraph’s adoption. When the transatlantic cable was finally laid in 1866, the information lag between New York and London quotations fell from ten days to a few minutes[^4].

In addition to speed, the link between an object and its price was also broken during this time. Nowhere is this better explained than in William Cronon’s account of the Chicago grain trade. Before the 1850s, grain traveled in sacks, with each lot belonging to an identifiable farmer and with an individually negotiated price. The elevator, the grading system, and the Chicago Board of Trade changed all that. Wheat was standardized into graded categories, such as No. 2 spring, and the interpersonal relationship between buyer and seller was severed. Consequently, price was no longer attached to individual lots of grain; instead, it became a common value attached to *any* No. 2 spring wheat, which could travel rapidly by wire to anywhere on the continental US and be used for trading[^5].

Recall from the previous post that the price current had asserted, “this is what the market says prices are” on a weekly basis. The telegraph and the graded commodity modified the rhythm and decontextualized price to a continuous, placeless, ownerless value, to be updated frequently. Post 1867, the price was spit out of the stock ticker throughout the country, in brokers’ offices, hotel lobbies, and thousands of “bucket shops” where clerks and shopkeepers could bet on price movements without physically owning any wheat, not too different from how things are today[^6].

In the early modern period, the Tokugawa shogunate, unable to control the Dōjima rice price itself, spent ninety years trying to suppress its distribution by banning the hilltop flag signalers. In Chicago, the same battle was fought in the late 1880s. On an August morning in 1887, Abner Wright, the president of the Chicago Board of Trade, personally threw the instruments of two telegraph companies off the exchange floor. A few months later, he took an axe to mysterious cables running out of the building’s basement (they turned out to connect the exchange to the police and fire departments). Much like a luxury brand today tries to discourage purchases by consumers *[who don’t fit its target](https://statenews.com/article/2024/10/not-for-everyone-brandy-melvilles-stance-on-body-positivity?ct=content_open&cv=cbox_latest)*, the Board was fighting to cut the bucket shops off from its quotations, insisting that the price it produced was its property. Its long legal battle ended in 1905 with the U.S. Supreme Court concurring with this perspective[^7]. The Court upheld the Board’s right to control the immediate distribution of the quotation stream it collected, treating the continuously gathered information much like a trade secret while it remained subject to confidential distribution agreements. The key insight to take away is that by the 1880s, the price itself had become a valuable, contested asset carrying significant value, distinct from the goods it referenced, and its distribution had become contested.

For our purposes, the key consequence of the continuous public quotation is its effect on price changes. A haggled price cannot meaningfully “change,” because no two of its instances are ever the same. With a continuously reported price, in contrast, every price movement can be observed, tracked, stored, compared, and attributed. Every trader with access to a ticker or a newspaper can see that wheat rose two cents on Tuesday, and they can begin debating about why.

#### **Explaining the price**

During the 19th century, the commodity price current evolved into trade journals that provided detailed prices, typically covering a single industry or commodity. Examples included the *Iron Age*, the *American Grocer*, the *Railway Gazette*, the *Northwestern Miller*, and *Bradstreet’s*[^8]. But unlike the Amsterdam *prijscourant,* which simply listed prices, trade journals also provided context, reporting the week’s prices *and explaining them*, such as attributing the advance in bar iron to coke prices and wage settlements, the weakness in sugar to the Cuban crop, the firmness in freights to car shortages, and so on.

With respect to our framework, the trade press favored the necessity vocabulary. In the early modern period, a merchant justifying a price in a letter had to provide the context, one correspondent at a time, and his claims had to be consistent with the prices in the price current enclosed in the same envelope. Any narrative he provided had to be consistent across correspondents and over time. By the 1880s, this personal communication had been replaced by the trade journal, which provided a common context and price knowledge to everyone in the community and which resonates with today’s practices. (In parallel to the developments, two major credit-reporting agencies, R. G. Dun & Company, and J. M. Bradstreet, started verifying prices at scale, such that legitimizing price changes was essentially outsourced to trusted third parties[^9].)

#### **Maintaining a fixed price**

In the retail environment of the early modern period, prices were fluid, and virtually every consumer transaction was consummated through haggling, with the buyer’s attire, accent, persistence, and relationship with the seller all affecting the price paid. In the 19^(th) century, the price tag changed this dynamic. The price tag is actually a bundle of several different innovations, including a *fixed* price (the price only changes when the tag does), a *posted* price (the price is displayed rather than quoted), a *one-price policy* (every customer pays the same price), the *association* of the physical price with the individual product (every item is tagged), the *guarantee* implied in the tag (this is the price), and finally, and its *scaling* (this is how everything in the store is priced) in the department store.

Interestingly, each of these innovations was invented separately, in different places, over a long period. For instance, historians have documented a single price for staple goods in ordinary American country stores well before the Civil War. Similarly, A.T. Stewart was advertising a one-price policy at his New York dry-goods store decades before Rowland Macy and John Wanamaker made the system famous. In Paris, Aristide Boucicaut’s Bon Marché sold at marked prices from the 1850s on, and his business model, which included free entry, low margins, and the right of return, became the template for the modern department store[^10].

In 1673, two centuries before the American department store, the merchant Mitsui Takatoshi opened the Echigoya dry-goods shop in Edo and spelled out the motto that is still commonly found in Japan today: *genkin kakene nashi*, translated as “cash sales at fixed prices.” This policy was advertised on flyers and shop curtains, and was celebrated in print by the novelist Ihara Saikaku. Within fifteen years, it became the foundation of the Mitsui commercial empire (the shop survives even today as the Mitsukoshi department store) and was so iconic that Hokusai painted the storefront, along with clearly visible signboards, in his famous *Thirty-Six Views of Mount Fuji*[^11]. The fixed posted price, like the futures exchange at Dōjima, operated at scale in Tokugawa Japan, decades or even centuries before the West got credit for inventing it.

To Mitsui Takatoshi’s 17th-century idea, the 19th-century American reformers added scale, publicity, and an explicitly moral overtone. The one-price system had deep roots among Quaker merchants, whose religious tradition held that asking one customer more than another for the same good was dishonest. Rowland Macy, who was a Quaker, offered fixed prices in New York from 1858 onward[^12]. John Wanamaker, a devout Presbyterian operating in Philadelphia’s Quaker-influenced commercial culture, opened Oak Hall in 1861 under the slogan “one price and goods returnable.” He added the money-back guarantee, made the physical price tag standard practice at his Grand Depot department store from 1876, and advertised this business model relentlessly for half a century[^13]. Retailing legend has long credited Wanamaker with a theological syllogism: if everyone is equal before God, then everyone should be equal before the price. While this quote’s provenance cannot be definitively established, and it is best treated as folklore, it faithfully captures the explicitly moral basis that undergirded the one-price system in the United States[^14].

Viewed through our price legitimacy framework, the one-price system is a fusion of virtue and mechanism vocabularies. The virtue justification is grounded in the concept of equality. Every customer, regardless of status, bargaining skill, or prior relationship, pays the exact same price. There’s no price discrimination through interpersonal interactions. The mechanism justification is based on depersonalization in this framing, because it shifts the price communication task to an inanimate object, and the human agency in establishing price at the point of purchase is taken out of the equation.

Interestingly, the medieval just price from the early modern period was two-sided in the sense that both buyer and seller bore moral duties, and negotiated the price within a commonly accepted moral framework. The adoption of the price tag ended the need for negotiation and, with it, the need for any moral principle to govern the actions of the two parties. Buyers now had only one self-referential choice to make, either pay whatever the price tag said or move on. Those who favored a single price framed this change as good for buyers, and in many respects, it was indeed beneficial for them. However, it brought an end to the reciprocity and explicit, virtue-laden transactions that had fueled commerce for centuries. When, as modern consumers, we encounter the dual pressures of empowerment and powerlessness when we see the price of some item in a retail setting, for example, we are experiencing something that people in this era experienced for the first time. They could decide whether to buy something, but had no power to decide what the price should be.

The posted price also had unintended consequences that its moral champions did not foresee. It made the price change itself a public, advertisable event, and it split price-change communication into two asymmetric halves. When prices were reduced, the markdown became theater. For instance, Wanamaker ran the first White Sale in 1878, followed by a number of promotional innovations, including the clearance sale, the anniversary sale, and the bargain counter. By the 1890s, the manufacturer’s coupon and the trading stamp had turned price decreases into celebratory events, heavily promoted occasions with an entire communicative repertoire of their own[^15]. In contrast, the price increase was accompanied by silence. Price tags were replaced quietly, with no one the wiser (except the most vigilant and observant shoppers).

Two further developments during this period deserve brief attention. First, the department store stopped justifying each price on its own merits and instead let the institutional aura (or what we’d call the store price image), represented by things like price guarantees, returns, delivery, curated quality assortments, and so on, vouch for their prices. Rather than the value vocabulary, which promises that today’s higher prices will fund tomorrow’s improvements, this was a promise of *continuing institutional assurance*, a call to judge prices by the brand that stands behind it[^16]. Second, the chain store scaled the scope of the posted price, introducing price uniformity across the country. As an example, A&P’s Economy Store format, introduced in 1912, standardized a stripped-down cash-and-carry model built around low posted prices. It helped the chain expand to roughly 1,600 stores by 1915 and many thousands during the following decade[^17].

#### **Public prices as state infrastructure in Meiji Japan**

After more than a century of attempts by the Tokugawa shogunate to suppress the flag-and-telescope networks that relayed the Dōjima rice price across Japan (discussed in the previous piece in detail), the Meiji state reversed this approach from 1868 onwards. It built, owned, and operated the medium through which prices were transmitted. The construction of the telegraph infrastructure began almost immediately after this. The first line between Tokyo and Yokohama began operating in 1869-70, and within a few years, the telegraph network extended all the way to Osaka and beyond.[^18] The rice and stock exchanges were re-chartered under national regulations in the 1870s, and the Osaka rice price that brokers had once relayed by flag in defiance of the shogunate’s bans now traveled instantly and legally over government wires. This shift produced measurable economic effects. The spread of the telegraph and government policy toward the network accelerated the integration of Japan’s rice markets from the Meiji period onward, with the Tokyo and Osaka markets moving essentially as one by the 1910s[^19].

An authority that cannot stop a price from circulating faces a choice about how it wants to deal with the circulation. It can choose to be either an adversary or a regulator. The Tokugawa state adopted an adversarial role for nearly a century without success. The Meiji state took the opposite approach, choosing to serve as the regulator by making price information a public infrastructure.

#### **Dealing with concerns about the credibility of public prices**

By the final quarter of the 19^(th) century, prices across industries were becoming public information, whether transmitted through wire, reported weekly in the trade press, or tagged on store merchandise. This spread also tempted sellers to benefit from the structural legitimacy of public prices by maintaining one price in public while charging a different, higher, or lower price in private.

A milder version of this price adjustment was evident in the example of the list price and its accompanying discount sheet. In industries such as books, drugs, and machinery, manufacturers published stable, widely circulated list prices, and communicated actual market prices through separately circulated printed schedules of trade discounts, quoted as chains of percentages off list (“50 and 10 and 5 off”)[^20]. The practical genius of this two-layer price structure was in allowing sellers to frequently change the prices customers paid, while the printed price remained stable. Over time, the two prices drifted apart; the discounts became large, selective, and secret; and the public price lost its meaning. Today, these ideas are well known in B2B pricing through concepts such as the pocket price waterfall, account-level incentive structures, and revenue leaks. And, like today, they bred cynicism among buyers and, worse still, led to the Standard Oil fiasco of the 1870s.

#### **The Standard Oil Case**

While the public infrastructure for communicating prices was being painstakingly built, the most successful industrial enterprise of the age was being built by bypassing it. The case of Standard Oil is instructive, both for its significance at the time and for its impact on what came later. Standard Oil’s rise can be traced, at least in part, to the so-called railroad rebate, the substantial hidden gap between the tariff that was publicly printed and what the favored shipper actually paid.

A notorious part of the scheme was the South Improvement Company of 1872, in which three railroads in Cleveland and a small group of refining companies led by John D. Rockefeller were involved in a secret pact. Under their agreement, the railroad companies would significantly raise published prices for transporting crude oil and then provide secret rebates to the participating refiners. As a specific example, the posted rate for transporting crude oil from northwestern Pennsylvania to New York was raised to \$2.56 per barrel, and the refiners were concurrently offered a \$1.06 rebate on this inflated rate. Compounding the trickery, participants were to receive *drawbacks* on their competitors' shipments, meaning that they would get a cut of the high price their competitors paid for shipping their oil.[^21] These conspirators subverted the public tariff to benefit themselves and manipulate the market in their favor, while penalizing others.

When details about this scheme became publicly known in February 1972, all hell broke loose.[^22] The railroad companies were raided, there were boycotts and mass protests. Within weeks, the Pennsylvania state legislature had revoked the South Improvement Company’s charter. While all this was going on, Rockefeller was using the scheme as leverage, threatening his competitors with the high prices. Within six weeks, 22 of Cleveland’s refiners sold out to him in the incident famously remembered as the “Cleveland Massacre.”[^23] In the settlement that followed in March 1972, the producers who had been exploited by the cabal ensured that, from then on, the shipping rates would apply “on a basis of perfect equality to all shippers, producers, and refiners.” There would be no sneaky rebates, drawbacks, or discriminatory incentives to favor one producer over another. Importantly, rates could no longer be changed without ninety days’ notice to the President of the Producers Union.[^24] This turned out to be the precursor of the more influential Interstate Commerce Act that came later, including two of its core principles: published rates applicable to all, and mandatory advance notice of price changes.

However, not every railroad followed this law and the sneaky rebates continued for another three decades. Throughout this period, Standard Oil did not acknowledge or explain its actions in any public venue. In fact, it used a variety of methods to operate under the radar such as unaffiliated partners and coded telegrams. When faced with criticism form the public, it did not respond, its attitude reflected in the (probably embellished) remark attributed to the railroad magnate William H. Vanderbilt, “the public be damned.”[^25]

An expose by the journalist Ida Tarbell carefully documented the rebates and drawbacks employed by Standard Oil over a lengthy period of time, that eventually compelled the federal government to sue Standard Oil under the Sherman Act in 1906. In 1911, the Supreme Court ordered the trust to be broken up into 30 separate companies, bringing this saga of price manipulation to an end.[^26] Much like the quasi-satirical tulip pamphlets of 1637 provided the primary data regarding tulip prices in the early modern period, the journalist’s angry, and presumably biased account of Standard Oil’s pricing strategy became the primary source for this case study. No information from within Standard Oil was ever provided to counter the negative portrait that had been painted by its adversaries.

The lesson for us here is that secret, selective discounting undermines the legitimacy of every posted price in the system when it is offered without clear guidelines or pricing logic. In the case of Standard Oil, once shippers came to believe the published tariff was meaningless, the entire mechanism vocabulary of railroad pricing collapsed, taking with it the political tolerance that had given railroads the latitude to set prices. Later, the major railroads supported the Elkins Act of 1903, which made any departure from a filed tariff a crime and penalized both the railroad granting a rebate and the shipper receiving it. By backing the law, the railroads sought to give their published prices the force and legitimacy of state authority[^27].

#### **How to change public price legitimately**

Once the challenge of hidden, opaque price shifts from public standards was identified and fixed, the next issue was how to legitimately change public prices. The answer was worked out in the railroad industry during the 1880s and 1890s, perhaps because of its three prominent features: (1) they were the big businesses, similar in social influence to today’s Mag 7 (2) their customers had often literally no other option to ship their goods, and (3) their pricing was perceived to be opaque and illegible by its customers[^28].

There were structural reasons for the opacity and illegibility of railroad prices. A railroad’s costs were overwhelmingly fixed and joint, shared across thousands of routes and commodities, which meant that costs were hard to allocate and could not be simply marked up to determine the rate for any particular shipment. Rates were therefore set on “value of service,” or what the traffic will bear. From the shipper’s perspective, the result was a rate structure that seemed arbitrary and discriminatory, with rates varying significantly between routes, commodities, and shippers, not to mention the discriminatory rebate described earlier. Interestingly, one important part of Congress’s remedy in 1887 was communicative, in the sense that railroads were required to publish, post, file, and adhere to their rates. The Act also imposed substantive restrictions on discrimination, rebates, pooling, and unreasonable charges.

#### **The public price increase as a proceeding**

Before tracking the law’s next steps, we should consider two movements in economic thought, running simultaneously and in opposite directions, because they help explain why the proceedings took the form they did. The first movement dismantled the core idea, stemming from Adam Smith’s concept of a natural price, that a good’s cost of production determines its price. Railroad economists Dionysius Lardner in 1850 and Arthur Hadley in 1885 worked out that in a network with high fixed and joint costs, there is simply no such thing as “the cost” of carrying one particular shipment. Individual rates must be set based on demand, reflecting the value of the service to different users. In the 1840s, the French engineer Jules Dupuit had already built the formal welfare case for charging different users different prices for the same bridge or railway, establishing the groundwork for a theory of price discrimination. From the 1870s through the 1890s, economists overturned the century-old doctrine that a good’s price gravitates toward its cost of production, showing instead that price is set by what buyers will pay for one more unit. The marginal revolution weakened the classical tendency to explain price primarily through production costs. It brought marginal utility and demand into price theory, while the emerging neoclassical synthesis treated price as the product of both demand and supply[^29].

In parallel, the second movement built the machinery that made cost the only publicly acceptable justification. Specifically, industrial cost accounting emerged as a formal discipline in the 1880s, with texts like Garcke and Fells’ *Factory Accounts* and, later, the standard costing framework of the scientific management movement. Techniques that had previously existed within individual firms became codified in textbooks, integrated into managerial systems, and increasingly standardized. This made “our costs” something that could be calculated, documented, compared, and managed. Henry Carter Adams, appointed the Interstate Commerce Commission’s statistician in 1887, helped construct its statistical and accounting system. After the Hepburn Act of 1906 strengthened the ICC’s authority, the Commission imposed more rigorous uniform accounting requirements on the carriers[^30].

These two movements together generated the great tension of the industrial age for every pricing communicator that remains unresolved to this day: *prices came to be set through value and demand, but could be justified far more effectively through costs*. The necessity vocabulary became so popular not because of its analytic strength, but because of its perceived (or real) verifiability. Given a choice between an honest claim that couldn’t be verified and a dubious one that could, audiences chose the latter, and centuries later, still continue to do so.

#### **Federal regulation of rate changes, 1887–1910**

Post-1887, the statutes that were introduced tightened the communicative screws one step at a time. The Elkins Act strengthened the legal force of the filed tariff. It increased penalties for departures from published rates and made participating shippers and carriers liable for rebate arrangements. The posted price was now legally binding, arguably the strongest form of price commitment. The Hepburn Act of 1906 lengthened the notice period to thirty days and empowered the Commission to prescribe maximum rates upon complaint, although only after an increase had taken effect and its effects had played out. The Mann-Elkins Act of 1910 allowed the Commission to suspend a proposed rate change before it took effect, for up to ten months, while it investigated. In any hearing involving an increased rate, “the burden of proof is on the carrier to show that the increased rate is just and reasonable”[^31]. A contemporary analyst astutely pointed out that the Act made all rate increases presumptively unreasonable until the seller proved otherwise[^32].

Thus, in 1886, an American railroad could change any rate whenever it wanted, keep it secret, and implement it instantly. But by 1910, things had changed dramatically. A rate increase had to be published in advance, filed with a federal commission, and, if challenged, suspended and proven reasonable by the railroad. Within a period of 24 years, raising price was transformed from something done privately and at will by the seller into something that was a public action, requiring due notice, explanation, and approval. A vast regulatory structure had to be built to manage this process across the economy. As an example, in July 1910 alone, more than 15,000 tariffs announcing announcing rate advances were filed in just the Northeast and Midwest US alone.[^33]

Louis Brandeis, who played a significant role in the Eastern Rate case that is discussed next, supplied this era’s memorable epigraph in 1914 when he said, “Sunlight is said to be the best of disinfectants; electric light the most efficient policeman.”[^34] This, then is the evolution of the authority vocabulary in the industrial age. In previous eras, whoever was in charge simply stated “This is the price” and everyone accepted the price. But now, the authority was responsible for establishing the framework for changing and communicating prices. In effect, it said, “Here are the rules you must follow in changing and announcing your price.”

#### **The Eastern Rate Case**

In 1910, the major eastern trunk lines filed for a general advance in freight rates of approximately 10%, arguing that wages had increased, materials cost more, and net earnings were inadequate to attract the capital that the growing traffic required. In response, the shippers organized, hired a Boston attorney named Louis D. Brandeis, and audited the railroads’ necessity justification. Drawing on the testimony of the efficiency engineer Harrington Emerson, who estimated that improving operating efficiency could save the railroads as much as \$300 million annually, Brandeis argued before the Interstate Commerce Commission that the railroads’ rising costs were not an external necessity but rather the product of their own inefficient management. He further argued that adopting so-called “scientific management” could save them a million dollars a day, making the price increase unnecessary. This claim made national headlines, ignited the “efficiency craze,” and in February 1911, the Commission denied the proposed advances[^35]. The Eastern rate case was one of the first major rate increases tested under the ICC’s new pre-effective suspension procedure, in the sense that the sellers’ necessity justification was probed line by line in an adversarial proceeding and then rejected. Cost-based price increases have been vulnerable to the same scrutiny ever since, whereby the seller’s rising costs are reclassified by the buyer or the audience as their own volitional choices.

#### **Public prices in British India**

Another interesting case from this period comes from late 19th-century British India, where the colonial government built elaborate infrastructure to communicate the prices and their meaning in the other direction, from the market to the authorities. Following the catastrophic famines of the 1860s and 1870s in India, the Famine Commission of 1880 and the provincial Famine Codes, drafted from 1883, established one of the world’s first formal early-warning systems, using food grain prices as the primary sensor. District officers across the subcontinent regularly reported staple prices, which were later consolidated into the long-running official series *Prices and Wages in India*. The codes specified graded conditions, scarcity and famine, in which sustained abnormally high prices, alongside crop, rainfall, migration, and mortality data, required the state to respond in an escalating way, from vigilance to engaging in public works at subsistence wages, whose turnout measured the depth of distress to full relief[^36]. Grain prices served as a warning signal, forcing the state to act. Today, we see many versions of this concept, from indexed pensions and contractual escalation clauses to the famine intensity scales still used by humanitarian agencies, for which the Indian Famine Codes were an important early antecedent[^37].

Unfortunately, this infrastructure also had a (very) dark side. The same telegraph and railway network that carried prices to the state carried them to grain merchants, enabling them to coordinate hoarding and price increases across famine-stricken districts. These led to brutal activities, such as callously moving grain from starving regions with millions of desperate, dying Indians to richer ones (or even abroad), where consumers had the money and were willing to pay. Worth noting, other scholars who have closely examined Western India’s price and famine data have taken a different position, crediting the Indian railways and the state’s price-reporting apparatus with reducing famine mortality over time. Whichever side of the argument you land on, it is clear that in this instance, prices functioned as the conduit for transmitting, perceiving, and administering information about the crisis and responses to it[^38].

#### **The third audience**

The observant reader will recognize that each post in this series has provided one insightful new idea or concept for the reader’s consideration and potential further development. The discussion of pricing in the medieval world introduced the concept of narrative mismatch, while the early modern world introduced the idea of *verifiability*. In this respect, the industrial age’s contribution, discernible throughout the discussion so far, is what I will call *the third audience*.

Every price communication has always had a *first audience*, the specific target who benefits directly from the information. Most often, this is the buyer who uses the price in their buying decision. When the price was publicized and the communication process was formalized with a set of rules, there was a new, *second audience*. This comprised not only the market at large but also specific constituents with an interest in the price, such as potential or ex-customers, competitors, investors, and intermediaries.

To these two audiences, the industrial age added a *third audience*, consisting of various institutional entities, such as commissions, courts, legislatures, and the press. They were not parties to the commercial exchange, but they had the ability and the standing to judge the legitimacy of a price change. The third audience performed (and continues to perform) a number of functions, depending on the context. The regulator could suspend or reject the proposed price change; the court could enforce or void the rules governing the price change; and the journalist could draw the public’s attention and arouse their emotions regarding the price change. In effect, the third audience functioned as referees, managing the commercial interaction between buyer and seller, preventing conflict before it occurred, and minimizing it when it did.

Today, the third audience is entrenched in the price communication process and is vital to legitimizing price change. But to the authoritative and expert constituents, there is a new entity, which we can loosely call “influencer,” who operates through various media and forms, and serves as both an intermediary between those who communicate price changes and those who make sense of it, as well as a conduit presenting a certain way of understanding the price change. Today’s third audience provides the mechanism to verify the seller's claims about the price and, in some cases, may even authenticate this information. Thus, even though dealing with the third audience entails costs in terms of effort and potential adverse effects, avoiding them is not an option for today’s sellers, and remaining under the radar is a thing of the past.

#### **Lessons from the industrial age for today’s pricing decision makers**

The first, and perhaps most significant, lesson is the aura of rigidity and discipline that accompanies a price change, and especially a price increase. The Interstate Commerce Act of 1887 made it compulsory for railroads to publish their rates and to provide 10 days' notice before increasing a price. A price reduction also had to be published, but did not require advance notice.

Many of today’s sellers would benefit from adopting greater discipline and openness in communicating their prices. The current sneaky shrinkflation ethos, which has pervaded many industries, is harmful to the long-term reputation and credibility of price communication mechanisms. Calling price increases “price updates” doesn’t fool anyone and doesn’t benefit the seller. Instead, it makes the seller look crooked, yet far too many companies have adopted this approach of price communication as the default.

A second lesson is that when a seller tries to justify a price increase on cost grounds, it should be prepared to provide supporting evidence to a skeptical audience. Back in 1910, when the eastern railroads asked for higher rates, Lord Brandeis argued that they should first try to cut costs with more efficient management. He also asked that operating costs be documented and analyzed more closely so that opportunities for savings would be easier to identify. Customers respond with the same mindset today when sellers claim their costs have gone up. Costs that are included in the explanation are challenged, and once which are not are questioned. Just as important as the question of what the new price should be is the question of how the new price will be explained in an authentic and convincing way to a resistant audience.

Third, opaque discounts given in underhanded ways hurt the business, and when they are common, the entire industry. The rebate era showed what happens when the published tariff does not apply to large or favored buyers. Opaque enterprise discounts from an unrealistic list price can create a similar problem. (Think of a furniture store that always has a sale of 75% off running). So can personalized offers that are hard for other customers to understand. While these practices are not unethical in the same way as the railroad rebates, they can, and do, raise similar doubts about whether the posted price is credible. The large railroad companies supported the Elkins Act of 1903 because it gave them a way out from the rebate pricing model and allowed them to hold one another accountable for their published tariffs. Interestingly, the Pennsylvania Railroad was one of this law’s principal proponents. For pricing practitioners today, the message is that when the underlying pricing model is suspect, tweaking the price communication approach will do little to help.

Fourth, price communications, even when targeted at customers, should always take into account the possibility of broader public scrutiny. Scrutiny by the third audience increases the credibility of price claims by allowing them to be assessed by less-informed and more partisan recipients. Yet a price change notification written solely to pass legal approval often becomes vague and evasive, and customers notice this to the company’s detriment. The merchant proponents of “one price” pricing policies in the industrial age provide a useful model of what to do. Their public promise, their rigid adherence to the one-price rule, and their repeated creative advertising were all meant to bolster the credibility of their prices. Price communication is most effective when the public explanation of prices matches the company’s pricing practices.
