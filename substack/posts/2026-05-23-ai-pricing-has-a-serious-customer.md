##### ***Summary.*** Virtually every pricing strategy we associate with SaaS offerings, from per-seat subscriptions and free tiers to volume discounts for big enterprise customers, was developed during a 40-year period when the incremental cost of adding a user was close to zero. But many AI products have significant incremental costs. AI pricing experts are working overtime to design new structures that make economic sense, including tighter free tiers, usage caps, metered billing, outcome-based fees, labor replacement benchmarks, and valuing AI agent labor. While most of the discussion has focused on the seller’s perspective, this post examines the other half of the challenge: the customer’s response to these AI price structures. In this post, I examine six AI price structures and consider customers’ responses to each. The AI products that succeed over the coming years will be those that deliberately pay attention to customer responses and adopt customer-congruent price structures, rather than treating pricing as an afterthought, a communication issue, or a PR issue.

### AI’s Challenges With Moving from Entrenched SaaS Price Structures

As AI products have taken center stage and been widely adopted over the past couple of years, much thought has gone into pricing them. Companies have run pricing experiments, often quite publicly, and writers in the field have generated hundreds of Substack articles and podcast hours apiece, devoting considerable attention to working out the right price structures. Two discussions of AI pricing that I have found particularly insightful recently are those by [Michael Mansard](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/ai-credit-monetization-from-hype-concrete-blueprint-compass-mansard-e24be/) and [Vin Vashishta](https://vinvashishta.substack.com/p/your-ai-pricing-problems-are-actually). These and other writers have identified core differences between the economics of SaaS and AI products, and have proposed both strategic and tactical pricing responses. However, what is missing from much of this discussion is the customer. Attention has been almost entirely on the seller’s economics and the price structures the AI company might adopt, with very little on how their customers will perceive and respond to those structures, or what that might mean for which price structures might work and which ones won’t.

Among others, insightful pieces on AI pricing have argued that [AI is upending SaaS pricing](https://a16z.com/podcast/ai-is-upending-saas-pricing/), that the underlying problems with AI pricing are [really about the business model, not pricing strategy](https://vinvashishta.substack.com/p/your-ai-pricing-problems-are-actually) per se, and that even [usage-based pricing is fraught with risk](https://marketledgrowth.substack.com/p/ai-is-breaking-saas-pricing-and-turning) for AI products. Almost all of these discussions focus on these issues from the seller’s perspective, considering unit economics and cost structures of AI products, whether price structures supporting usage-based, outcome-based, or hybrid approaches make sense, and how to construct credit-based price structures. See Pangram’s pricing below for a classic example of credit-based tiered pricing.

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/8a179e3c-9447-45e1-b780-01651e26bb64_728x720.png)

Pangram’s credit-based price structure, May 2026. Minimal free tier, credit conversion to customer value is clearly communicated.

What hasn’t been adequately considered is what customers who use AI products and have to pay for them think about these shifts, and which new price structures they will accept (even if it is reluctantly) or reject outright. Customers spent four decades being trained in SaaS pricing structures grounded in abundance. They developed work routines and everyday habits that were shaped by the affordances of these generous price structures. Now, they are being asked, often abruptly, not only to work with new, threatening technology but also to deal with diametrically opposed price structures in its place that are grounded in scarcity. The complaint by user TerryL in OpenAI’s developer community is a prototypical example of having to deal with a scarcity-based price structure.

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/93dc989a-78ef-499f-b0e5-6556ac60b957_1192x836.png)

Source: OpenAI Developer Community, Accessed May 2026

Every significant price change a buyer encounters passes through a perceptual and evaluative psychological process before a decision is made. Individual customers or procurement teams form reference price points anchored on prior contracts and benchmark deals. They judge the new price structure against norms of fairness shaped by the seller’s stated reasons and by competitors’ prices for comparable value. End users also feel the effects of the new price structure to the extent it changes how they use the product (through things like usage caps that may throttle them mid-task or credit meters that count down as they work), and their reactions may play an outsized role in the customer’s renewal decisions These issues have been studied for well over 50 years in marketing and customer psychology, and this accumulated knowledge provides useful insights for considering how customers might respond to new AI price structures.

This post seeks to bring the customer back into the conversation about AI price structures. After briefly considering why AI companies are moving away from well-entrenched, familiar SaaS pricing models, I examine six emerging AI price structures from the customer’s perspective, using consumer psychology as a lens to understand how each is likely to be perceived.

### A Customer-Centric Perspective on Six Challenges with Pricing AI Products

For more than forty years, the marginal cost of serving one additional user of a software product was close to zero. However, as vigorous discussions on AI pricing challenges have shown, this assumption no longer holds. Every query a customer sends consumes non-trivial computational resources that the seller has to pay for, and the cost is incurred at the moment of use rather than being absorbed into a fixed cost base. Stated differently, the variable costs of every act of customer consumption (and value derivation) are significant. Obviously, price structures that were effective during the lengthy era of zero marginal costs, from per-seat subscriptions to free tiers to flat-rate enterprise contracts, no longer make sense under this new cost structure.

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/82277d42-202f-4bad-b9f2-cb97c81c794e_869x811.png)

Source: Chris Hawkes, Cursor is done, pricing is unsustainable, YouTube video

Companies pricing AI offerings have responded by experimenting with new price structures, often in full public view. Every price change is quickly noticed and debated endlessly on X by customers and social media gurus. Cursor’s mid-2025 move from [a flat \$20-a-month Pro plan to a credit-based system](https://www.fintechweekly.com/magazine/articles/cursor-pricing-change-user-backlash-refund) is a useful example. Under the new structure, the old \$20 “all-you-can-use” fee now buys \$20 worth of API credits at the underlying model’s cost. For customers, it meant going from all-you-can-eat to rationing. See the complaint by a Cursor customer above.

The change made economic sense for the company, since the old plan could not cover the high costs of serving customers, especially heavy users. But it also collided with the expectations customers had formed under the flat-rate plan, leading to a public backlash and a [CEO apology within weeks](https://cursor.com/blog/june-2025-pricing). While AI pricing experts and companies experimenting with new structures have put a lot of thought and effort into cost recovery and margin protection, they haven’t yet sufficiently considered how customers will perceive and respond to those structures. I will do so next, considering six widely-discussed AI pricing challenges.

#### *1. Avoiding the free tier & low-priced, basic tier is financially essential for AI companies, but psychologically agonizing for customers.*

In classic SaaS pricing models, the free tier was easy to justify financially. A free incremental user costs very little to serve, so companies use free access to attract users, cultivate habits, and convert some small percentage of them to paid plans. Companies could make money even with users in the lowest-priced basic tiers. The free tier and the cheap basic tier became workhorses of many Saas pricing strategies. For example, [less than 10% of free-tier users converted](https://medium.com/@sohail_saifii/i-calculated-the-real-cost-of-free-tier-users-saas-companies-are-lying-dea34e757cf0) to any paid tier in many traditional SaaS offerings, revealing how extensively companies were willing to subsidize users when the marginal cost of a free user was near zero. But this logic no longer works for AI products because each free query still consumes costly computing resources, and the costs can add up quickly, affecting the company’s income statement.

Customers’ expectations, however, remain anchored in SaaS pricing tiers because they’ve been trained to think of software as something they can access for free or at a modest monthly fee. The strongly encoded “free” reference point from SaaS makes free-tier restrictions for AI products psychologically difficult for customers to accept. Limiting free access to a few prompts, or removing it entirely, is like performing a hard reset on the customer’s dominant reference price. Moving from free to paid is experienced as a loss, and the perceived loss is usually larger than the appreciation customers felt when the free access was first offered[^1]. This also applies to customers in lower-priced basic tiers. They felt entitled (“I am paying what they’re asking for”) and expected to use the product as much as they reasonably wanted after paying the fee.

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/fdad7d34-9ad8-4fd5-b71c-6e027543e4ca_1571x1012.png)

HeyGen’s credit-based price structure, May 2026. Minimal free tier, credit conversion to customer value requires clickthrough & cognitive effort.

To a certain extent, this helps explain why the major AI companies have been cautious about restricting or removing the free tier, even when the math doesn’t work. Offerings like ChatGPT and Google Gemini continue to offer significant free tiers[^2], absorbing the expense for now, and, in turn, conditioning users of other smaller or niche AI apps to expect the same. As of now, many adopters see AI as an always-on digital utility rather than the scarce, precious computational service that it is. However, it should be clear that this mismatch between customer valuation and the AI product’s cost structure is not a sustainable solution, especially as the gloss of novelty wears off for consumers and investor tolerance wanes.

#### *2. Power users are the hardest customers to move out of all-inclusive price structures.*

The traditional SaaS instinct is to prioritize power users because of the many benefits they bring to the business. They validate the product’s economic value, exhibit the highest engagement, demonstrate greater psychological and behavioral loyalty, serve as internal advocates for customer renewals, and drive external use cases to bring in new business. In AI contexts, however, the power user may often be the least profitable user (or, likely, the most loss-making). In this world, heavy customer use directly translates into heavy inference costs, and the customer who gets the most value from the product will also cost the most to serve, often far more than the subscription fee covers. This creates an obvious temptation to move these users to metered pricing or implement usage caps as quickly as possible. Push comes to shove, a cruder alternative approach may be to raise prices to bring them in line with costs.

Here, too, consumer psychology suggests this may be difficult to pull off in practice. Research on the flat-rate bias shows that customers often prefer all-inclusive pricing even when pay-per-use would cost them less[^3]. In this research, flat-rate users paid 7-15% more than they would have under a pay-per-use structure. They value protection against unexpectedly high bills and dislike the feeling of watching a meter run while they work and tracking usage. Not as relevant for AI products (but definitely relevant for SaaS), these customers also tend to overestimate how much they will use the product, which makes the flat rate feel more valuable than it actually is.

Power users, in particular, are vulnerable to these effects. They use the product frequently and build their workflows around unrestricted access[^4]. For them, a move to metered pricing feels like a constraint on how they use the product. The freedom and predictability that made the product valuable in the first place are no longer available.

This is a significant pricing conundrum the AI industry has yet to resolve. The customers whose use is most expensive to the company are also the customers most attached to all-inclusive plans, and the price structure that fixes the high variable costs problem is also the move that those customers are most likely to interpret as a reduction in the product’s value rather than as a reasonable, even necessary, cost correction to maintain economic sustainability[^5].

#### *3. Transparent input cost deflation can create customer concerns about unfairness and derail price structure management.*

Unit token costs for frontier AI models have fallen by roughly an order of magnitude per year for several years running, with no clear sign of stopping. The Stanford AI Index documented that the cost to query GPT-3.5-level performance fell more than 280x between late 2022 and late 2024[^6]. This is good for unit economics but also creates a pricing challenge. If an AI company prices its products based on today’s costs, a competitor with lower costs (and deeper pockets) may undercut it. If it prices based on expected lower future costs, the company may lose too much money and not survive long enough to see those lower future costs.

The customer-side problem is just as challenging. Research on price fairness shows that customers track the relationship between a seller’s costs and its prices, and judge unchanged prices as unfair when costs are visibly falling[^7]. This problem is particularly visible in the AI product space. Model providers have been [publicly announcing lower token prices](https://www.reuters.com/business/google-expected-court-coders-consumers-io-conference-2026-05-19/), and users can often see the costs of the underlying inputs more clearly than they could in traditional software. For example, aggregator sites like [Artificial Analysis publish cost-per-token comparisons](https://artificialanalysis.ai/) across providers that get updated weekly. This is an unusually high level of cost transparency. Thus, while a customer may not know the AI company’s entire cost structure, they can still form a general idea of the impact of lower input costs on prices and whether those prices are changing proportionally.

This puts AI companies in a bind[^8]. They have to manage a price structure in a highly transparent environment, where their informed customers (also likely to be power users) can track the cost reductions they are enjoying in real time. Thus, while holding prices steady may be the financially smart move, especially if the company is adding capabilities or absorbing other rising costs, customers will naturally ask why the falling input costs are not reflected in lower prices on their bills[^9].

Companies that visibly share input cost reductions by lowering prices, or, alternatively, clearly explain why they haven’t cut prices to customers, can build trust for future pricing changes. In contrast, companies that maintain prices or adopt opaque price structures without a credible explanation may raise questions and provoke outrage. The customer may keep paying, but the relationship will become brittle. The next structural change in the company’s pricing is likely to be judged (harshly) against this memory of costs going down while prices stayed the same. We are still early in the effort to figure out the best practices in AI pricing to manage this cost transparency, but it is clear that the approaches that succeed will be those that carefully consider customer response.

#### *4. Outcome-based pricing changes what the customer thinks they are buying.*

A recurring proposal in AI pricing commentary is that AI products should be priced based on outcomes that are relevant to customers. Depending on the application, it could be per closed support ticket, per qualified lead, per booked meeting, per merged pull request, per recovered insurance claim, and so on. The straightforward logic behind this idea is that outcome-based pricing shifts the customer’s attention away from tokens and usage units and bases pricing on measures of the economic value delivered by the AI product’s use. See Fin’s outcome-based pricing below.

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/5d5f47fe-6b46-4e1e-8f77-b6c4e5bae515_920x720.png)

Fin’s outcome-based pricing, May 2026.

However, this simplifies matters too much. A key issue with outcome-based price structures (in AI applications or elsewhere) is that the specific usage context significantly impacts their suitability. The same AI product can produce dramatically different outcomes in different cases. Consider two customers who sign up to deploy human-sounding AI agents for customer service. However, one customer has a high-performing call center already in place, whereas the other company’s service operations are dysfunctional and struggling. If the AI company charges them per resolved ticket, the same product is likely to produce dramatically different bills for the two customers.

What’s more, the differences will be difficult to attribute to the AI rather than to the underlying operation. To the extent that the outcome depends on factors resident in the customer, such as the strength of its brand, data, employees, processes, organizational culture, or prior investments, rather than the AI product being deployed, outcome-based pricing becomes hard to justify and implement. Even in well-instrumented digital settings, attribution research has shown that assigning credit across multiple contributing factors produces substantial and persistent disagreement[^10]. The problem with AI products is sharper still, because the customer’s contribution to the outcome is often invisible to the seller, and consequently, outcome pricing can quickly become an attribution problem. The more the outcome is produced jointly with the customer, the more the price structure can feel like the AI company is claiming a share of value that the customer believes it created itself.

This helps explain why outcome-based pricing works best in narrow categories and for discrete, hard-to-dispute events. Examples of these types of applications include loans closed, lawsuits won, rental agreements signed, and claims recovered, all with clear boundaries and binary outcomes. This is not the case for many AI applications. Even in customer support, defining a “resolution” can be difficult, and gray areas quickly become sources of disagreement[^11].

#### *5. The labor replacement price structure has to deal with the challenge of customer categorization.*

Another idea discussed in AI pricing is that products replacing knowledge work should be priced relative to the cost of the human labor they substitute for, not relative to comparable software. Here’s a 2023 post from [Dan Nguyen-Hu, who has called this “staff augmentation-based pricing,”](https://dannguyenhuu.substack.com/p/the-price-is-ai-ght-a-short-discussion)[^12] arguing that AI agents performing work historically done by employees should be priced as a fraction (perhaps a quarter or third) of the fully loaded costs of those employees.

The legal platform Harvey Assistant offers a useful illustration. It is reportedly priced at \$12,000 to \$14,400 per lawyer per year, based on the argument that the tool replaces associate time and should be priced accordingly[^13]. The economic logic is reasonable. If an AI product performs work that substitutes for expensive professional labor, the seller should be able to charge a meaningful percentage of what the professional’s time would have cost. This provides a compelling narrative for the quoted price, and Harvey’s per-lawyer figure (a small fraction of a first-year associate’s fully loaded cost of roughly \$300,000) demonstrates that customers can be brought along with this logic.

On the other hand, this argument should be persuasive to the customer. The customer should believe that the professional will indeed be substituted fully, and be willing to take the risk to do so. In the legal example, if the law firm retains the associate, Harvey Assistant hasn’t really saved them the Associate’s salary. Thus, the same AI product can command very different prices depending on whether the customer views it as “software” or a “labor substitute,” and the extent to which they see it as a substitute rather than a complement. Supporting the complement argument, recent research on generative AI in customer support found that AI assistance increased productivity by 14% on average, with the gains concentrated among less experienced workers and almost no effect on experienced workers[^14]. The economic value of saving labor costs by reducing headcount versus still incurring those costs while freeing up employee time to do other things is significantly different, and these dueling categorizations will affect customers’ acceptance of the price quote. It’s worth noting that this is still an issue in flux and is likely to evolve as AI technology advances and is adopted for different purposes.

At present, most enterprise AI products are still evaluated by customers through a SaaS lens, and we are still quite far from the labor-replacement approach. To achieve this price structure, the seller will not only have to demonstrate that all the tasks associated with a particular role are fully replaced, but also manage the risk to customers of fully replacing human labor with AI. And finally, the not-insignificant ethical, social, and political ramifications of making these arguments need to be taken into account.

#### *6. Agents turn the pricing question into the thorny one of valuing the delegation to an AI.*

The shift from AI as a tool to AI as an agent creates the thorniest pricing problem among those covered in this post. Traditional software pricing assumes that the customer purchases access to a product and then decides how to use it. This is reflected in core SaaS structures such as per-seat pricing (where a seat refers to the one occupied by a human), per-query pricing (which assumes a human is the one querying), and so on. Even in AI, per-token pricing assumes that the units consumed by the customer are a reasonable proxy for the value received, and outcome-based pricing assumes that the outcome can be defined and attributed with some confidence.

AI agents expose the limits of each of these assumptions. An agent can decide what steps to take in solving a problem, call other tools, interact with other systems, and act on the customer’s behalf. Thus, from buying software for use, the customer has now shifted to delegating work to a system that can operate without their direct involvement, like hiring a contractor to do a job.

Customers have different, longstanding pricing models for tools and for human labor. But their experience with pricing software that behaves like a human is minimal. As discussed in the previous section, how customers categorize the AI agent, whether as a tool, a contractor, a substitute employee, a managed service, or something else, will determine how much they will be willing to pay and the nature of the value they will expect to receive.

This is why the challenge of pricing agents cannot be solved by simply choosing a billing unit, because each unit has its limitations. For instance, charging on a per-task basis raises questions about what counts as a completed task, while a per-action price may make the customer feel they are paying for the agent’s inefficiency. If the agent takes 40 steps to do what a better-designed agent would do in 10, the customer’s bill reflects the agent’s ineptitude rather than the value delivered. Additionally, as discussed earlier, an outcome-based price suffers from attribution issues.

The fundamental challenge with AI agent pricing is the customer’s understanding of what has been delegated and who is responsible for the delegated tasks, especially when they are not completed satisfactorily. The present challenge with pricing AI agents is that the details regarding what the customer is buying, how the quality of work will be assessed, how underperformance will be factored into pricing, and who absorbs the cost of mistakes will all need to be spelled out, something that might be Herculean given the countless possibilities in any given context.

### The work of building sustainable AI price structures is just beginning

Pricing strategies, such as those implemented by algorithms, can erode consumer trust when customers feel prices are adjusted by opaque systems rather than by understandable human judgment. Some of that trust can be rebuilt, though only when sellers actively explain the pricing logic and give customers sufficient evidence to believe the process is fair[^15]. This is an important lesson that should serve as the basis for AI pricing. Given their entrenched experience with SaaS price structures, customers will be reluctant to accept a new price structure simply because it makes sense to the AI company internally. They will evaluate whether the structure makes sense, is consistent with other related AI and non-AI price structures for the products and services they buy, and corresponds to the value delivered by the AI product. What’s more, as the amount and nature of delivered value evolves, so will customers’ response to AI pricing.

We are in the early stages of a years- or decades-long pricing process for AI products, as traditional SaaS recedes further into the background and, with it, the price structures we were used to will also fade. At least for now, AI companies will keep experimenting with new price structures as costs change and agents become more capable. Many of the pricing changes will continue to occur in full public view, where customers can compare notes and challenge the company’s explanations. Whether these price structures succeed will depend on customer-perception work that most AI companies have barely begun.

One response to this argument is that AI pricing problems are really business-model problems, and that pricing adjustments cannot fix a flawed business model. This assessment has merit, especially when the change from the old offering to the new offering is discontinuous. Abandoning a failing business model and designing a new one is a lengthy, complex process. In the meantime, customer relationships still need to be maintained, and their reference points for pricing and price structures need to be understood and addressed.

The [pricing capability gap](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/the-pricing-capability-gap-in-value) I wrote about in another post includes a specific capability the AI industry now urgently needs: the ability to design price structures with the customer’s perceptual response front and center from the start. Companies should ask what the customer thinks they are buying, which reference price they are using, and whether the price structure provides sufficient justification to be seen as fair. The AI companies that develop this capability will spend less time apologizing for pricing rollouts and walking them back. They will be able to introduce price structures that fit the economics of AI offerings while honoring the customer’s understanding of the value being delivered. This is the work that has barely begun. Significant marginal costs have returned to AI-driven software, and customers are noticing the impact on their wallets. AI pricing will not stabilize until the customer’s side of the conversation catches up with the seller’s side.
