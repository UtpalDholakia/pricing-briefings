The **Value Pricing Framework** is a broadly applicable, flexible approach to making and evaluating pricing decisions. It is equally suited for a startup whose founder is starting to think about pricing issues, as it is for a large, established corporation with dozens of business units and a complex portfolio of goods and services. The Value Pricing Framework can inform managerial thinking about a variety of pricing issues and help an organization to develop and implement its pricing strategy.

The framework begins with the four pricing decision inputs, the so-called **four pillars of pricing** (see figure below for the graphic of the framework). Of these, three inputs help to establish the price, and the fourth input regulates the first three by determining their relative importance in the firm’s pricing strategy. The three pricing decision inputs are the **company’s costs** that are relevant to the pricing decision, the **customer value,** expressed in terms of the buyer’s monetary valuation of the benefits provided by the offering, and **reference prices** used by customers to make buying decisions. The fourth input, the firm’s adopted** value proposition,** regulates the respective weights of costs, customer value, and reference prices in the pricing decision.

The Value Pricing Framework

At the framework's center are two types of pricing decisions: **set a new price** and **change a current price**. A vast majority of pricing decisions involve changing the price. The opportunities to set a new price (for the very first time) are few and far between; entrepreneurs are more likely to get such opportunities because they are more likely to develop a pricing strategy or make a pricing decision from scratch than managers in established companies. This means that most of the time, the organization's historical prices and pricing approach set relatively rigid and narrow boundaries, at least in the short term, for changing prices.

In the framework, the pricing decision involves considering two aspects of the price: **the price level**, defined as the average price or the visible price charged for the item or the line, and the **price structure**, which is the overall assortment of prices, price-benefit combinations, price variations, the range of prices, and price offers for the product.  Asking prices or listed prices that show the price level are more visible and get a lot of attention (in the media and by consumers, for example), but thinking through and designing a price structure carefully is a far more potent driver of pricing success. (A detailed discussion about price level and price structure is available [here](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/understanding-price-structure-updated).)

There is often a significant gap between the prices that are quoted, listed, or communicated in visible places like catalogs, sales pitches, store shelves, and websites, and in what customers actually pay after discounts, markdowns, allowances, and other incentives are considered. The Value Pricing Framework, therefore, explicitly distinguishes **price realization,** defined as the actual prices paid by customers, from the **pricing decision**, which typically has to do with visible prices and the price structure. This increases clarity in pricing strategy by allowing pricing decision makers to recognize, analyze, and manage the divide between their asking prices and their realized prices. The process is labeled **price execution** in the Value Pricing Framework.

To assess the success of pricing decisions, four **pricing success measures** are included in the Value Pricing Framework: **sales revenue**, **profit margin**, **customer response**, and **employee buy-in**. The idea is to provide a more complete, balanced assessment of an organization’s pricing strategy that emphasizes a future orientation and sustainability. Rather than simply maximizing a single measure ([sales revenue](https://hbr.org/1975/01/market-share-a-key-to-profitability) or [profit margin](https://hbr.org/1992/03/lessons-from-germanys-midsize-giants) in most cases), the framework suggests that an effective pricing strategy should balance these often conflicting outcomes by assigning weights to them depending on the specific organizational goals and details of the pricing context (discussed below).

The approach of assigning weights and achieving balance applies to both sides of the pricing decision, to the **pricing decision inputs,** and the **pricing success measures** in the Value Pricing Framework. In most cases, the pricing decision maker should maintain a balance between increasing unit sales and revenue and earning a healthy profit margin, while also paying attention to the response of the customer base to the company's pricing policies and ensuring that confident and knowledgeable employees stand ready to execute its pricing strategy. Thus, there’s more to a good pricing decision and effective pricing strategy, beyond the math typically used in pricing models. It includes a consideration of the emotional and behavioral human response to the pricing on both the customer and the employee side, actively bringing psychological issues into the domain of pricing strategy.

The expanded multi-pronged consideration of success measures is the win-win-win-win quadfecta of the Value Pricing Framework[^1]. One important implication is that rarely does it make sense to give all the weight to a single success measure (just like it does to give weight to one input factor), which is why the idea of “maximizing,” whether it is unit sales or revenue or gross margin, is something that is not endorsed here.

In the framework, these four pricing success measures, in turn, feed back to the pricing decision inputs (the so-called four pillars), resulting in a cyclical process of price management that reflects the ongoing dynamic involvement of customers and employees, and adjustments based on their responses. In a nutshell, setting or changing prices is never a one-time deal, nor is it even a periodic activity. In today’s environment, pricing strategy is a continuous adjustment to human and non-human forces in the marketplace based on a clear understanding of pricing goals, price structures, and the organization’s pricing capabilities[^2].

The final part of the Value Pricing Framework, shown at the periphery in the figure, involves an in-depth consideration of the **pricing context** within which the company operates and pricing decisions are made. The decision maker must monitor relevant economic, social, political, and technological trends and variables, in particular, specific visible reactions to price from customers and employees, considering how these will affect the pricing process described in the Value Pricing Framework and the company's pricing structure. The pricing context injects contingencies, opportunities, and increasingly, a greater dynamism that requires frequent price adjustments, communication about prices, and a periodic consideration of the organization’s price structure and pricing policies. Next, each concept in the Value Pricing Framework is discussed in more detail.

### **The Pricing Decision Inputs**

Effective pricing decisions rely on careful consideration of the four decision inputs separately, and in tandem. This is because each input plays a different role individually during decision making, and together, they interact to guide the direction of the company's pricing strategy.

#### **Costs**

In every pricing decision, costs are a necessary consideration because, in the long run, costs and prices are linked inextricably. For a business to remain viable, its average prices must, without exception, be higher than its average costs. In this sense, average costs establish the floor or lower bound on the average price over the longer term in every case. However, beyond this simple concept, [understanding how costs change](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/a-balanced-assessment-of-cost-based) with sales and classifying costs into those that must be considered and those that should be ignored for a particular pricing decision are important considerations. The concept of relevant costs is critical to numerous powerful pricing strategies.

For instance, once relevant costs are adequately understood and a long-term viable pricing structure is put into place, designed to cover the average costs, the manager then has the flexibility to design creative short-term pricing offers to attract new customers or to increase sales to existing customers while adding disproportionately to the company's profits. This idea is covered in more depth in many articles and case studies on this blog.

#### **Customer Value**

Every product feature potentially provides customer benefits. In the Value Pricing Framework, customer value is quantified by mapping the product's features to benefits and benefits to the customer's willingness to pay. For pricing decisions, customer value is an important consideration because it is unrelated to costs. It provides a unique data point about viable prices, helping to generate an upper bound on prices customers consider worth paying. An understanding of current customer value also provides guidance about how to increase it.

For example, customers may place tremendous significance on a feature that costs the company very little money to offer. When buying season ticket packages, for example, sports fans may be happy to pay a hefty premium to receive playoff ticket purchase priority, a feature that costs a professional sports team virtually nothing to offer. Similarly, over the past year, under its new CEO, Starbucks has [implemented a policy](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-02-06/starbucks-writing-on-cups-policy-gets-mixed-customer-reviews?sref=d3S20v77) of having baristas personalize to-go cups for customers by writing short messages or drawing doodles to increase their connection to the brand. In the words of one [Starbucks customer](https://www.wsj.com/business/smiley-faces-are-in-animals-are-out-the-politics-of-starbucks-cup-doodles-6c2477e8?gaa_at=eafs&gaa_n=AWEtsqfyVUQJ8hKXZ1NeCvhGZr_AXPqhxro4XKKMa3hQpR6Fp3NpSvEEh685uwkLiao%3D&gaa_ts=68f566e3&gaa_sig=pOr5i-Zlm57FxlSfkkRuVgUBW90yyetUe-2ZPUf9TSwyh8oLe2nzxi1dkqt5Tp-tgtsOd7ExexWotmP_m0Wh2g%3D%3D), “These initiatives are super cute and honestly always make my day.”

On the flip side, customers may be willing to pay little or nothing extra for features that are very expensive to deliver. In early versions of Amazon's Kindle reading device, for instance, the text-to-speech feature was added at a significant cost. However, [few customers used this feature](https://www.cnet.com/tech/services-and-software/amazon-retreats-on-kindles-text-to-speech-issue/), leading Amazon to [drop it in later versions](https://arstechnica.com/gadgets/2009/03/amazon-backs-off-on-kindles-text-to-speech/) to [avoid dealing with objections](https://goodereader.com/blog/electronic-readers/text-to-speech-in-e-readers-receives-another-setback) from the publishing industry. Now, with [improvements in technology](https://www.businessinsider.com/how-to-do-text-to-speech-on-kindle-fire) and greater customer appreciation, the [feature is back](https://ilmk.wordpress.com/2016/05/13/text-to-speech-comes-to-the-paperwhite/). Knowing how features translate to benefits and benefits translate to dollars and cents valuations by customers allows the company to design and then make value-aligned pricing offers.

#### **Reference Prices**

Customers rarely judge prices individually. When assessing whether a price is a "good" price or not, customers usually compare the price to other prices and then decide. In the [words of Hans Rosling](https://www.everydaykanban.com/2018/08/03/beware-of-lonely-numbers/), "A lonely number also makes me suspicious that I will misinterpret it. A number that I have compared… can instead, fill me with hope." The prices customers use for comparison are called reference prices in the Value Pricing Framework.

Reference prices can be anything. They can be prices that the customer is familiar with, like the product's previous price, or they can be prices encountered during the purchase process like competitors' prices, or even prices of totally [unrelated products](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1509/jmkr.41.4.457.47014).

Reference prices play the dual roles of establishing a frame of reference and helping the customer to form a range of prices deemed as reasonable. For pricing decisions, [staying within the range](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article-abstract/15/2/243/1841497) of reasonable prices is an important consideration because customers tend to focus less on price and more on non-price attributes when prices are maintained within the range.

The second role of reference prices is persuasion. Prices [serve as persuasive cues](https://www.amazon.com/gp/product/B07TCSWB8Z/ref=dbs_a_def_rwt_bibl_vppi_i1). By strategically supplying reference prices through means such as prices ending in 5, 9, or a round number, explicit comparisons, or any number of other methods, the manager can influence how customers [judge the price](https://www.amazon.com/gp/product/B07TCSWB8Z/ref=dbs_a_def_rwt_bibl_vppi_i1) at every stage in their buying journey.

#### **The Value Proposition**

The fourth pricing decision input is the company's value proposition, which articulates its core differentiators. From a strategy standpoint, it is the most significant of the four inputs. Without a compelling and clearly articulated value proposition, it is virtually impossible to have an effective pricing strategy. *A differentiated value proposition that is compelling to targeted customers wins half the pricing battle.*

The value proposition verbalizes the marketing strategy and [core values endorsed by the firm](https://thepricingconundrum.substack.com/p/why-the-companys-core-values-should). It provides a broad set of guidelines to make specific pricing decisions. Its primary function in the Value Pricing Framework is to establish the relative weights managers give to the other three decision inputs.

For example, a company that has built its brand and value proposition around a Value For Money positioning strategy, like the discount grocer Aldi, weighs its costs and its prices relative to competitors heavily. It will price its products always to be significantly lower than its peers. In contrast, the fast-casual restaurant chain Panera Bread, whose value proposition [centers around](https://www.panerabread.com/en-us/food-values/our-values.html) "creating a guest-centered, personalized experience using freshly prepared, responsibly raised and high-quality ingredients," places greater emphasis on customer value and less on costs or reference prices. Another example is Sweetgreen, another fast-casual restaurant chain that has positioned itself around the [mission of](https://www.sweetgreen.com/mission) “building healthier communities by connecting people to real food,” and emphasize sustainability in their operations through sourcing strategies for carbon minimization, calculating an accurate carbon footprint for each menu item, and designing and building restaurants to minimize environmental impact. What this means for Sweetgreen’s pricing strategy is high prices, such as [\$16 for a salad](https://www.nytimes.com/2025/04/28/business/sweetgreen-jonathan-neman.html)[^3]. For both Panera Bread and Sweetgreen, the upshot of their respective value propositions is that they are willing to charge higher prices than competitors, as long as they remain within a reasonable range.

### **Pricing Decisions & Price Execution**

The Value Pricing Framework distinguishes between setting the prices and price structure and executing the price strategy. This is because there is a gap between the prices companies ask for and what they get. It is one thing to quote a price, another entirely to be paid in full. In business markets, customers may get a quantity discount, an allowance to run an advertising campaign, free freight, or an additional discount for paying promptly. In consumer markets, buyers may use a coupon, purchase during an exclusive flash sale, receive a promotional giveaway, or receive incentives as members of a rewards program. These are just some examples of ways in which the gap between the asked-for price and the realized price expands.

For many companies, the price realization gap can be significant and undermine pricing effectiveness. As I wrote in the first edition of my book, when the [2017 Toyota Prius was launched](https://www.truecar.com/used-cars-for-sale/listings/toyota/prius/year-2017-max/), for example, its listed base price was \$30,960. But this price meant little to customers. They only paid an average of \$28,352, a gap of 8.4%. Even worse, a fifth of Prius owners paid less than \$27,513, resulting in a price realization gap of 11%. In business markets, a cement manufacturer listed one of its products at \$11.97 in its price lists. When all incentives given to customers were taken into account, however, it was only earning \$6.53 per bag sold. Put differently, its price realization gap was 45.4%[^4]. Similarly, in higher education, the gap between the published tuition for a university in the United States and the actual price paid is significant. According to [one study from 2024](https://feed.georgetown.edu/access-affordability/the-truth-about-college-sticker-prices/), “In the 2023-24 academic year, the discount rate for all undergraduates [increased](https://www.highereddive.com/news/tuition-discounts-private-colleges-nacubo/716115/) to 51.9%, up from 50.8% in the previous year. At private nonprofit colleges and universities, the discounts were even more substantial, reaching a new high of 56.1% for first-time, full-time students.” These examples are not unusual; huge price realization gaps are common in every industry.

To understand the extent of harm created by this gap, consider a mountaineer who decides to climb Mount Everest and spends two years preparing and planning for the climb. During the climb, however, they only end up reaching the first base camp before turning back. The mountaineer's decision-making process was perfect, but their execution missed the mark. Think of the asked-for price as Mount Everest, the process by which it is set as analogous to the mountaineer's preparation, and price execution as the mountaineer's actual climb.

Notice that in this analogy, the reason why the mountaineer couldn’t make it to the top could be due to any number of reasons, such as physical fitness, the support team's competence, or bad weather. The same is true for price realization. Controllable factors and the uncontrollable aspects of the pricing context can both affect price execution. The fact remains that a thoughtfully established price is only meaningful when it is earned by the company. Otherwise, it is like a mountaineer charting an elaborate plan to climb Mount Everest but only getting to its foothills.

#### **Why does the price realization gap occur?**

There are many reasons, of course, and we can only scratch the surface in this piece. One factor has to do with the company simply not having a good understanding of what the gap is and how many and what amounts of incentives customers are getting, individually or collectively. And this is far more common than you might think, even in 2025’s AI, data- and analytics-obssessed corporate culture. A second reason concerns who within the company, if anyone, has responsibility for price execution, and how they are compensated for managing the price execution gap. Oftentimes, no one is in charge, and no one is getting any rewards, monetary or otherwise. Not surprisingly, no one cares.

Third, price execution may fall outside the domain of managers who make pricing decisions, and the company's pricing guidelines for price execution are simply not communicated properly to frontline employees. These managers may carefully go through a process in which they consider the decision inputs carefully before setting prices. However, someone entirely different within the company, say the sales organization, then takes over the negotiation process. This was the case with one large company in the oil and gas space that I worked with recently. The hapless pricing group worked diligently to calculate and change prices on the 10,000+ products and services in their catalog, while the sales team completely ignored these prices, relying instead on their own judgment and their desire to achieve sales targets. Not surprisingly, there was a significant price realization gap to the company’s detriment. To summarize, which concessions are given during the negotiation and the reasons behind them will dictate the size of the price realization gap.

A significant amount of recent academic research has sought to understand how to measure, analyze, and close the price realization gap, which I will consider another time in another post. Here, the main point is that price execution is just as important as making good pricing decisions for pricing strategy success. Doing all the work to figure out the best price to charge customers is of little value if they only pay a fraction of it.

### **Pricing Success Measures**

The last part of the Value Pricing Framework measures the success of the organization’s pricing strategy, including its pricing decisions and price execution. Intuitively but incorrectly, many managers evaluate the effectiveness of a pricing strategy narrowly in terms of either their sales maximization or profit margin maximization goals, giving priority to one or the other. While both metrics are obviously significant, it rarely makes sense to charge prices that will bring in the most customers or sell the most units, or even generate the highest revenue. The company could simply lower prices far beyond reasonable or sustainable levels to achieve such goals. Nor is it advisable to pursue the maximum profit margin. This would mean setting prices in the highest possible range, close to the ceiling, selling a few units, and earning a huge profit margin, again at the detriment of sustainability, customer focus, or a longer-term orientation.

But the reality is that in most cases, the goals of revenue and profit margin conflict with one another. One can only be achieved at the other's expense. Companies can *buy* unit sales by sacrificing profit margin, or they can exercise restraint, giving up low-priced sales thus increasing earned margin at the expense of market share. Complicating things even further, pricing strategy not only affects the organization's financial performance as measured by its sales and profits, but it also has meaningful effects on customers and employees, which are typically ignored, but need consideration.

Today’s customers, more than ever, want to buy from companies whose pricing strategies reflect their own values and whose prices are fair, transparent, stable, and aligned with the product's economic value. Just witness how consumers still flock to stores or websites every time a new Apple iPhone comes out or a limited-edition [Nike sneaker is released](https://marker.medium.com/nike-cant-keep-doing-this-to-us-can-it-54123f427e25). These items are not cheap, but customers find their prices to be congenial. On the other hand, consider an online retailer that is constantly changing prices of products on sale or applying surge pricing for items like fast food, where surging prices make little sense. Customers hate such meaningless price volatility because it complicates their buying decision and makes it hard to form or use reference prices. This type of pricing often leads to a negative response. Along similar lines, there is a trade-off between financial performance and customer response. Giving customers more and more benefits will satisfy them, but without commensurate price increases, the company’s profit will erode. Happy customers, but unhappy CFO, and irate shareholders will ensue!

For employees, a well-strategized and well-executed pricing strategy can be a source of organizational pride. When prices and price increases are explained, align with common sense, and come across as fair, they are easy to communicate to customers and defend during sales negotiations. Such well-thought-out pricing contributes substantially to creating an aura of competence around the company. It provides confidence to employees and boosts their morale. On the other hand, when a company adopts an "aggressive growth at any price" mentality and embarks on a path of offering discounts or changing prices in an ad hoc way, employees lose their confidence in their products and become confused and embarrassed executors of the pricing plan. In a nutshell, buy-in from frontline employees is critical for an organization’s pricing success, yet they are rarely included in the pricing decision and often inadequately trained for price execution.

The soft, people-based measures are routinely overlooked by managers, even though they are crucial for pricing success. The Value Pricing Framework aims to fix this oversight. With this framework, the manager is explicitly advised to balance the four success measures. They not only pay attention to changes in financial measures of pricing performance, but they are also sensitive to the reactions of customers and employees to the company's pricing decisions and price execution.
